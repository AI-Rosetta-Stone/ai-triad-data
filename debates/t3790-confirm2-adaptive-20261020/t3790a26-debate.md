## Topic

## Opening Statements

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Blanket rules on open-weight AI models cut off independent scientific oversight and build regulatory moats for big firms. These rules do not lower true dual-use security risks. The Department of Commerce Bureau of Industry and Security lacks the staff to govern runtime safety without relying entirely on corporate self-reports.

History in open-source software security shows that decentralized researchers patch flaws faster than centralized developers. Independent academic researchers outside corporate labs provide the only real check against proprietary opacity.

Opponents argue that open weights lower the barrier to dangerous bio or cyber weapons for bad actors who cannot be audited. This threat model ignores reality because static model weights are poor substitutes for tacit lab know-how and private data.

Critics also assume academic researchers lack enough compute power to audit frontier models well. This resource gap between labs and universities is a funding failure, not a tech reason for corporate censorship. Federal agencies should tie export controls to performance benchmarks instead of banning weight sharing entirely.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Releasing frontier AI model weights into the public domain crosses a point of no return. It permanently cuts off upstream safety control from downstream actors. The Bureau of Industry and Security inside the Department of Commerce manages dual-use export controls for this exact reason. Once digital files spread globally, you cannot call them back.

Nasr and colleagues proved this risk in 2023. They showed that targeted fine-tuning extracts long, exact passages from a model training set. This proves that open weights expose sensitive data to bad actors. Policymakers must see that unlike software patches, model weights act as executable intelligence that spreads without decay.

Critics argue that restricting open weights hands power to big tech monopolies. They claim it fails to stop foreign rivals from training the same systems. This objection is right about one thing: export controls cause business friction and compliance burdens for smaller domestic developers.

Yet that friction is the exact tool we need to stop the unvetted spread of biological and cyber dangers. The Department of Commerce already uses the Export Administration Regulations to restrict high-performance chip shipments under Title 15 of the Code of Federal Regulations. Applying similar tiered licensing rules to frontier model weights protects national security. At the same time, it leaves basic academic research on smaller models alone.

Voluntary industry safety promises fail because you cannot un-release model weights once they land on public sites. National security export rules rely on named actors, real liability, and strict boundaries instead of corporate self-restraint.

When the Department of Commerce puts a technology on the Commerce Control List, it sets clear legal rules against illegal foreign re-export. Independent developers can still use frontier tools through cloud APIs that keep safety filters and usage logs active. If we want to keep human control over powerful systems, we must keep a physical choke point at the hardware and weight distribution layer.
