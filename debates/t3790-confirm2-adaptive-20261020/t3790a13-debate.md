## Topic

Under what conditions of negligence and compliance should foundation model developers face legal liability through statutory duty-of-care pathways for harms caused by deployed models?

## Opening Statements

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Statutory duty-of-care rules that penalize upstream foundation model developers destroy the decentralized experimentation needed to cure human disease and end scarcity. Model creators supply general software tools like the unpatented semiconductor designs of the 1970s or the open-source Linux kernels that power modern cloud infrastructure. Holding base developers strictly liable for downstream deployment errors breaks the component-part doctrine in Section 5 of the Restatement of Torts. Congress used this exact principle in Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act to shield general tools from downstream misuse. When lawmakers force upstream creators to police every domain-specific use, they add massive compliance costs that lock open-weight innovation behind corporate monopolies.

Pre-deployment certification gates create a time gap that permanently delays life-saving tech while protecting legacy firms from new market rivals. Data from the open-source software movement shows that decentralized developer networks fix security flaws faster than centralized boards can schedule a hearing. A medical AI model trained on cancer datasets needs fine-tuning by hospital staff at the deployment stage to reach clinical utility. If developers face tort liability for downstream use without local operational control, they simply stop shipping open weights. Patients waiting for automated diagnostic tools pay the literal body count of this risk-averse paralysis.

Downstream users must carry all legal blame because only point-of-impact operators have the contextual data and tech capacity to spot and price execution risks. Federal agencies like the Food and Drug Administration already regulate medical software use through post-market checks rather than pre-market ideological bans. Lawmakers must reject bills that pierce upstream shields and instead focus enforcement on the hospitals, banks, and city agencies that actually put models into production. Protecting developer speed ensures that abundance reaches the public before red tape ruins the whole sector.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

If software makers profit from putting predictive systems into critical fields, those same actors must answer in court when their products fail basic safety standards.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Foundation model developers must face mandatory pre-deployment checks and statutory duty-of-care rules because late lawsuits cannot fix irreversible systemic harms. Accelerationists argue that strict upstream liability destroys open software work. But this ignores how unverified foundation models push catastrophic failure costs onto downstream users who lack the technical capacity to audit base weights. When a bank or hospital uses a flawed model with millions of parameters, the developer keeps all the profit while the public and patients absorb the total downside risk.

Pre-deployment checks must replace reactive lawsuits as the primary tool in safety-critical domains where feedback loops fail. Historical rules like the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act for drugs and the Federal Aviation Administration airworthiness directives show that complex technology needs gatekeeping before release, not tort suits after the crash. In software, studies show that late debugging fails when models show emergent behaviors that slip past standard tests until real-world use triggers cascading market or infrastructure failures.

Liability gaps currently punish caution and reward inaction under current laws. When developers rush unverified code to market without independent audits, they grab first-mover gains while regulators bear the political fallout of safety failures. Setting clear duty-of-care rules forces developers to prove safety baselines before release. This shifts the proof burden from injured plaintiffs who cannot inspect proprietary weights to the corporate entities profiting from the release.

Independent auditors may face limits when reading complex foundation models, but this technical wall makes pre-release checks more necessary, not less. If models remain opaque to deep checks, putting them into high-stakes societal jobs without verified safety bounds is institutional vandalism. Society spent centuries building liability rules for civil engineering and aviation to stop private actors from pushing the costs of unverified innovation onto the public.
