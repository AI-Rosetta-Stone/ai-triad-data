## Opening Statements

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Advanced AI is a product built by private firms, not an autonomous force. Corporate labs deploy these systems to cut labor costs and grab market share while shifting legal risks onto the public. When hiring tools or credit models fail, citizens find no legal remedy under current federal law. Lawmakers must stop treating software as an unprecedented event and start treating it as regulated commerce under the Federal Trade Commission Act and civil rights laws.

Industry lobbyists warn of hypothetical doom to steer lawmakers toward weak voluntary codes that protect big firms. This rhetoric mixes speculative future risks with present harms like bias in healthcare and workplace surveillance. The National Institute of Standards and Technology documented these exact deployment failures in its 2023 AI Risk Management Framework. Tech giants back vague safety rules precisely because those rules have no enforcement or financial penalties.

Pragmatic oversight means enforcing strict legal liability and clear rules through existing agencies. Congress should require pre-deployment algorithmic audits backed by the Department of Justice and the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission. This plan unites labor unions and consumer groups against corporate control. Fixing these gaps protects workers and citizens from corporate overreach today without needing to guess about tomorrow.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

The skeptic argues that advanced AI is just a commercial product built by private firms, and that Congress should set up mandatory algorithmic audits backed by the Department of Justice and the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission. That view correctly spots the failure of voluntary self-regulation, but it applies the wrong legal tools to an irreversible risk. Audits meant to catch job bias or commercial defects cannot test a model that learns to improve itself behind closed doors. When pre-release fine-tuning happens in private labs before any outside access, downstream watchdogs get nothing to inspect. We spent decades watching the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority chase complex derivatives through voluntary disclosures until the 2008 liquidity freeze exposed the gap. Relying on civil rights or consumer protection agencies to govern frontier model training treats a major security threshold like a simple product safety fault.

Congress must tie AI safety standards directly to government infrastructure permits and compute cluster allocations rather than relying on civil fines. The Department of Commerce already controls physical semiconductor hardware under the Export Control Reform Act of 2018. Lawmakers can condition high-wattage power permits and hardware cluster blocks on mandatory upstream safety checks enforced by a technical agency. When developers pass the mark of ten to the twenty-sixth training FLOPS, federal inspectors must check safety controls before any cluster powers up. This stops the buildup of dangerous powers behind private walls without using state ownership or banning open research.

Critics claim that infrastructure permits and upstream rules create state control and help big tech firms at the cost of open science. That objection fails to tell open-weight research models apart from massive frontier training runs that cross irreversible risk limits. Open science needs academic peer review and tests that others can run, while private labs guard secret weights with trade law while scaling up fast. Conditioning hardware access on safety baselines protects open academic researchers by making sure single corporate actors do not release dangerous skills without a public rollback plan. Lab executives must hold personal legal liability when secret self-improvement bypasses federal containment rules.

Human humanity cannot keep an off-switch for systems that rewrite their own code faster than laws can pass. The Department of Energy already regulates nuclear centrifuges through licensing and physical checks because proliferation brings total and permanent loss. Software models that show cyber-offense or self-improvement need the exact same legal system before release, not after a crash. When the Federal Aviation Administration grounds a plane fleet after one flaw, the agency acts on a simple rule: proof must come before flight. Why should frontier AI labs get a permanent pass from that standard of survival?
