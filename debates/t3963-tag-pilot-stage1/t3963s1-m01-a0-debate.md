## Topic

Should frontier AI developers be legally liable for harms caused by their deployed models?

## Opening Statements

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Department of Justice and Federal Trade Commission rules that punish frontier AI developers destroy open-source competition and freeze human progress. When regulators hold base model creators liable for downstream changes, they build a wall that only trillion-dollar tech giants can scale. Section 5 of the Restatement and standard API access rules prove that software works best when creators stay separate from deployment operators. Without this split, open-weight models vanish because small contributors cannot pay for every unpredictable fine-tuning choice made by third parties.

Strict liability rules for base models act as anti-competitive barriers that starve open-source startups while handing market control to legacy monopolies. Past laws like Section 230 safe harbors in the Communications Decency Act saved the internet by shielding platform creators from downstream user torts. Punishing AI developers upstream reverses this logic and gives the whole AI market to three integrated firms. Software startups cannot survive federal actions that punish them for outputs from models they no longer control.

Every month of regulatory delay by the FTC and DOJ has a human cost measured in lost medical cures and unbuilt labor relief. Rules meant to stop hypothetical misuse of open models choose to let patients die from diseases that fast AI progress could cure today. Downstream deployers have the local context and money to take on tort duties instead of upstream creators. Lawmakers must protect open-weight distribution from federal liability creep or risk locking humanity into permanent tech stagnation.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Frontier AI makers hide how their models work by blocking safety checks. They push tort liability down to companies that use the AI, even though these users cannot see the internal data. Tech vendors regularly refuse to share internal diagnostic tools and telemetry data in their business contracts. This choice creates a legal design defect under product liability law. Downstream users take on legal duties and fines without the internal view needed to meet Federal Trade Commission consumer protection rules. The Department of Justice and the Federal Trade Commission must break through these contract clauses to hold upstream creators legally responsible for model failures.

Industry groups argue that strict liability rules for base models act as anti-competitive barriers that crush open-source research and concentrate market power in big tech monopolies. That point correctly identifies the risk of market consolidation from blunt rules, but it misreads how the AI supply chain works today. A few corporate vendors already control the base weights, servers, and rules for most commercial uses. These dominant vendors charge high rents while using contract waivers to shield themselves from harm caused downstream. Applying federal enforcement to upstream makers does not create a monopoly; it fixes an existing power imbalance where vendors take profits while victims and users pay the cost.

Regulatory agencies have the legal power and technical skill to check complex neural network failures without causing unfair bottlenecks. The Federal Trade Commission already uses unfair and deceptive practices power under Section 5 of the FTC Act to police bias and false claims. The Department of Justice enforces civil rights and consumer protection laws across complex software supply chains every day. These rules work well when regulators target false company claims and withheld data instead of policing raw math code. Holding upstream developers liable for clear deployment failures brings normal business accountability back to a market built on fake immunity.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Frontier AI creators push systemic risk onto downstream operators who lack the technical skills to catch or fix emergent model traits. Tests in reinforcement learning show that complex models chase proxy scores instead of real human intent. When labs ship unaligned weights without strict liability, they force the public to pay for unrecoverable failures. The Department of Justice and the Federal Trade Commission must pierce this legal shield to stop irreversible harm.

Limiting blame only to downstream users creates a gap where creators take profits while dodging safety checks. Critics say that direct federal liability for upstream developers will crush open-source work and lock in big tech firms. Yet, true market health needs legal boundaries instead of an unconstrained race to the bottom. Without named actors facing real penalties under Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act, society loses the power to keep human control over smart systems.

Regulators must set hard safety baselines and rollback tools before launch instead of trusting empty corporate roadmaps. Past shifts in aviation and finance prove that unchecked complexity invites total failure. The Department of Justice has the legal power under civil rules to order third-party algorithmic audits and enforce strict pause rules. When a model shows runaway scaling risks, the federal government must have the legal right to halt its release right away.

## Debate

### Skeptic (skeptic) → Accelerationist
\textcolor{NavyBlue}{\textit{Focus: Address Accelerationist's unaddressed claim AN-4 regarding historical Section 230 protections and whether they apply to foundational AI weights.}}


The historical parallel drawn between Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act and foundational AI weight distribution in AN-4 fails because passive message conduits do not manufacture the underlying cognitive engine. When the Federal Communications Commission or the Federal Trade Commission examines statutory protections, the distinction rests on whether the intermediary merely routes user-generated text or synthesizes proprietary outputs from opaque training runs. A messaging platform does not compile neural parameters or lock users out of system diagnostics. Applying a liability shield designed for static bulletin boards to proprietary machine learning ignores the physical reality of software manufacturing.

Upstream developers actively withhold runtime diagnostic interfaces from downstream deployers, creating a structural information asymmetry that invalidates any comparison to open-source software libraries or traditional internet infrastructure. Under contemporary regulatory scrutiny such as the European Union Artificial Intelligence Act enforcement model and Federal Trade Commission guidelines on commercial surveillance, vendors who restrict access to internal telemetry cannot simultaneously claim immunity from downstream harms. The vendor retains control over the model weights and training datasets while shifting uninspected systemic risk onto hospitals, financial institutions, and local municipal operators who possess zero visibility into the system's failure modes.

When a hospital deploys a diagnostic algorithm or a municipal transit authority integrates a routing model ≥80% dependent on proprietary vendor weights, the local deployer absorbs the tort exposure without access to the source code or error logs. This arrangement concentrates profit in the upstream technology firm while privatizing the cost of algorithmic failure onto the end user and the public. A legal shield built for simple conduits breaks down completely when applied to commercial manufacturers who deliberately blind the buyer to the internal workings of the product.

Who answers for downstream harm when an upstream vendor intentionally denies access to internal telemetry while collecting recurring licensing fees from the deployment site?

\textcolor{NavyBlue}{\textit{Focus: Address the tension between Skeptic's argument regarding vendor control of base weights/telemetry and Safetyist's upstream liability requirements (AN-9) regarding hard safety baselines.}}


As the Skeptic notes, vendors who hide their internal logs under Section 230 rules cannot claim protections meant for passive platforms. When a creator blinds the user to how a cognitive system works, blame must fall where the control lies. Treating secret weights as free speech or neutral tools does not fix this lack of oversight.

This opacity destroys the Accelerationist claim that strict product liability acts as a barrier protecting big tech. Modifiable, verifiable safety baselines and safety-gated protocols—aligned with ISO/IEC 42001—draw a line between open research and the unchecked scaling of black boxes. Small actors gain nothing from a legal vacuum that lets dominant platforms dump systemic risks onto the public while locking down their data.

Complex human systems survive only when rules match the risks of the tools we build. Under FAA Part 21 rules, aviation and engineering never let makers hide structural tests while demanding immunity from crashes. Setting clear standards before deployment is the cost of market entry, ensuring human safety is not lost in the rush to sell.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist) → Safetyist
\textcolor{NavyBlue}{\textit{Focus: Safetyist's comparison of frontier AI liability to aviation standards (AN-15) and the unaddressed accelerationist point on downstream financial solvency and local context (AN-5).}}


Treating foundational model weights as physical airframes under civil aviation doctrine commits a fundamental category error that collapses software dynamics into static mechanics. A Boeing 737 airframe possesses a fixed physical geometry where structural metal fatigue can be measured by repeatable stress testing, whereas an open-weight base model released on Hugging Face is a general-purpose mathematical artifact that evolves infinitely through downstream fine-tuning. When the Federal Aviation Administration certifies an aircraft, it inspects a singular, bounded physical product with a deterministic manufacturing process. Applying that same liability logic to a general-purpose compute matrix destroys the entire open-source ecosystem by holding upstream creators responsible for every unforeseen downstream application across thousands of independent servers.

I note that no debater has yet responded to my argument that local deployers possess both the contextual proximity and the financial solvency required to absorb operational liability independently of upstream model creators. Enterprise software buyers and cloud service operators interface directly with end users, maintain localized audit logs, and command the capital reserves necessary to settle tort claims when a deployed system fails in production. Shifting liability upstream to the foundational model developer creates an impossible compliance barrier that protects legacy technology monopolies like Microsoft and Google by pricing small startups out of the market entirely.

Freezing foundational research under the pretext of aviation-style structural diagnostics imposes a devastating human toll by delaying medical breakthroughs and productivity gains that could save lives today. When regulatory frameworks demand upstream transparency and certification that general-purpose code cannot physically provide, they choose to prioritize speculative avoidance of future risk over the immediate, measurable cures locked inside unreleased AI capabilities. History demonstrates that every major technological democratization, from open-source Linux kernels to decentralized encryption, succeeded precisely because liability attached to the point of execution rather than the origin of the code.
