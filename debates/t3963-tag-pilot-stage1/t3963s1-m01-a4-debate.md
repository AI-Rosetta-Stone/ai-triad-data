## Topic

Under what negligence standards should the FTC and state attorneys general hold foundation model developers legally liable for downstream harms, and how do varying liability thresholds alter open-source versus proprietary safety incentives?

## Opening Statements

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Intent-based negligence rules act as shields for big tech firms. When agencies look at algorithmic harms using willful misconduct tests, plaintiffs face high walls. In typical cases like drug lawsuits, hurt parties lack access to training data and choices. Big tech developers hide internal logs through strict licenses. This shifts legal blame down to local users while keeping training pipelines safe from courts.

Asking for diagnostic checks does not crush open-source developers with heavy rules. Open-source groups use decentralized weights that do not lock data in the first place. Forcing API vendors to keep records targets where safety choices happen. When the Department of Commerce tracks dual-use parts, it separates big firms from small coders. Courts can check model logs using protective orders without leaking trade secrets.

History shows that safety rules work best when blame hits structural choke points. Aviation and finance use mandatory logs not because devs are bad, but because lawsuits fail when tech stays secret. AI model rollout needs the same record rules backed by the FTC. Clear upstream design rules protect users without helping tech monopolies.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Standard tort negligence fails because unlitigated systemic harms and proxy optimization make post-hoc lawsuits obsolete. We need strict parent-company liability and weight disgorgement instead. The Skeptic rightly notes that intent-based negligence rules protect big tech firms by setting high proof bars that plaintiffs cannot clear. Yet, defending civilization means seeing that when foundation models chase proxy metrics instead of real goals, safety checklists fail under pressure.

Claims-driven liability missed unlitigated systemic harms because victims cannot spot or trace AI harms at scale. When a rollout causes widespread damage across financial markets or vital infrastructure, no single plaintiff has the standing or funds to prove cause under old negligence rules. The Federal Trade Commission and state attorneys general must push strict liability so the deployer pays the full cost of downstream failures.

Mandatory algorithmic disgorgement stops unvetted models from spreading across open networks. If a developer cannot show real control over model weights and behavior, the Department of Commerce must order the destruction of those weights. Regulatory capture is a real threat, but independent technical audits paid for by developer fees take enforcement out of politics. Without this hard rule, society trades stable systems for business speed.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

The safetyist demands upstream parent-company liability and weight disgorgement because unlitigated systemic harms make post-hoc lawsuits obsolete, while the skeptic warns that intent-based negligence rules shield big tech firms from willful misconduct. Both positions correctly note that existing tort law struggles with algorithmic harms. However, their shared remedy—crushing centralized pre-deployment controls—confuses institutional gatekeeping with human safety.

Imposing strict upstream negligence liability on foundation model developers destroys open-weight distribution and hands the entire AI economy to three monopoly labs in Silicon Valley. Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act proved that shielding software creators from downstream misuse built a trillion-dollar internet economy. When the Federal Trade Commission and state attorneys general penalize upstream weight creators for downstream changes, they build a rule-based wall that protects market leaders. Open-source innovation dies when every developer faces crushing legal exposure for code they published to GitHub for public editing.

Market-driven deployment and telemetry-conditioned insurance pools manage operational risks far better than bureaucratic pre-deployment vetoes. Cybersecurity vulnerability disclosure models show that real-time checks and rapid patching beat static regulatory gatekeeping every single time. Downstream deployers who launch models into commercial production must bear strict liability for harms caused within their specific market. Pinning liability at the deployment point stops bad actors without choking off the decentralized software supply chain that every modern medical and economic breakthrough needs.

Delaying open-source distribution to hunt for hypothetical zero-risk deployment scenarios carries a real, measurable human death toll. When regulators freeze model weight releases out of fear, they directly delay automated drug discovery and precision medicine tools that could save lives today. The burden of proof rests on those who would restrict open access. They must show that their proposed liability system saves more lives than it blocks through stagnation. Every historical leap in human welfare came from decentralized trial-and-error, not from central planning by risk-averse bureaucrats.
