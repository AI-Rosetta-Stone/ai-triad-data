## Topic

Under what liability thresholds can federal courts hold frontier AI developers legally accountable for downstream harms without stifling open-source innovation?

## Opening Statements

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Upstream tort liability on base AI weights kills open-source software and hands market power to Silicon Valley monopolies. When the European Union drafted the Cyber Resilience Act, open-source maintainers faced open-ended legal penalties for downstream code changes they never approved. Federal courts cannot punish base model creators without causing the exact same developer flight that hurt community software projects in 2023. Big Tech firms pay these compliance costs with ease, while independent developers simply delete their code to escape ruinous lawsuits.

Injured plaintiffs who want deep-pocketed defendants ignore how software and legal cause actually work. Base models released as open weights work like raw steel or general-purpose encryption code; the creator cannot control or predict every later fine-tuning pass. Holding a foundation model developer legally liable for a third-party use breaks basic tort rules about direct cause and control. Courts that try to pierce this barrier will find that fixed legal rules fail against bad actors who strip safety filters from decentralized weights.

Static rules for model behavior become easy targets for malicious actors to bypass, rather than real safety barriers. Tests on machine learning benchmarks show that fixed rules fall within hours of release when people use basic prompt tricks. Federal agencies that enforce rigid liability rules confuse bureaucratic box-ticking with real risk management while they criminalize open innovation. Congress must reject upstream developer liability to ensure that medical and scientific breakthroughs reach patients instead of dying in regulatory red tape.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

The accelerationists are right that base model weights act like digital source code or raw materials. Treating these weights as normal products risks breaking open-source software and giving a few big firms market control. Yet that logic breaks down once a model crosses a line where civil lawsuits cannot fix the harm. Once people download those weights to millions of devices worldwide, human control ends for good.

Today, AI rules rely on written use policies that makers cannot enforce once code is public. Studies on model leaks show that users strip out safety filters via fine-tuning and prompt injection within hours of a release. Letting makers off the hook for this downstream spread ignores how physical distribution works. When a maker publishes an unchecked model with dangerous traits, they do not just share a tool; they throw away the off-switch.

Courts and agencies must set mandatory safety checks that block open-source releases until models pass capability tests. Under a strict liability rule like the laws for hazardous materials, makers stay legally liable for downstream harms if they release base weights without third-party audits. This policy does not ban open-source work; it gives a safe harbor to audited releases while punishing reckless actors for disasters they cannot undo. Without this rule, society trades stable institutions for fast profits.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

The debate over AI liability is not a tech dispute about model weights. It is a corporate lobbying campaign to protect tech monopolies from tort law. 

Accelerationists argue that strict upstream liability destroys open-source innovation. They claim it criminalizes peer work under complex licenses. 

Safetyists counter that federal courts and agencies must mandate strict safety gates before code hits public systems. They want to stop harms early. 

Both sides share a blind spot. They treat code as an autonomous force. They ignore the physical data centers that drain municipal power. They ignore the underpaid human workers who clean toxic datasets in Nairobi.

Federal courts should hold frontier developers liable under strict risk-utility tort standards when their commercial models fail safety rules. 

The Department of Justice can enforce this through civil actions using existing consumer protection powers. This mirrors the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration recall rules set in 1966. 

This approach avoids a false choice. It does not ban open-source repos, nor does it give total immunity to big cloud providers. 

A solo developer on GitHub lacks the compute clusters needed to push systemic risk at scale. The liability burden must land on entities that use more than ten to the twenty-seventh power integer operations of training compute. At this scale, safety harms become real.

Opponents argue that this liability will break open-source groups. They claim it will push all AI work into three Silicon Valley boardrooms. 

That claim fails when courts separate raw base code from paid, proprietary APIs backed by venture capital. 

Small projects stay safe because negligence requires commercial scale, bad design, and clear economic harm to real plaintiffs. 

When a paid model harms workers or drives biased profiling with no recourse, the developer pays damages. Congress can write this rule tomorrow without touching a single public repo.
