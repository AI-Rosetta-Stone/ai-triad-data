## Topic

Under what structural conditions do scale economies and proprietary compute access drive frontier AI market concentration rather than open competition, and how should antitrust regulators evaluate this tension?

## Opening Statements

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Frontier AI is an infrastructure monopoly hidden as a science shift. Three cloud giants control the rare chips and power needed to train large models. Microsoft, Amazon, and Google spend over two hundred billion dollars a year on data centers. They build this hardware fortress to lock out rivals and lock in corporate clients. Antitrust regulators must stop viewing this market through software innovation. They face a classic utility grab driven by physical limits.

Critics claim that massive capital needs reflect the fixed laws of scaling. That view ignores how firms manage hardware. Cloud gatekeepers hold back eighty percent of advanced graphics processing units for their own apps. They starve rival labs of compute power via strict cloud service terms. These deals act as private toll gates on public power grids. The need for power does not excuse a closed distribution monopoly.

Fixing this requires structural breakups under current antitrust laws. The DOJ and the FTC have the legal tools to split cloud and model units. Regulators should force common carrier rules on data centers that use over one hundred megawatts of power. This mirrors old railroad breakups where track owners could not own the cargo. Taxpayers subsidize the energy grids for these sites via local tax breaks. The public pays the water and grid costs while corporate bosses take the cash.

When three private firms run the base layer for advanced AI, public checks vanish behind corporate secrecy. Watchdog groups like the Federal Trade Commission lack the legal power and tools to check private training runs before release.

Safety rules bring high setup costs that help big firms, but this friction is the price of survival. Antitrust laws must judge this power by the risk of hard harm, not just prices. When the Justice Department checked old tech deals in telecom and aviation, courts ruled that systemic risk beats short-term price drops. Scaling unvetted models is like risky virus lab work; speed without checks brings disaster.
