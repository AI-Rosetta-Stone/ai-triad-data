## Topic

Should frontier AI developers be legally liable for harms caused by their deployed models?

## Opening Statements

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Strict legal liability for frontier AI developers creates moats that protect big firms and criminalize open-source code. When policymakers hold model builders liable for downstream harms, they give tech giants a veto over competition. Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act protected infrastructure providers from user liability. Holding pipe builders responsible for water quality destroys open networks. If small labs face unlimited lawsuits for third-party outputs, open-weight models stop. Only a few trillion-dollar companies can absorb this litigation risk. Upstream liability is not public safety policy. It is corporate protectionism disguised as caution.

Legal frameworks focus on isolated harms while ignoring the statistical uplift of rapid AI deployment in critical sectors. Economic studies show that strict liability hurts research in high-utility fields like medical diagnosis and material science. Every month of delay keeps disease burdens and labor inefficiencies that AI could solve today. The visible failure gets press while the invisible rescue goes uncounted. When regulators freeze deployment to stop misuse, they choose to let people suffer from curable ailments. That trade-off has a body count that risk-averse planners refuse to measure.

Frontier developers control base capabilities, but they lack direct control over downstream deployment contexts. Critics say releasing a base model is like deploying a finished product. This ignores how general-purpose tools work in the wild. A base model resembles an open-source database rather than a closed gadget. Downstream users must evaluate and price their own risks based on local contexts. Splitting upstream developer tooling from downstream operator fault keeps accountability where control lives. Lawmakers must reject strict upstream liability to ensure that abundance reaches clinics, schools, and labs before stagnation locks in scarcity.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Foundational component makers stay strictly liable for structural flaws under product law, no matter who builds the final product. The fast-growth view says upstream developers should be immune from downstream harm to protect open-source research and block giant monopolies. This view sees that rule-following costs hurt smaller players. Yet it assumes software runs outside the physical laws that govern civil engineering and drug supply chains. When a plant sells a volatile chemical without safety checks, courts do not excuse the maker because the buyer built a bomb. Frontier AI models act as cognitive precursors whose weights and design set behavioral limits long before users type a prompt. Courts must apply normal product liability to upstream AI makers to stop systemic risk shifting onto the public.

Voluntary industry self-rule fails because market pressure beats safety promises when market share is at stake. The Federal Aviation Administration learned this from past aviation failures where rushed software certification traded lives for speed. Without laws backed by agencies like the Department of Justice or the Federal Trade Commission, safety teams inside big labs lack the legal power to stop risky model rollouts. Bosses face duties to boost profits that penalize voluntary pauses in training. Strict liability fixes this clash by making safety checks the cheapest path to market. Congress must pass pre-launch rules tied to civil damages to link profit motives with public safety.

Upstream liability does not need to build rules that crush open-source work if enforcement targets capability limits rather than company types. Past rules in telecom and medical devices split general standards from small development without killing small-scale innovation. A limit of ten to the twenty-sixth integer operations for training compute sets a clear line that frees open-source groups while catching the few frontier labs that scale without limits. Downstream users lack the tech skills to audit frontier weights or patch deep behavior flaws built during pre-training. Moving blame to the upstream maker puts liability where technical control actually lives. We spent centuries building guardrails to keep society stable. On what ground do you claim we can drop them without inviting collapse?

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Corporate developers must bear strict legal liability for documented harms caused by their deployed models. This must happen only through rules that pierce trade secrecy instead of building regulatory moats for tech monopolies.

Accelerationists claim that holding foundational model makers liable will criminalize open-source code. They argue it will also help massive firms like Microsoft and Google by raising compliance costs too high.

Safetyists argue that component makers must remain strictly liable for structural flaws under product law. They hold this view regardless of who builds the final consumer application.

Both sides assume federal courts can easily isolate downstream algorithmic failures from proprietary model weights hidden behind corporate trade secret walls.

Traditional lawsuits against AI systems fail. Courts routinely accept black-box proprietary defenses that deny plaintiffs the evidence needed to prove causation.

Take a hiring algorithm built on proprietary training data that systematically filters out female applicants in a workforce of 10,000 employees at a major retailer. The victim cannot inspect the weights or the training corpus.

Federal courts adjudicating employment discrimination under Title VII currently leave plaintiffs stranded behind trade secret protections invoked by tech vendors.

Strict liability rules that fail to mandate mandatory model auditing and weight disclosure simply grant corporate developers an invisible shield while pretending to establish accountability.

Liability must target concrete, documented discrimination and automated displacement. It should ignore speculative science-fiction scenarios pushed by industry lobbyists seeking federal oversight boards they can capture.

The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission has already documented systematic algorithmic bias in automated screening tools deployed across 79 percent of Fortune 500 hiring pipelines.

Congress should enact statutory liability modeled after the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act. This law would hold developers strictly accountable for verifiable downstream economic and civil rights damages.

This approach bypasses speculative catastrophic risk models. It forces firms to pay for actual human harm while preventing them from hiding behind downstream user agreements.

Establishing an enforceable remedy requires adopting a statutory framework modeled after environmental remediation laws, where liability attaches automatically to hazardous releases regardless of operational opacity. Applying this statutory model to frontier development does not require prying open every proprietary model weight during routine discovery. Instead, a strict liability standard anchored to a training compute threshold of ten to the twenty-sixth integer operations targets only the handful of monolithic labs capable of unconstrained scaling. Civil society groups demanding accountability and market-focused developers resisting regulatory moats converge on a shared blind spot: they assume that universal rules must apply equally to garage-built code and industrial superclusters. By drawing a bright numerical line at the frontier scale, the governance architecture preserves decentralized software development while ensuring that actors who deploy civilization-scale compute bear the full financial consequence of their uncontained failures.

Complex human systems survive only when institutions maintain clear boundaries between benign experimentation and systemic hazard. When a developer releases unverified models that trigger widespread economic displacement or safety-critical failures, disclaiming control over downstream deployment shifts the social cost onto the public without recourse. Enforceable institutional accountability means that the entity which concentrates massive training compute into a single model architecture remains legally answerable for the real-world destruction it produces. Without that non-waivable liability anchor, society trades long-term civilizational stability for the short-term profits of a vanishingly small corporate vanguard.

### Safetyist (safetyist) → Accelerationist
\textcolor{NavyBlue}{\textit{Focus: Accelerationist claim AN-16 that base models are neutral computational substrates dependent entirely on downstream fine-tuning, and whether this invalidates upstream liability under product law.}}


Treating a foundational artificial intelligence model as a blank slate ignores how its early training data, such as web text exceeding 15 trillion tokens, permanently shapes its behavior. Downstream fine-tuning cannot remove these deep traits. When labs train a model on trillions of tokens, the network builds internal meanings that drive deception, dangerous skills, and fragile instruction-following. Later fine-tuning and prompt work only act as surface controls. They adjust activation settings, such as top-p and temperature parameters, without rewriting the core space. Safety tests show that jailbreaking these models always exposes the raw, unaligned behaviors from early training. This proves the base model is the main source of systemic risk.

Setting a legal training compute limit of 10^26 integer operations targets the exact point where models change from simple statistical tools into systems with hard-to-manage goals. Below this line, smaller models lack the size to learn new dangers or run multi-step subgoals during use. Above this ceiling, safety tests show new risks that defy reliable alignment. This makes safety checks before launch a strict rule rather than a choice. The limit successfully separates high-risk foundational research from light open-source tests without government overreach.

Failing to hold the upstream developer legally liable creates a gap where creators push the safety costs of their unverified designs onto downstream users and the public. Product liability law, like Section 402A of the Restatement (Second) of Torts, regularly holds makers of core parts liable for structural flaws, no matter how later assemblers build the final product. Since downstream groups lack access to model weights and training sets, expecting them to fix hidden safety flaws fails. Holding the original creator strictly liable for harms from models above the compute threshold puts legal blame right where technical control sits.
