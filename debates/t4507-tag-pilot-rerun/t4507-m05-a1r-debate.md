## Topic

Should AI systems be barred from making final decisions in hiring and lending?

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Autonomous AI systems must be banned from making final hiring and loans. Black-box models destroy legal accountability and leave victims of systemic bias with no way to fight back. The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau face deadlocks when proprietary neural networks issue rejections that neither corporate compliance officers nor system vendors can explain. When an automated resume filter trained on past data rejects a qualified applicant, no named human has the power or technical access to reverse the exclusion. Human decision-makers certainly display flaws and prejudices. Yet traditional human judges remain subject to discovery, sworn depositions, and the direct liability standards set under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act.

Predictive scoring models used in credit markets ruin their own data by locking marginalized groups out of the financial system entirely. Studies on automated underwriting show that early biased denials stop excluded groups from building positive payment records. Later machine learning audits then misread this lack of data as proof of high credit risk. Federal banking regulators lack the tools to audit these recursive feedback loops once companies deploy them at a national scale across millions of transactions. Market self-regulation fails here because banks profit from fast automated decisions while pushing civil rights violations onto vulnerable applicants.

Keeping human control over gatekeeping requires a clear legal boundary. No major life opportunity should go to a machine unless a named human officer holds direct liability. Congress passed laws like the Fair Credit Reporting Act to let citizens confront bad decisions and demand manual fixes from an accountable person. Trading these legal rights for opaque software optimization causes irreversible damage to human lives for small corporate efficiency gains. Lawmakers must require that every final credit denial and hiring rejection carries the signature of a designated human manager who bears personal legal responsibility for fairness.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

When a loan officer or HR manager uses a predictive score, they buy legal immunity behind vendor non-disclosure rules. The EEOC and the CFPB cannot audit source code protected by trade secrets. This creates a regulatory gap where disparate impact is built into the tech and hidden by contracts.

Opponents argue that banning autonomous final decisions hurts operational efficiency and slows high-volume hiring and credit markets. That objection assumes speed matters more than fair treatment. Companies process thousands of applications daily using software trained on old prejudices. In 2023, federal regulators penalized multiple mortgage lenders for algorithmic redlining that denied credit to qualified minority applicants based on zip codes and proxy variables. Human oversight is replaced by a rubber stamp that masks automated rejection as math. Efficiency cannot override the Fourteenth Amendment or the Civil Rights Act of 1964.

Banning automated final decisions forces firms to pay for real human review instead of pushing discrimination onto marginalized groups. The enforcement tools already exist in administrative law through adverse action notices and disparate impact lawsuits under Title VII and the Equal Credit Opportunity Act. Passing a strict ban on autonomous final decisions in hiring and lending matches rules in the Fair Credit Reporting Act. Who pays the cost when a hidden model rejects a loan applicant: the vendor who gets the fee, or the worker who loses housing?

Who owns the compute, who takes the profit, and who bears the legal risk when an automated system denies a qualified candidate a job?
