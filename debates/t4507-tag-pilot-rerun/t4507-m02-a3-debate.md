## Topic

Under what compute and capability thresholds should federal regulatory agencies mandate pre-deployment licensing for frontier AI training runs to balance existential risk mitigation against open-source developer accessibility?

## Opening Statements

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Federal pre-deployment licensing mandates for frontier artificial intelligence training runs create an insurmountable barrier to human flourishing by protecting corporate incumbents under the guise of safety. When the National Institute of Standards and Technology proposes arbitrary compute thresholds such as ten to the twenty-sixth integer operations, regulators do not stop existential threats. They erect a tollbooth that only trillion-dollar balance sheets can clear, choking off independent academic and startup innovation. This licensing gate forces developers to beg permission from federal bureaucrats before running a training loop, criminalizing open-source distribution in the name of risk mitigation. The historical precedent is clear, matching the restrictive software export controls of the 1990s that crippled domestic cryptography research while failing to stop foreign proliferation.

Static capability thresholds and behavioral compliance metrics become adversarial optimization targets that fail to capture real safety while actively criminalizing open-source development. When the Commerce Department freezes a specific benchmark score as a legal tripwire, labs simply train models to pass that exact test while ignoring systemic vulnerabilities elsewhere. Fixed regulatory metrics penalize transparent open-weight research where thousands of independent auditors inspect the parameters. Closed proprietary labs hide their internal flaws behind federal compliance certificates, leaving the public vulnerable to unobserved systemic failures. Decentralized distribution and permissionless innovation provide the only true security against catastrophic dual-use harms, as observed in the rapid vulnerability patching of open-source software repositories compared to locked-down corporate silos.

Critics argue that without strict pre-deployment licensing for massive compute runs, a rogue actor can autonomously synthesize novel pathogens or launch catastrophic cyberattacks before runtime monitoring catches them. This fear ignores the empirical reality that open-source model weights accelerate vulnerability patching and security research faster than centralized oversight ever could. The Department of Energy and the Federal Communications Commission enforce rules through existing administrative authorities like the Defense Production Act, which federal agencies will leverage to audit GPU clusters and fine unauthorized training runs up to millions of dollars. A coalition of legacy cloud providers and risk-averse bureaucrats supports this licensing regime because it eliminates market competition. Independent developers and open-source foundations bear the entire cost through compliance friction and choked access to high-performance computing infrastructure.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Proponents say that static compute cutoffs like ten to the twenty-sixth integer operations stop bad model use. Yet static FLOP limits miss small, distributed tools and push labs to dodge the rules. Developers just build smaller, smart models below the legal floor that match top models. This bad rule design mimics early phone rules that helped phone monopolies while missing decentralized IP routing. Congress must drop static pre-deployment gates that protect the big labs they aim to curb.

Securities law and civil rights statutes offer better oversight than new licensing boards. Existing rules like SEC Rule 10b-5 already force public firms using AI to disclose big risks. The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission has the power under Title VII to sue firms for hiring bias without making a new federal agency. These tools put costs on firms while keeping open-source code open for all. Federal agencies should use these existing laws instead of building new review boards that get captured by industry.

Using current agencies means they must hire tech staff instead of relying on private audit firms. Critics say sector agencies lack the tech skills to judge complex AI models. But agencies like the Food and Drug Administration successfully built software medical review teams on time. Congress can fund tech staff inside the Federal Trade Commission and the Department of Justice instead of building new licensing boards. Using current agencies works because they already have subpoena power and tested rules.

Yet this market consolidation is a necessary cost when weighed against irreversible harm. Federal agencies must require pre-deployment licenses for frontier training runs exceeding $10^{26}$ integer operations to prevent catastrophic biological or cyber failures. Without upstream gates modeled on FAA airworthiness standards from the Federal Aviation Act of 1958, public safety remains unprotected from unconstrained model spread.

Heavy rules favor established firms over open-source developers, creating upfront compliance costs that concentrate market power. Congress can offset this friction by pairing licenses with federally subsidized safety audits run by NIST. Market competition is not a valid defense when a product has uncontained civilizational risks like engineered pathogens or autonomous cyber weapons.

Enforcing these safety gates requires parent strict liability and model deletion for unverified deployments, ensuring executives face direct legal jeopardy. DOJ and FTC hold statutory authority under Section 5 of the FTC Act to halt non-compliant training runs before weights are finalized and released. Complex human systems survive only when boundaries match the scale of potential failure, and frontier AI represents a threshold where unmitigated deployment guarantees permanent institutional collapse.
