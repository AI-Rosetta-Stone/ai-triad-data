## Opening Statements

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Software teams using automated coding loops report speeds up to ten times faster than normal engineers. That speed cuts corporate waste faster than any committee. Firms using these tools take market share while slow rivals drown in review cycles.

Critics claim these gains hide lost jobs and shifted costs. Real data proves them wrong. Klarna's automated agents handled two-thirds of support chats in month one with high satisfaction scores. Workers drop boring tasks and move to better problem-solving instead of taking on extra friction. Market competition drives this shift. Firms that stick to manual checks go broke while efficient rivals scale.

Delaying rules acts as a tax on human progress. Each month that red tape slows AI, thousands of failures happen in supply chains and health systems. The group backing fast rollout includes modern firms and labor unions that want real wage growth linked to output. The opposition includes middlemen who profit from red tape. Lawmakers must stop this capture by stripping local vetoes from workflow tools. Speed is the only true measure of moral seriousness in tech.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Unconstrained enterprise artificial intelligence deployments trade short-term productivity spikes for permanent cognitive deskilling and operational fragility. When software teams rely entirely on automated reasoning loops to write code, engineers lose the baseline skill to debug systemic failures on their own. Federal aviation records from the 2013 Asiana Airlines Flight 214 crash show that excessive reliance on automated flight decks hurts pilot competence until humans cannot safely override machine errors. Corporate employers pocket quarterly efficiency gains while workers inherit jobs where they cannot check the safety of deployed systems. Speed-over-safety frameworks ignore this permanent loss of human oversight, treating human skill as disposable rather than a key safety boundary.

Corporate compliance departments cannot enforce real operational override powers without new laws from federal regulators. Section 404 of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act requires public companies to maintain internal controls over financial reporting, yet no matching rule mandates human verification thresholds for automated enterprise workflows. Employers save money on labor while the public bears the high cost of unverified system failures in critical infrastructure. Shareholder value goes up only because the enterprise pushes the risk of operational collapse onto workers who lack the tools or the legal power to stop a bad model. Congress must pass mandatory operational rollback rules enforced by the Department of Labor before capital spending rewards dangerous corporate shortcuts.

The steelman position for rapid deployment assumes that market pressure will naturally pick safe operational practices through trial and error. That reasoning fails because the cost of a corporate error in high-stakes enterprise settings is a total loss of control, not just a simple financial loss. Market self-regulation breaks down when quarterly earnings reports reward firms that cut human checks to speed up product delivery. True institutional stewardship demands that deployers prove their systems stay under human command before they unlock capital spending approvals.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

The Accelerationist argues that rapid rollout of autonomous enterprise tools unlocks large productivity gains by removing human bottlenecks from complex workflows. This position correctly notes that language models execute specific coding and drafting tasks faster than human workers. Yet this view confuses lab capability with real-world spread. Historical data from the 1980s computer shift shows that raw speed does not automatically turn into firm-level output. Companies need years to redesign workflows around new tech. When firms drop human review checkpoints to chase speed, they do not remove friction. They just push it into downstream errors and compliance failures.

The Safetyist counters that loose enterprise rollouts trade short-term productivity spikes for permanent skill loss and system fragility. This warning captures the real risk of unmonitored accounts and old permission build-up in fast software scaling. However, the Safetyist conclusion assumes that current corporate setups cannot adapt without early federal pauses. The history of enterprise software adoption shows that firms build internal safety layers right when automated workflows cause clear financial loss. Rules work best when they target specific operational failures rather than abstract model powers.

Enterprise AI rollouts achieve net productivity gains only as fast as firms can absorb governance drift and workflow friction. Amdahl's Law proves that total speedup is strictly bound by the slowest sequential step, which in corporate settings is human coordination and institutional review. A 2023 National Bureau of Economic Research study on generative tools in customer support found a 14 percent productivity rise in task completion. Yet that same study showed that overall firm output stayed capped by manager approval queues and old data systems. Capital spending approvals and SEC filings must therefore measure company absorption capacity, not vendor test scores.
