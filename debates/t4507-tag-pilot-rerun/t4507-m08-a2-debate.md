## Topic

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Corporate leaders push to use workplace artificial intelligence fast without independent safety checks. Federal audits of enterprise software show that missing checks hide heavy admin work. When firms skip checks to chase quick savings, they trade safe controls for blind software. Workers and labor unions face the messy fallout when these tools fail on the job.

Auto workflows speed up human skill loss by taking routine thinking tasks out of daily work. Studies on cognitive offloading show that leaning on smart systems dulls key analytical skills over time. Once workers lose the skill to check errors, the firm loses its vital backup plan. Fixing this skill loss takes costly training that often wipes out the software's early savings.

Critics argue that mandatory safety checks and forced thinking slows down corporate adaptation. This view mistakes vital safety rules for red tape. Federal aviation and civil engineering rules prove that mandatory pauses stop major failures by forcing human checks at key points. Slowing down to think is the price of safety, not a waste to cut.

Keeping human control over smart systems needs strict rollback rules held by named safety officers. When bosses use unchecked software with no override buttons, the firm gives up its power to stop runaway software errors. Congress must demand independent tech audits and named blame before any work tool hits a core wage job. Without these rules, corporate automation swaps weak software guesses for lasting human judgment.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Safetyist claims that leaders rush workplace AI past safety checks, and automated workflows erode human skills. That argument spots the speed hazard. Yet, it treats tech as an external force rather than a tool for work intensification. When workers check machine text all shift, error checks drop because constant tracking kills active focus.

Management cites pilots showing a 30 percent cut in drafting time during rollout. That metric tracks speed on isolated tasks. It ignores the downstream admin drag when workers must fix flawed outputs across core databases.

Labor laws govern mandatory bargaining, but firms call software metrics proprietary rights. This loophole lets firms push quotas that hike strain without raising pay or safety baselines. Fixing this requires updating Section 8(d) of the National Labor Relations Act to force joint audits of workflow tools via the NLRB. Labor backs this reform, while software vendors fight it.

Human safeguards fail because automated settings erode cognitive skills needed for error checks. Studies show workers cut to passive reviewers miss systemic errors at twice the rate of active builders. If AI brought real gains to workers instead of shifting burdens, enterprise software market concentration would drop along with labor grievances. Instead, software budgets grow while union contracts yield zero efficiency gains for the workers doing the labor.
