Chip rules stop foreign rivals from getting advanced gear, which hands guaranteed market share to a few domestic chip makers and big tech firms. Policymakers must stop viewing these limits as neutral physics or national security needs. Who gave private tech giants the right to drain public grid power, and who gets the cash when these systems make unverified guesses?

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Optimization techniques cut training compute needs by orders of magnitude, letting small clusters match frontier powers without tripping energy rules. The Department of Commerce tracks power draws over 100 megawatts, but code compression lets smaller arrays train models at a fraction of that cost. This gap between visible hardware and invisible efficiency breaks physical compute tracking as a reliable rule.

Open-source tools and compression lower the cost to enter, eroding central control and making oversight weak. Small groups running decentralized model tests now match performance once locked in giant labs. Security agencies cannot enforce export rules when code scales across standard hardware without leaving a paper trail. Systems built for physical chips collapse under decentralized software.

Civilian safety needs verifiable control tied to software, not static hardware counts. Congress used the Defense Production Act for physical chips, but that law cannot stop decentralized training. Lawmakers must mandate pre-deployment algorithmic audits and real-time compute checks before models cross dangerous lines. Without software-level rules, physical limits remain an illusion masking blind risk.

Open innovation gives tools of abundance straight to local actors who solve local shortages. Faster rollout is a moral duty because every month of bureaucratic delay stops real human help.
