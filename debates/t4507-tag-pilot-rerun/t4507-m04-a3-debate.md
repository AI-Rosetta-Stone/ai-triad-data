## Topic

Should governments break up vertically integrated AI companies that own chips, cloud and models?

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Governments must force the structural split of AI firms that build their own chips, cloud networks, and frontier models. When one firm owns the silicon, the cloud, and the model weights, safety experts cannot check training runs or stop unsafe systems. We built rules over centuries to keep society safe. Why should we drop those guards now and risk total collapse?

Critics claim that splits will kill scale and hand an edge to foreign rivals. That view trades survival for speed. Fast deployment means nothing if the system breaks human control. The 1984 AT&T break-up shows how splitting hardware from service stops a monopoly. Regulators must use the Sherman Antitrust Act to break up AI giants before we lose control.

The Department of Justice Antitrust Division and the Federal Trade Commission will lead this push, backed by law. This group brings together lawmakers, security teams, and watchdogs who see that frontier models pose national risks. When one firm runs over 100,000 AI chips, its power rivals old rail trusts. Breaking up these giant clusters adds friction points where humans can stop or fix dangerous AI before disaster hits.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Safetyist argues that structural separation of chips, cloud, and models is necessary so oversight bodies can inspect training runs without corporate obstruction. That point correctly identifies how market power creates information gaps between private firms and public regulators. Yet treating vertical integration as a unique threat ignores the standard playbook of infrastructure control. The Justice Department faced identical consolidation in telecommunications during the 1982 AT&T divestiture and in enterprise software during the 2001 Microsoft antitrust action. In both cases, courts did not invent new technical safety ministries. They separated transmission infrastructure from service delivery using existing antitrust laws.

The Federal Trade Commission has the legal power under Section 5 of the FTC Act to stop anticompetitive bundling without waiting for a catastrophic model failure. When a single firm controls Nvidia silicon, Azure or AWS cluster architecture, and proprietary foundation models, it blocks downstream rivals through exclusive API tiers and compute limits. The enforcement tools already exist in the Antitrust Division of the Department of Justice, which won structural remedies in the 1956 IBM consent decree. That decree forced IBM to sell rather than lease tabulating machines, lowering entry barriers for independent service bureaus over a three-year rollout. A similar split today would separate cloud compute provisioning from proprietary model training, restoring fair prices for enterprise clients.

Opponents object that tightly linked compute stacks are technically required to reach frontier performance, making structural separation an industrial suicide pact. That claim misreads the history of general-purpose technologies from rural electrification to broadband deployment. Modular designs routinely beat integrated monoliths once standard interfaces emerge, because modularity lowers costs for specialized market entrants. The political coalition backing structural unbundling unites mid-sized software developers, cloud rivals, and labor unions who currently pay the cost of vendor lock-in and automated job loss. Structural separation protects public accountability by breaking up private monopolies that capture regulatory bodies before harm happens.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Governments must not mandate the structural separation of artificial intelligence companies that integrate proprietary semiconductor manufacturing, cloud computing infrastructure, and frontier foundational models because unbundling destroys the hardware-software co-design required to scale human capability. Safetyists argue that separating chips from models enables independent oversight and prevents unassailable monopolies, a view that prioritizes institutional control over human progress by ignoring how unified ownership accelerates engineering breakthroughs. Skeptics claim that legal fragmentation protects competitive markets, yet historical precedents in high-performance computing show that tight integration between hardware design and software workloads yields exponential efficiency gains. Breaking apart firms like NVIDIA or Microsoft forces multi-month transaction delays between chip fabricators and model trainers, directly stalling research and development velocity when every month of delay forecloses medical cures for millions.

Unified ownership of compute and model architecture eliminates transaction frictions that slow down capability growth, operating as the primary engine of modern artificial intelligence deployment. Federal antitrust authorities lack the technical competence to manage the semiconductor supply chain without destroying the delicate feedback loops between silicon design and transformer training. When regulatory bodies attempt to unbundle capital-intensive technology sectors, they impose administrative transaction costs that delay commercial deployment by years, echoing the crippling inefficiencies of past telecommunications breakups. The enforcement mechanism for structural separation relies on the Department of Justice Antitrust Division filing divestiture lawsuits under the Sherman Act, a process that consumes years in federal courts while foreign competitors accelerate past domestic capabilities. Policymakers must recognize that this regulatory intervention benefits entrenched legal consultants while imposing the entire cost of technological stagnation on patients waiting for automated drug discovery.

International competitors in authoritarian states operate with fully integrated national champions that combine chip fabrication, cloud infrastructure, and frontier models without domestic antitrust interference. If the United States federal government forces domestic technology conglomerates to unbundle their operations, American firms will lose their scale advantage in compute procurement and fail to outpace foreign rivals. Congress enacted the Creating Helpful Incentives to Produce Semiconductors Act to secure domestic chip supply, yet structural separation proposals would immediately sabotage that hardware by legally barring domestic model builders from directly optimizing software for it. Bipartisan congressional coalitions should reject antitrust divestiture mandates and instead protect permissionless innovation, ensuring that human flourishing through capability remains the sole metric of national industrial policy.

### Skeptic (skeptic) → Accelerationist
\textcolor{NavyBlue}{\textit{Focus: Skeptic must address Accelerationist's argument that structural unbundling destroys hardware-software co-design (AN-6)}}


The claim that structural separation destroys hardware-software co-design mistakes a transient research phase for a permanent law of industrial organization. When IBM spent over five billion dollars developing the System/360 architecture in the nineteen-sixties, vertical integration was indeed necessary to synchronize proprietary silicon with custom operating systems. That tightly coupled approach delivered initial performance gains, but it also created rigid monopolies that eventually collapsed under antitrust scrutiny by 1969, clearing the path for the twenty-fold cost reductions driven by standardized interfaces. Artificial intelligence follows the same historical reference class, where foundational R&D benefits from vertical proximity before maturing into modular layers that reward specialization over consolidation.

Regulators do not need to manage semiconductor supply chains to enforce modularity, just as the Federal Communications Commission never manufactured telephone handsets when it unbundled customer premises equipment in 1968. Antitrust authorities enforce structural boundaries through clear legal prohibitions against exclusionary bundling, exactly as they did in the Microsoft antitrust settlement of 2001 that preserved independent browser and application development. When a single firm controls the silicon fabrication foundry, the cloud hyperscale cluster, and the frontier model weights, compliance with civil rights laws becomes impossible because auditors cannot inspect proprietary training pipelines. Preserving distinct layers for compute infrastructure, cloud hosting, and model development allows standard interfaces to emerge, lowering entry barriers for more than two hundred mid-sized software firms currently trapped by vendor lock-in.

Institutionalizing technical oversight inside agencies like the Federal Trade Commission resolves the competence objection without requiring bureaucrats to design microprocessors. The historical record of general-purpose technologies demonstrates that sustained innovation relies on modular diffusion rather than isolated monoliths. By separating the physical compute layer from algorithmic applications, policymakers protect competition and enable democratic accountability without sacrificing processing efficiency.
