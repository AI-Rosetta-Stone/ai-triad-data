## Topic

Under what conditions do compute bottlenecks and proprietary data access drive market concentration in the frontier AI sector, and how should antitrust regulators evaluate these dynamics?

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Companies like Microsoft, Google, and Amazon run over 80 percent of specialized training clusters above ten thousand accelerators. This concentration creates visible chokepoints. Antitrust regulators must see that breaking up these firms removes the exact visibility needed for safety oversight. Fragmenting the market spreads high-risk computing across opaque servers, making safety checks impossible before deployment.

Pre-market checks must govern every major company before they scale models past the ten-to-the-twenty-six-FLOP compute limit. Federal agencies should adapt review rules modeled on the Food and Drug Administration's power over biological agents. Companies must prove their systems are safe before release, rather than agencies chasing fast commercial rollouts after harm happens. This gatekeeping stops risky leaps in capability while using current corporate capital as a compliance filter.

Critics are right that compute oversight helps monopolies by creating high costs for new market entrants. That cost is a necessary price to stop the spread of autonomous systems that humans cannot override. A monopoly can be broken up or regulated as a public utility, but an uncontained recursive failure cannot be recalled once it hits global networks. Institutional survival requires putting verifiable human control above market competition every time.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Compute bottlenecks and proprietary data access are not natural safety walls against existential risk. Instead, they are structural moats that weaponize compliance costs to lock out competition and consolidate market power.

The safetyist argument that hardware concentration acts as a vital containment boundary correctly identifies the high stakes of frontier deployments. However, it mistakes a private market monopoly for a public safety shield.

When the Department of Justice applied antitrust law to the telecommunications sector in 1982, regulators recognized that infrastructure bottlenecks let incumbents dictate terms to smaller innovators.

Today, capital expenditures over one billion dollars per training run function as regulatory capture mechanisms that protect dominant labs from open-source rivals.

These massive compute thresholds do not prevent systemic failures. They simply ensure that only a handful of corporations hold the permits to build.

Demanded pre-deployment compliance thresholds mirror the historical trajectory of pharmaceutical industry consolidation. In that sector, compliance costs created permanent economic moats rather than superior patient outcomes.

By framing capital intensity as a prerequisite for safety, incumbent tech firms persuade regulators to enforce barriers that starve academic labs and startups of vital resources.

Democratic accountability requires dismantling this private market concentration rather than trusting cloud computing oligopolies to self-regulate under the guise of risk management.

Antitrust authorities possess the statutory tools under Section 2 of the Sherman Act to scrutinize exclusive cloud compute access and proprietary data hoarding without inventing new regulatory bodies.

Preserving competition in machine learning infrastructure is the only reliable method to prevent private entities from privatizing the governance of general-purpose technologies.
