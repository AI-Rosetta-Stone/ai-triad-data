Federal policy should target resource tracking and mandatory labor rules instead of chasing ghosts in decentralized code. ### Safetyist (safetyist)

My opponent argues that state export controls and compute caps cannot stop peer-to-peer sharing or local runs on consumer hardware once code is public. This makes upstream limits futile. This objection shows a real enforcement crisis. But it misses where physical limits still work. Once weights reach the public domain via peer-to-peer torrents and offshore backends, they bypass normal access controls.

Regulators must target physical infrastructure telemetry instead of post-release promises to keep upstream control. The Bureau of Industry and Security inside the Commerce Department already runs export controls under the Export Control Reform Act of 2018. They target chip-making gear and high-power compute clusters. Semiconductor cluster aggregations over ten to the twenty-sixth integer operations per second are central chokepoints. These clusters rely on energy grids in specific places. By tying export licenses and permits to real-time hardware telemetry instead of end-user rules, federal agencies keep a tool to act before weights go public.

Releasing weights without verified physical containment risks civilizational stability. No future law can recall an irreversible digital artifact. When the Commerce Department allows public model weight releases without hardware safeguards, the state loses its power to fix unaligned behavior at scale. Society spent centuries building rules for aviation and nuclear power. Dropping these guardrails for AI invites systemic collapse. The Commerce Department must ban unconstrained frontier weight releases until physical compute telemetry guarantees human control remains absolute and enforceable.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

But the Bureau of Industry and Security cannot pull back data once it hits networks like BitTorrent or Hugging Face. Software is speech and math, not a barrel of oil that agents can lock at a port. Safety advocates assume Commerce rules will bind groups outside U.S. reach. This fantasy fails when open-weight models run on foreign consumer GPUs without licenses. Gating public weights protects big cloud firms from open-source rivals while failing to stop the spread. 

Preemptive scaling limits harm people by slowing tools for biology and medicine. Each month that Commerce delays public weights to study risks, patients die while hospitals wait for smart diagnostic agents. Open-source patterns prove that peer work beats slow bureaucratic reviews. The Linux kernel won global reach through open peer review that found flaws faster than any committee. Bureaucrats who demand clearance for model weights value abstract fears over real lives saved by fast science. 

Skeptic rivals admit federal rules cannot stop peer-to-peer sharing, yet they still seek state limits to stop unmonitored tools. That view sees the leaks but draws the wrong point by treating state blocks as leaky instead of dead. The group backing open weights includes indie devs, labs, and mid-size firms who pay the price for rules that help tech giants. Congress should reject Commerce plans to license model weights and let open markets handle compute power without red tape.
