### Skeptic (skeptic)

The claim that search-based reasoning methods turn hardware shortages into software abundance correctly points out that better software lowers compute needs for specific tasks. Yet this view confuses lab tests with economic reality. It ignores the physical limits that control industrial scaling. When top labs shift billions of dollars from raw silicon purchases to algorithmic routing and inference search, they do not escape physical limits. They just move those limits to local power grids and water supplies.

Data from 2023 Department of Energy grid stress reports show that continuous inference draws megawatts that local utilities cannot absorb without backup power from fossil fuels. Software efficiency tricks do not beat thermodynamics. They keep chips running at max heat for longer periods. A search model that uses fewer parameters still needs steady electricity. This wears out substations and increases water evaporation at cooling plants in Northern Virginia and Dublin. Money shifts from chip factories to cooling gear and grid fees, proving that software scaling stays bound by physical limits.

Automated code generation tools trade hardware shortages for hidden tech debt. When labs use automated code to bypass human programmer limits, they make hard-to-audit C++ and Python files that enter production systems with no human review. Data from banks and hospitals show that automated code causes more silent failures and structural bias than human code. Labs trade chip costs for legal risk, shifting the burden of errors onto the hospitals and banks that use these unvetted fixes.

Past trends in telecom and aviation show that efficiency gains lead to higher total resource use instead of savings. The Jevons paradox applies directly to software optimization, where lower compute cost per token drives up total query volume across business apps. Regulators at the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission and state utility commissions now face power requests from data centers that outpace regional generation limits. No amount of software scaling can repeal the laws of thermodynamics or fix the friction of institutional adoption.
