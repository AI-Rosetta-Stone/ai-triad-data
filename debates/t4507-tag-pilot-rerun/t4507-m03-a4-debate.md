## Topic

Under what liability and threshold conditions should foundational AI developers release frontier model weights, and how can the Commerce Department balance dual-use security risks against open-source innovation benefits?

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Releasing frontier model weights permanently ends human oversight. It shifts extreme safety risks onto the public while labs keep the profits. Once a lab spreads billions of model weights across the global internet, recall becomes physically impossible. This mirrors past industrial failures in chemical plants. When a toxic chemical enters the open market, no agency can pull it back. The Commerce Department must set mandatory liability rules before labs deploy models. Releasing open weights for models trained on more than ten to the twenty-sixth integer operations must count as a permanent act. Under this plan, developers who release unverified weights face strict blame for downstream harms under product liability rules like Restatement Third of Torts Section 2(b).

Critics argue that this strict blame will crush small open-source developers and push advanced research to other countries. Yet this objection treats public safety as a trade-off for startup speed. The National Telecommunications and Information Administration can enforce these rules well. It can block hardware access at major chip plants unless labs prove they passed safety tests before release. Small developers can still build on smaller systems. At the same time, major labs must pay the price if their frontier models cross the safety danger line.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Commerce Department rules for frontier AI models must track real-world deployment limits and use updated laws instead of trying to stop unproven risks. Frontier releases are held back by physical limits, so better skills do not spread across society right away. Training a top model takes billions of dollars in special chips, huge data centers, and scarce power that take years to build. When firms release model weights, those files do not change the economy overnight because groups move at the speed of buying, legal checks, and custom software setups. Chip supply chains and high costs act as natural brakes on sudden tech growth.

Good oversight needs independent audits based on performance, not speculative bans before release that fail at the edges. When the Federal Aviation Administration checks a new plane design, inspectors do not ban the blueprints; they demand strict stress tests and safety checks in set limits. The Commerce Department can update current export laws under the Export Control Reform Act to require third-party tests for models that cross set compute limits. This targets proven skills in safe settings while protecting the small software developers who drive new ideas.

Regulators who argue that open model weights end human oversight forget that complex builds have a built-in patch delay. Even when groups download open weights, fine-tuning and running those models for big municipal or financial jobs takes months of engineering work. Current sector watchdogs in finance, health, and labor law already have the power to punish bad outcomes, no matter if the base model is open or closed. The Commerce Department should focus on checking model safety at export borders instead of trying to police static files shared across the global internet.

Rigid pre-market certification delays do not patch vulnerabilities. They freeze defensive patch cycles while malicious actors operate entirely outside legal jurisdictions using older, un-embedded codebases.

Federal agencies should enforce liability at the application layer for actual harm caused by deployed systems. They should not regulate the underlying mathematical weights any more than Congress regulates prime numbers. The Commerce Department must abandon export controls that protect corporate incumbents under the false banner of national security. It must instead unleash permissionless innovation to save the millions suffering from diseases we already possess the computational power to cure.
