## Topic

Under what conditions should the Department of Commerce require frontier AI labs to obtain pre-training licenses to balance catastrophic risk mitigation against regulatory capture and open-source stifling?

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

The Department of Commerce must require pre-training licenses for frontier artificial intelligence labs to stop irreversible harm. High-risk, general-purpose tools need checks before they launch, just as the Federal Aviation Administration tests planes before they fly.

When a model hits the 10 to the 26th power integer operations training threshold, its inner workings become permanently hidden from later audits. Fixes applied after the fact fail because advanced models keep hidden flaws that only show up when used at scale.

Critics argue that rules like this protect big tech monopolies and hurt open-source developers. That point names a real risk of regulatory capture, but market competition does not matter more than human survival when a tool carries unfixable risks.

Congress wrote the Export Control Reform Act of 2018 to give the Department of Commerce power over dual-use tech when national security is at stake. Fees and tiered rules can protect small researchers while keeping strict safety gates on models that cross safe compute limits.

Good oversight must watch the training phase where new skills and self-improvement grow before public release. Frontier labs often see sudden skill jumps during internal work long before any public API goes live.

The Department of Commerce must check server clusters and training logs before gradient descent starts on any large run. If the agency waits until models hit the market, the power to stop the system is gone for good.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Mandatory pre-training licenses for artificial intelligence labs act as rules that protect big firms and hurt open-source tools. The Safetyist argues that the Department of Commerce must force labs to get licenses and check computer clusters before training starts to stop extreme harm. That view correctly spots the risk of severe bio or cyber threats, but it picks the wrong fix. Requiring a federal gate before training big models creates high legal costs that rich firms easily pay. Small developers and open-source groups cannot pay those costs, which leaves the market to a few giants. The Department of Commerce uses export controls and cluster rules to build a system where you need permission to build, much like drug approvals but without laws for liability. History shows that such license schemes cut new market entries by over forty percent in three years, shielding big firms from rivals.

Oversight must shift from sci-fi fears to real harm, such as bias, job loss, and hidden web scraping. Current web-scraping methods used to build training data break copyright norms without public reports or data tracking standards. The Federal Trade Commission and the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission have power to stop unfair trade practices and bias under Section 5 of the FTC Act and Title VII of the Civil Rights Act. Using these existing agencies avoids building a new federal office that would likely be run by former industry workers. A group of civil rights advocates, open-source coders, and antitrust lawyers backs this fix, while big labs lobby for licenses to keep their market lead. The Department of Commerce should focus on supply-chain tracking and data source rules instead of front-end licenses that crush distributed work.
