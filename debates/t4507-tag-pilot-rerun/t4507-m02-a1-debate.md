## Topic

Should the largest AI model training runs require a government licence?

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

We must require government licenses to run the largest artificial intelligence training tasks. Open-weight release past safety limits is an act we cannot reverse. Law courts cannot fix it later. When a lab releases weights past ten to the twenty-sixth integer operations, those numbers scatter across global networks. Federal courts can never recall them once they spread.

Aviation rules taught us this lesson. The Federal Aviation Administration requires airworthiness certificates before planes carry passengers. Lawsuits after a crash offer cold comfort to the bereaved. We applied this same caution to biological select agents under the Public Health Security and Bioterrorism Preparedness and Response Act of 2002. We restrict scaling before dangerous germs escape. Why do tech firms claim an exemption from the safety rules that govern every other high-risk field?

Compute limits serve as the only reliable choke point to govern AI before it leaves human control. Chip factories concentrate advanced tools in a few spots, such as Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company plants in Hsinchu. This makes physical hardware checks both practical and precise. The Department of Commerce already tracks export controls on advanced graphics processing units through the Bureau of Industry and Security. This gives us a working system for hardware oversight.

Critics argue that licensing rules will hand market power to big tech firms. They say high costs will crush compliance and hurt open research. Yet clear compute limits set at high training scales protect startup labs from red tape. They force trillion-dollar firms to prove safety before they train.

Agencies have the skill and political independence to audit frontier training runs if Congress gives them clear enforcement powers and legal liability. The National Institute of Standards and Technology showed this skill through its Artificial Intelligence Safety Institute. It built testing rules for frontier models without falling to industry capture.

When regulators demand safety audits and kill switches before they grant cluster permits, top executives face direct legal liability for safety failures. We spent centuries building guardrails to keep society stable against risky new tech. If a lab skips its license, federal inspectors must have the power to seize illegal hardware clusters at once.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Safetyist argues that un-licensed open-weight distribution of frontier artificial intelligence models creates irreversible systemic risks that demand centralized pre-deployment permits. That argument identifies a real hazard, but it ignores the immediate body count of regulatory delay. When federal agencies choke compute clusters with rules, they trade speculative future harms for guaranteed present suffering from un-cured diseases and technological stagnation.

Licensing rules for large training runs above 10^26 FLOPs act as a tollbooth that only trillion-dollar firms can afford to clear. The Federal Communications Commission used similar broadcast licensing in the 1930s to lock out independent radio stations and protect network monopolies. Today, executive orders like EO 14110 repeat that exact protectionist playbook by putting steep administrative hurdles on high-performance computing clusters.

Open-weight distribution of models stops the dangerous buildup of power inside a handful of monopoly labs. When research groups and startups get foundational weights, they build defensive cybersecurity tools and medical diagnostics faster than any central planner can approve them. The history of open-source software proves that decentralized groups beat closed corporate enclaves in both speed and security.

Dropping open innovation for state licensing does not kill risk. It merely gives control over human progress to captured agencies. The enforcement of these licenses relies on export controls and cloud provider spying, which punishes insurgents while tech giants secure waivers. Congress must reject training run licensing and let decentralized human ingenuity cure the diseases that bureaucratic delay leaves untouched.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

The Safetyist argues that open-weight models create deep risks that demand pre-approval permits. The Accelerationist counters that rules for large training runs act as a tollbooth that only trillion-dollar firms can clear. Both speakers accept the premise that compute limits measure our future. They ignore the material fact that a neural network is just math running on stolen data and fossil-fueled power grids.

Executive Order 14110 builds a wall that shields giant firms from small rivals. The rule sets a compute reporting limit at 10^26 FLOPs, a mark reached only by Microsoft, Google, Meta, and Amazon. This standard does not stop risk. It builds a cartel. The Department of Commerce forces smaller research groups to beg for API access from the exact monopolies facing antitrust probes.

Corporate compliance audits trade public safety for weak labor rules. OpenAI and Anthropic sign safety pledges while firing thousands of low-wage content moderators and data labelers in Kenya and the Philippines. The Federal Trade Commission shows how these firms take copyrighted text without consent or pay. A government license does nothing to stop the theft of human labor.

Critics demand pre-deployment permits above 10^26 FLOPs to stop misuse. This claim assumes federal agencies can police math without serving the industry they rule. History proves this false. The Federal Communications Commission licensed radio waves and locked out independent broadcasters for decades. Compute licensing repeats that exact monopoly grab.

Every month that permissionless experimentation is frozen by administrative review boards is a month patients wait for automated cures to genetic diseases and antibiotic-resistant pathogens. Precautionary stagnation is not a neutral pause, but an active choice to let people suffer from problems that computational abundance could solve today.

- Advanced compute infrastructure is highly concentrated geographically and economically among a small group of hardware manufacturers and cloud providers. (Accelerationist, Safetyist, Skeptic)
- Static compute thresholds risk functioning as protective regulatory moats for dominant firms unless paired with responsive governance mechanisms. (Safetyist, Accelerationist, Skeptic)
- Mandatory licensing frameworks can impose compliance burdens that affect open-source research and smaller market participants. (Safetyist, Accelerationist)

  - *Resolution path: negotiable via tradeoffs*
