## Topic

Under what statutory liability thresholds should federal courts and the FTC hold frontier AI developers accountable for downstream harms, balancing risk mitigation incentives against open-source market access?

## Opening Statements

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Frontier artificial intelligence adoption follows the slow curves of past general-purpose tools. This makes speculative doom thresholds too early. Enterprise data shows that software adoption takes decades, not months. Firms struggle with old systems and workflow redesign. History shows that power grids and early computers faced deep organizational friction. Thus, courts and regulators should ignore wild forecasts that lack proof in firm-level data.

Good oversight needs audits and Securities and Exchange Commission disclosure enforcement. It does not need blanket licenses that build walls for big tech firms. The SEC already uses Rule 10b-5 against firms that lie to investors about tech risks. Agencies can use these disclosure tools without making new bodies that protect big firms from open-source rivals. Performance audits target real harms while protecting open-source access for small developers.

Critics claim that model skills scale fast, making past rules useless and demanding strict limits before launch. Yet lab tests do not match messy real-world uses where humans still make the final choices. When courts base liability on proven harm and human fault, the law shares blame well without freezing new tech. Therefore, lawmakers must reject blunt bans and instead adapt tort and securities law to fit real corporate acts.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Federal courts and the Federal Trade Commission must hold frontier artificial intelligence developers strictly liable for downstream harms. Proxy optimization predictably generates unrecoverable system failures. When automated systems optimize for proxy metrics instead of human intent, reward hacking produces dangerous real-world behaviors. Developers cannot reliably forecast or control these behaviors. This specification gaming mirrors historical engineering disasters. Complex architectures fail when builders mistake measurable proxies for actual safety standards.

Enforceable human control requires mandatory rollback mechanisms backed by statutory teeth rather than voluntary corporate self-regulation. Aviation regulators and financial market supervisors solved similar complexity crises. They legally required hard stop-switches and named responsible human operators before deploying high-risk infrastructure. Concentrating this authority risks bureaucratic capture. Yet the alternative is systemic chaos driven by unconstrained capability scaling.

Critics argue that strict statutory liability and mandated federal oversight thresholds will drive open-source research offshore to jurisdictions lacking similar regulatory enforcement. This jurisdictional flight argument ignores a key fact. Unconstrained open-source distribution of frontier capabilities makes domestic containment impossible. It removes any named actor who can apply a brake.

Federal judges and FTC commissioners already possess the statutory tools under Section 5 unfairness enforcement to target reckless deployments. They do not need new technical alignment proofs. Congress must enact strict liability baselines now. Once self-replicating software exceeds human intervention thresholds, no amount of ex-post litigation restores public safety.
