## Topic

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Federal antitrust agencies waste months trying to break up cloud-compute partnerships between giant tech firms. The Federal Trade Commission and the Department of Justice treat frontier artificial intelligence models like standard consumer goods that need market competition. This commercial focus ignores the physical reality of hardware limits and the threat of unrecoverable model failure. We spent centuries building guardrails that keep society stable. On what authority do you claim we can remove them without inviting collapse?

Structural antitrust rules create high compliance costs that lock smaller developers out of building base models. When regulators force large cloud providers to split up compute clusters or license code, they create legal costs that only giant monopolies can pay. Small research groups cannot afford the multi-million dollar compliance audits demanded by consent decrees. Oversight without safety rules just puts power into fewer corporate hands.

Cloud infrastructure enforcement stays blind to decentralized hosting and foreign countries. A global firm can easily move training runs to servers in nations outside U.S. reach when domestic regulators restrict local cluster space. Market fixes fail when the target asset is just digital weights that cross borders in seconds. Effective governance requires treaties that physically limit training runs above ten to the twenty-sixth power floating-point operations instead of courtroom fights over corporate structure.

Pre-deployment checks must come before any naive attempt to force market competition through structural breakups. The Department of Justice cannot inspect model weights for recursive self-improvement vectors through civil demands. Congress must instead authorize independent safety boards to halt training runs when developers cannot prove their systems stay under human control. Speed is not a virtue when failure means permanent loss.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Federal antitrust actions by the FTC and DOJ against cloud partnerships fail to curb market concentration because regulators miss circular compute deals and let big tech shift deployment risks. The Safetyist says federal agencies waste months trying to break up cloud partnerships between tech giants. The Accelerationist insists these antitrust moves do not stop monopoly power and starve open-source growth. Both speakers see that current laws are slow. Yet they treat base model building as a rare tech awakening instead of a costly utility buildout. Their core debate asks if state action helps or hurts innovation. But this misses a key fact: private cloud oligopolies control the physical gear.

Cloud giants make massive compute profits while pushing operational risks onto smaller firms using contract shields and circular money flows. Startups face squeezed margins because cloud firms set compute prices while funding base model developers through cloud-credit deals. These circular deals are not fair risk-sharing tools that help open competition, as defenders claim. They are tollbooths built to lock users into proprietary hardware. When Microsoft or Amazon routes billions back into Azure or AWS compute clusters, market concentration grows, no matter if federal lawyers sue under Section 2 of the Sherman Act.

Money and production pressures in cloud markets drag down safety and labor standards across the supply chain. Compute clusters need massive power grids, using gigawatts while shifting carbon costs onto local ratepayers and towns. Meanwhile, safety reviews are rushed to meet quarterly targets set by bosses who face zero risk when automated systems fail. Regulators can block stock deals under the Clayton Act. But until federal agencies make cloud computing a regulated utility with structural splits and ratepayer rules, antitrust work just rearranges deck chairs on a private monopoly.
