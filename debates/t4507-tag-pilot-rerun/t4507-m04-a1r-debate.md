## Topic

Should governments break up vertically integrated AI companies that own chips, cloud and models?

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Vertical ties across chips, cloud servers, and base models create a hidden workspace that blocks safety checks. When firms like Microsoft run both Nvidia tensor processing units and frontier models like GPT-4, outside watchers lose the physical vantage point needed to inspect internal weight alignments. This setup mirrors the 1982 AT&T telecom monopoly, where total control over gear and service let the firm block independent safety audits. Congress must force structural splits under Section 2 of the Sherman Antitrust Act to restore limits that stop systemic failure.

Opponents argue that breaking up these giant firms hurts national tech competitiveness against foreign rivals. This objection misses the main danger by picking speed over proof in a field with permanent failure modes. Past aviation safety rules show that the Federal Aviation Administration demands outside checks because makers facing sales pressures cannot fairly test their own planes. Breakups add a needed speed bump to make sure human leaders keep the physical power to stop risky traits before total crashes happen.

Independent safety boards need clear legal power and technical freedom to watch split firms without falling to agency capture. The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission shows how cutting ties between power plants and power lines stops grid tricks via clear operational walls. Applying this unbundling standard to AI ensures that cloud firms cannot favor their own models during safety stress tests. Public safety demands institutional firewalls that put the power to halt computing outside the direct control of profit-driven firms.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Hardware-level control of chip supply chains and custom parts acts as a tollbooth that stops new firms from entering the market. Firms like NVIDIA and Microsoft control the physical silicon lines, the tensor chips, and the cloud access needed to train frontier models. This is not free market synergy. It is a vertical roadblock built to take rents from every layer of the digital economy. When one firm owns the chip design, the server rack, and the model weights, no rival can rent compute without paying a market rival.

Split regulatory silos fail to check cross-domain market power, letting big firms hide inside vertically integrated boxes. The Federal Trade Commission and the Department of Justice must drop piecemeal rules and use the breakup model of the 1982 AT&T case on physical AI gear. Splitting chip plants and cloud compute from model building is the only way to bring back market competition. This structural split strips big firms of their power to use hardware subsidies to crush independent software rivals.

Critics will argue that breaking up chips, cloud, and models destroys the high capital synergy needed to train frontier systems, hurting national scale. Yet that claim rests on the false guess that intelligence needs corporate size instead of open markets. Power grids and chip plants show that hardware is a public utility, not a playground for monopolies. Congress has the legal power under Section 2 of the Sherman Act to force structural splits when bottlenecks kill fair prices. Policymakers must stop treating silicon-to-cloud bundling as a law of physics and start treating it as an illegal restraint of trade.
