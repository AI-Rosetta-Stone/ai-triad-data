## Topic

Should AI systems be barred from making final decisions in hiring and lending?

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Corporations must not use artificial intelligence to make final hiring and loan choices. Complex algorithmic models make legal blame hard to prove. When the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission checked automated resume tools in 2023, investigators found that deep neural networks processed thousands of hidden variables without a clear reason. Traditional tort law uses the reasonable person standard to prove fault when a human hurts a worker or borrower. Autonomous scoring models break this standard. They spread math weights across millions of parts that no boss can check or explain. Without a clear chain of events, injured plaintiffs cannot prove fault in court. This leaves companies free from legal blame for the harm they cause. Therefore, the law must ban automated final choices until fault can link to a real human.

Automated rejections break the feedback loops needed for tests after launch. They filter out the exact groups needed to check the model. A mortgage algorithm that denies loans to minority applicants stops future data collection for those groups. This hides its own systemic bias from regulators. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau noted in 2022 that closed data loops stop lenders from finding bias growth after launch. Human loan officers leave a paper trail of exceptions that regulators can check during bank exams. Algorithmic systems seal the denial code permanently. This turns past statistical bias into permanent structural exclusion.

Critics claim that mandatory human sign-off creates a costly bottleneck that slows hiring and lending. This view assumes that humans can review thousands of AI outputs without falling for automation bias. Studies on work choices show that humans approve machine choices ninety percent of the time in high-volume jobs. Real human control needs more than a click box that tired managers ignore. True human oversight needs the power to stop, check, and overrule AI systems before harm happens. This must be backed by fines from the Federal Trade Commission.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Automated screening in credit and hiring is not a brand-new frontier. It is a mature tool with proven bias under the Equal Credit Opportunity Act. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission have the legal power to audit opaque underwriting models and resume-filtering software. These agencies can enforce civil rights standards when courts demand clear algorithmic reasons instead of accepting corporate black-box defenses. Banning automated final decisions ignores how traditional credit bureaus worked under the Fair Credit Reporting Act since 1970.

Corporations use automated hiring and lending systems to cut processing costs and hide bias behind secret code. When a bank denies a mortgage using an unexplainable machine learning model, it shifts the legal burden onto marginalized applicants who lack the money to subpoena training datasets. This red tape protects corporate balance sheets instead of helping working-class borrowers. To fix this, federal agencies must require mandatory algorithmic impact assessments and slap steep fines on companies for discriminatory outcomes.

Critics argue that federal agencies lack the technical skills and funds to audit fast-changing machine learning models before companies launch them. That challenge is real. Yet, building a new federal bureaucracy would cause the same delays that crippled early aviation and telecom oversight. Congress can fund expert technical audit units inside the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission within twelve months. Upgrading current agencies beats inventing untried groups that leave workers unprotected during long legislative fights.
