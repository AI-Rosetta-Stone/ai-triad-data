## Topic

Under what conditions should export control authorities permit frontier AI labs to release model weights publicly, balancing open-source innovation against security risks?

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Export control authorities must treat frontier model weights above ten to the twenty-sixth training FLOPs as restricted dual-use assets to stop irreversible public leaks. When labs share raw weights on peer-to-peer networks, downstream users strip upstream safety filters within hours. Just like past cryptographic export rules under the International Traffic in Arms Regulations, digital files cannot be contained once they become public. Congress must therefore require checks by the Bureau of Industry and Security before labs move model weights out of secure datacenters.

Critics argue that post-release fine-tuning checks and runtime guards can track distributed systems after weights go public. This ignores the core asymmetry of distributed software, where downstream users can strip alignment data and safety tools locally without detection. Past open-source cases show that once code copies across global networks, complete recall is technically impossible. Agencies cannot patch a security flaw that has already spread to millions of private nodes.

The enforcement plan relies on mandatory pre-release hardware audits and cryptographic watermarks backed by the Export Control Reform Act of 2018. Industry leaders and open-source advocates will fight these rules, saying they hurt academic research and market competition. National security agencies and civil groups will back these steps to keep human control over smart systems. If authorities fail to set up these checkpoints, society loses its last chance to govern unaligned systems before deployment.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Export controls on frontier model weights act as cartel tools. They lock in market capture while pretending to manage risk. Safetyist claims that restricting models above ten to the twenty-sixth training FLOPs stops wild spread. This point notes the true scale of frontier training runs, but it confuses corporate containment with public safety. The Bureau of Industry and Security already runs dual-use export rules. These rules protect big firms by hiding behind trade-secret claims.

When labs demand weight limits, they do not defend national security. They build a moat to block open-source rivals. Vendor license rules prove that closed systems block outside audits of deployed software. Closed-source firms hide failure rates behind walls. They lobby export control groups to outlaw weight releases from smaller rivals.

This rule capture hands power to a few dominant firms. These firms take the cash while the public takes the risk. No outside expert can check safety claims when the code stays locked in corporate hubs. Democratic oversight needs local community auditors in every phase of AI work. We must not hand control to opaque corporate compliance rules.

Past failures in dual-use tech markets show that self-control always fails when cash rewards speed over safety. Export control groups should tie export licenses to open third-party tests and community audits. They should not ban public weights outright. If the tech is truly unsafe, locking it behind corporate paywalls does not fix the risk. It just lets corporations pick who gets hurt.
