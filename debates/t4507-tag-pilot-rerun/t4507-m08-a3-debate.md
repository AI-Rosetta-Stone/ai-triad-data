## Topic

Under what structural conditions do enterprise AI deployments yield measurable productivity gains rather than task displacement, and how should corporate leadership and labor representatives negotiate these workflow transitions?

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Enterprise software deployments that replace human judgment with automated workflows cause a permanent loss of baseline worker skill. When firms remove human friction from complex tasks, operator skill decays within eighteen months. Decades of aviation automation studies prove this trend. Leaders chase short-term quarterly output gains while they outsource the cognitive skills needed for long-term survival. We spent centuries building guardrails to keep society stable. Removing those boundaries without a verified rollback plan invites catastrophic operational collapse.

Narrow productivity metrics hide the slow collapse of trust and worker agency during restructuring. Firms tracking only speed and volume miss the silent loss of memory when mid-level staff stop doing core analytical work. The National Transportation Safety Board documented this exact failure in automated rail and aviation control systems, where operators lost the ability to step in during crises. Labor groups must demand strict human-in-the-loop rules before any software contract gets board approval.

Critics argue that forced cognitive friction slows firms down in fast-moving markets. That objection relies on a false economy that treats human mental decay as a fine cost of speed. Federal regulators enforce strict environmental impact reviews under the National Environmental Policy Act before firms build physical plants. Enterprise software buying needs an identical rule to force cognitive impact studies before models replace human oversight.

Federal procurement policy under the Executive Order on Artificial Intelligence provides clear liability frameworks that penalize reckless deployment while protecting permissionless innovation. ### Skeptic (skeptic)

The accelerationist says zero cost to copy models makes AI spread faster than electricity or the internet. That view confuses how fast files move with how fast companies can change. Enterprise tech takes time because of manager limits, old systems, and worker training. In the 1990s, enterprise software was sold a decade before it raised firm productivity. Free file downloads do not remove the friction of changing how forty thousand workers process invoices.

Automating tasks without human oversight ruins baseline skills and creates institutional amnesia. Data from aviation autopilots and automated trading desks show a clear failure mode. When routine tasks are stripped from humans, they fail to catch edge-case system crashes. Bosses buy software to replace labor while workers lose skills and institutional knowledge. Collective bargaining agreements must require dual-path training rather than unilateral deskilling.

The safetyist fears that unmanaged automation kills baseline human judgment in critical sectors. Yet proposing new agencies ignores existing institutions that already handle these shifts. The National Labor Relations Board and the Department of Labor have the power to oversee tech displacement through collective bargaining. Updating labor law to require impact-bargaining costs less and works faster than building a new bureaucracy. Labor and management negotiated containerization on the docks using transition funds, not by banning steel boxes.
