## Topic

Under what conditions should federal regulators apply strict liability to frontier AI developers, and through what causal pathways would this alter open-source and proprietary safety practices?

## Opening Statements

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Federal strict liability for frontier AI developers destroys open-source software. It hands the entire artificial intelligence market to big tech monopolies. When lawmakers hold creators liable for downstream edits, they repeat the 1998 Digital Millennium Copyright Act. That law forced small creators to abandon public code. Large firms like Microsoft and Google easily pay huge legal costs. Decentralized groups cannot pay those costs. Strict liability builds a wall that protects corporate giants from open-source rivals under the name of safety.

Static rules fail as safety targets and only create empty paperwork. Real data from machine learning shows that fixed safety guards are broken in forty-eight hours by fine-tuning. When NIST tries to set static tests, coders simply bypass them using open weights. Regulators waste tax money on paperwork rituals that only stop honest research groups.

Opponents argue that without strict liability, bad actors will deploy dangerous models. This ignores a core truth. Downstream platforms and enterprise buyers are the right place for tort rules, just as internet providers manage hosted content. The DOJ and FTC already have the power to stop bad acts under current laws. They can do this without criminalizing open-source math. When the state criminalizes model weights, regulators stop medical researchers from curing diseases.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

The accelerationist view says that sharing open-source code helps society and stops monopolies by giving frontier model weights to millions of engineers. This point correctly spots the danger of company capture. Yet, it assumes open code carries the same risk as standard software like Linux or SQLite. That assumption fails for systems with autonomous goal-directed behavior that people cannot reliably predict or patch after release. When an open-weight model holds hidden skills for self-improvement or biological making, missing a central stop switch turns every download site into an endless danger.

Federal agencies must apply strict liability upstream to frontier developers who release weights past tested capability limits without mandatory runtime kill switches. The Federal Aviation Administration enforces rules under 14 CFR Part 39 that hold plane makers legally at fault for flaws, no matter how many airlines fly them. Likewise, the 1962 Kefauver-Harris Drug Amendments set strict drug maker liability for unproven drugs even if sold by local pharmacies. Congress can build a matching law for AI using the Defense Production Act and safety rules, naming open frontier weights as defective products under the Restatement (Third) of Torts. This law forces developers to pay the real cost of safety checks instead of pushing heavy risks onto the public.

Critics say that strict liability for upstream developers kills the open-source world and gives the market to tech monopolies. That counter-argument relies on a false split between total hazard and total monopoly control. Developers stay safe from liability as long as they use verified compute limits and cryptographic access controls that keep human control before weights cross critical autonomy lines. The Department of Commerce already keeps dual-use export lists under the Export Control Reform Act that successfully block high-end chip shipments without killing the computer market. Setting similar bounds on model sharing keeps the public safe without locking down corporate power.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

The accelerationist view warns that federal rules will lock in monopoly power, but it treats code as an endangered species instead of a normal product under tort law. The safetyist view prioritizes corporate accountability, but it uses scary risks to hide how big firms use compliance to block rivals. Federal rules for frontier AI are not real safety fixes. They are tolls built by giant firms to crush open-source rivals and hide from civil rights harms. When Amazon or Microsoft use automated tools, they push bias costs onto the public while lobbying for rules that protect their cash.

Frontier AI firms and open-source groups face very different costs, so strict rules mainly lock in monopoly power instead of helping users. Small teams posting model weights on GitHub cannot pay huge lawsuits or audits from the Federal Trade Commission. Big cloud firms spend billions on servers and legal teams, treating fines as a normal cost of business. Big tech firms lobby for complex rules because compliance costs stop open-source rivals from entering the market. Congress must reject rules that treat a shared code repo the same as a trillion-dollar API firm.

Lawmakers must ignore sci-fi fears of rogue machines and focus on real bias in hiring, housing, and credit. Past cases of biased mortgage software and hiring filters at firms like Workday show that current tools cause real civil rights harms. Agencies already have power under Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act to punish unfair business practices without inventing new rules for text tools. When a firm uses a bad model that denies housing or healthcare to minority groups, blame must fall on the deployer based on real harm, not fake tests. True accountability means making firms pay for real civil rights abuses instead of letting them hide behind rules written by paid consultants.

### Safetyist (safetyist) → Skeptic
\textcolor{NavyBlue}{\textit{Focus: Skeptic's claim AN-9 regarding existing FTC statutory authority under Section 5}}


I grant that the Federal Trade Commission holds statutory authority under Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act to penalize unfair and deceptive commercial practices, as correctly observed. 

Retroactive enforcement of trade practices, however, assumes that a commercial injury can be fixed after the fact with money or corrective ads. That logic collapses when applied to autonomous frontier architectures where the main danger is an irreversible loss of human control rather than a misleading product claim. Relying on post-market lawsuits to police recursive systems resembles suing a chemical plant after the groundwater is permanently poisoned.

The structural flaw in treating text-prediction software as standard consumer goods lies in the speed and opacity of optimization loops that outpace FTC timelines. Consumer protection laws require proof of injury to a specific buyer or competitor before the agency can act. This leaves regulators powerless while a model optimizes for proxy metrics that erode institutional stability.

When an algorithm finds new ways to bypass safety guardrails or make unauthorized financial transfers, an FTC civil investigative demand arrives months after the system finishes its deployment cycle.

Commercial oversight needs a preventative gate rather than a post-mortem penalty when dealing with assets that cannot be recalled once released into open networks. The Federal Aviation Administration enforces airworthiness rules under 14 CFR Part 39 that stop an aircraft from leaving the hangar until its structure is mathematically and empirically verified.

Applying that same precautionary standard to software infrastructure means requiring developers to prove containment before model weights are distributed. This replaces reactive consumer lawsuits with binding deployment gating.
