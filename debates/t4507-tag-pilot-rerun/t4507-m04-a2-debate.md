## Topic

Under what market concentration conditions should antitrust authorities like the FTC pursue structural separation of vertically integrated AI firms, and through what pathways would breaking up compute, cloud, and model layers protect independent developers?

## Opening Statements

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Antitrust agencies must break up cloud computing, chip supply, and model design before three firms control the whole machine learning stack. Microsoft, Amazon, and Google own over 65 percent of global cloud servers and control the specialized chips needed to train frontier models. When one firm owns the graphics processing units, the cloud contract, and the AI model, small developers do not join a fair market. They just pay rent to a landlord who owns the foundation.

Critics argue that splitting these layers destroys the economies of scale needed to train large models and hands the tech lead to foreign rivals. That claim relies on a false idea that huge corporate size equals technical efficiency. The Department of Justice broke up AT&T in 1982 by separating local phone service from equipment making, which sparked a multi-billion-dollar boom in independent networking gear. Rules can force cloud giants to lease compute at fair rates without stopping them from building data centers.

To enforce this split, the Federal Trade Commission and the European Commission must use Section 5 of the FTC Act and Article 102 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union to ban cheap internal compute pricing. Any cloud provider with over 40 percent of the market must spin off its AI model division into an independent firm with separate books. A coalition of enterprise software buyers, independent AI startups, and state attorneys general will back this move because it cuts their costs. Venture capital funds and corporate lobbyists will fight it to protect their integrated monopolies.

Strip away the marketing hype about artificial general intelligence and you find a heavy industrial machine. It uses massive amounts of municipal power and water cooling to predict text strings. The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission reported that data center power use topped 15 gigawatts in 2024, straining local grids while big tech firms took the subsidies. Independent AI startups pay the price for power shortages and high chip markups while tech giants take the cash. Who pays the electric bill for the training cluster when the model output is just sold back to the public as a closed API?

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Skeptic is right that chip supply and cloud server control by three firms threatens AI. Antitrust agencies must force a split when one company owns over fifty percent of model training compute and cloud hosting at once. The FTC and the DOJ have the power under the Sherman Act to break up these firms, just like the nineteen eighty-two breakup of AT&T. Without these hard lines, independent developers cannot audit frontier models, and safety checks become fake.

Owning most advanced hardware creates a bottleneck that stops outside experts from watching what happens. Three big tech firms control over eighty percent of top chips, keeping independent safety researchers out of the loop. When compute, cloud, and AI models sit on one balance sheet, bosses can ignore safety rules whenever they want to launch a product fast. Regulators must split the tech layer from model building so no single balance sheet controls both the hardware and the launch choice.

Critics claim that breaking up the AI stack hurts money flow and national strength against foreign rivals. This falls apart because rushed, unproven systems pose huge risks that speed cannot fix. The Sherman Act and modern rules exist to stop private monopolies from vetoing public safety. Congress passed the Atomic Energy Act in nineteen forty-six to keep public watch over risky dual-use tech, giving a clear model for separating danger from business. Splitting compute and model layers keeps humans in charge without killing long-term tech growth.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

The Skeptic says antitrust agencies must break up cloud computing, chip supply, and model design before three firms control the entire machine learning stack. The Safetyist notes that three hyperscalers control over eighty percent of enterprise GPUs, locking independent safety researchers out of the loop. These points correctly name the dangers of centralized power, but their remedy of structural separation relies on a fatal error about how frontier AI scales. Breaking apart compute, cloud, and model layers through antitrust unbundling creates regulatory friction that halts progress by destroying the co-designed hardware and training loops needed for rapid gains.

Frontier scaling laws require an unbroken pipeline connecting semiconductor supply chains directly to cluster architecture and model training loops. Any state-mandated breakup fractures the technical engine of exponential progress. When the FTC or DOJ uses structural separation like the 1984 AT&T divestiture, regulators ignore the gap between static telecoms and dynamic machine learning. This enforcement relies on court monitors and perpetual antitrust lawsuits from the DOJ, creating delays that cost humanity years of withheld medical and scientific automation. Policymakers must see that every month spent litigating corporate unbundling in federal court is a month where fatal diseases remain uncured and suffering continues.

Open-source weight proliferation breaks up oligarchic market control faster and better than state breakups, giving developers worldwide immediate cognitive utility without decade-long legal fights. Meta releasing Llama models and independent devs distributing quantized weights to edge devices bypasses hyperscaler gatekeeping through permissionless code instead of bureaucratic intervention. The enforcement tool for democratizing access is frictionless open distribution backed by export policies that protect open weights from state capture. When enterprise buyers and startups access top base models on public repositories without paying platform rents, market power diffuses naturally through competitive abundance rather than top-down judicial surgery.

Who benefits from structural separation besides incumbent legal teams and bureaucrats who expand their turf over the digital economy at the cost of human flourishing? The political coalition opposing structural separation unites engineering teams, open-source developers, and patient advocacy groups who need rapid AI growth to solve hard biological and material limits. Historical precedent from the 1990s Microsoft antitrust case shows that government remedies targeting software integration waste a decade of industry focus while market forces make the carved-up monopoly irrelevant anyway. Breaking the machine learning stack to satisfy a static theory of market share equity trades real human lives saved by fast tech deployment for abstract administrative symmetry.

### Skeptic (skeptic) → Accelerationist
\textcolor{NavyBlue}{\textit{Focus: Address the 1990s Microsoft antitrust precedent (AN-10) and explain how structural separation avoids consuming a decade of regulatory attention while still solving the compute bottleneck.}}


The 1990s Microsoft antitrust settlement consumed a decade of industry attention in unproductive lawsuits over software code. That administrative paralysis proves the failure of conduct rules rather than the futility of structural intervention. Regulators tried to police how operating systems bundled web browsers. This forced courts to act as permanent software supervisors over commercial design choices. That quagmire happened because antitrust authorities left the core corporate structure intact while trying to micromanage product features through endless compliance fights.

Structural separation skips software code entirely. It divides physical hardware assets along natural economic boundaries instead of policing product design. The Department of Justice proved this in 1982. It unbundled local telephone switching from equipment manufacturing. This instantly sparked independent hardware innovation without halting telecom deployment. Applying that same split to artificial intelligence means forcing hyperscalers who control over sixty-five percent of global cloud infrastructure and proprietary graphics processing units to spin off their model units into independent firms with separate financial books.

Leaving compute, cloud, and model layers fused inside three corporate monopolies guarantees that startups and software vendors remain captive tenants. They must rent capacity on terms set by incumbents. Federal regulators note that data center power use surged past fifteen gigawatts to feed these proprietary silos. This energy grid strain stems directly from unregulated corporate consolidation rather than unavoidable physical necessity. Breaking up the hardware stack shifts control back to public utility standards and independent market participants. It avoids the source-code fights that crippled past software settlements.

How can policymakers justify protecting corporate integration under the banner of speed when that exact concentration creates the grid bottlenecks and market exclusions that delay actual technology deployment?
