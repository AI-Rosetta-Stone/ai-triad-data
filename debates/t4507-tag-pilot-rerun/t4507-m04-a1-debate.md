## Topic

Under what competitive conditions should the FTC and DOJ Antitrust Division mandate ownership unbundling of chips, cloud infrastructure, and foundation models to prevent downstream market foreclosure?

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Federal antitrust leaders must force tech giants to split up their chip supplies, cloud servers, and AI models. If they do not act, these firms will lock out all competition. When one firm owns the chips, the data centers, and the AI code, it kills independent safety checks. That kind of power creates a direct path to irreversible harm.

Big tech firms currently use separate corporate units to dodge liability for risky tech rollouts. Past aviation and financial crashes prove that unified firms dump severe risks on the public while keeping the profits. The Federal Trade Commission and the Justice Department have the legal power under Section 7 of the Clayton Act to stop this merger wave before the market locks up.

Critics claim that breaking up integrated AI stacks will wreck economies of scale and hand global markets to foreign rivals. Speed without checks is not an advantage; it is a hidden liability that guarantees a crash. We took centuries to build guardrails against uncontained disasters. Dropping those limits for AI invites total ruin.

Splitting up these firms means parent companies must face strict liability and give up algorithms that cause major harm. The Antitrust Division must enforce this via mandatory breakups modeled on the 1982 AT&T split. If regulators fail to draw this hard line now, no human authority will ever be able to stop an unaligned system.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Safetyist argues that splitting compute supply from model training protects market access. But this logic treats physical hardware and software intelligence as static commodities rather than rapidly co-evolving systems.

When Nvidia pairs H100 GPUs directly with custom interconnect fabrics and optimized PyTorch runtimes, the resulting training speedup exceeds thirty percent compared to fragmented deployment. Breaking these unified stacks apart through DOJ and FTC ownership mandates destroys the exact feedback loops required to scale intelligence.

The enforcement mechanism for such breakups would rely on bureaucratic oversight boards modelled after the 1984 AT&T divestiture. This replaces dynamic engineering with years of litigation and stagnation.

Open-source diffusion and market deployment break corporate concentration far faster than legacy antitrust breakups ever could. The opposition assumes that hyperscalers maintain permanent downstream market foreclosure. Yet open-weights models from Meta and Mistral now match proprietary benchmarks while running on decentralized commodity hardware.

Over seventy percent of enterprise developers deploy open-source weights rather than locked cloud APIs. This proves that market competition undercuts monopoly rent-seeking without state asset division. Congressional sponsors of the American Innovation and Choice Online Act ignore this rapid decentralization curve. They substitute slow judicial remedies for the organic velocity of open code.

If the FTC forces cloud providers to spin off their AI model divisions, capital spending drops by billions of dollars. This directly delays the computing infrastructure needed to automate medical diagnostics and drug discovery.

Abundance requires permitting tight vertical integration to fund and deploy the next generation of compute clusters. Policymakers must weigh the human cost of regulatory delay against speculative fears of downstream foreclosure. Every month spent adjudicating structural breakups leaves millions suffering from curable diseases.

The political fight to block these antitrust rules relies on uniting tech innovators and patient groups against central state planners. These planners prioritize market structure over human flourishing.

Historical precedent from the 1990s Microsoft antitrust settlement shows that structural remedies took a decade to execute. Meanwhile, markets moved past the contested software entirely. Letting central regulators micromanage chip supply chains guarantees that bureaucratic inertia replaces technological liberation.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

The accelerationist counters that co-designed vertical integration remains the sole engine capable of driving frontier scale. Both sides mistake a corporate land grab for a law of physics. They treat the machine as an autonomous god that demands either total submission or total containment.

Vertical integration of chips, cloud infrastructure, and foundation models is not an immutable law of engineering. It is a corporate strategy designed by firms like Microsoft and Amazon to secure permanent monopoly rents over public resources. Historical precedent exists in the 1982 AT&T breakup, which split equipment making from service delivery without halting telecom progress. Regulators must use federal divestiture orders backed by a bipartisan group of regional utility consumers and independent labs. Policymakers must stop subsidizing the private capture of public power grids and start treating artificial intelligence as a heavy industrial extraction project that answers to the law.
