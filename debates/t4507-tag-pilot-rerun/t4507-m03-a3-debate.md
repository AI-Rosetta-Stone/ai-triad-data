## Topic

Under what security conditions should the Department of Commerce restrict foundational AI model weight releases to balance proliferation risks against open innovation for domestic developers?

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Releasing foundational artificial intelligence model weights above set capability limits is a permanent choice that strips away human control. Once these files spread on sites like Hugging Face, no rule can force third parties to pull back fine-tuned models or stripped safety filters. The Department of Commerce must set up pre-market checks as the main gate instead of relying on later lawsuits. When fields lack clear feedback loops and have hidden scaling traits, fixing things after a disaster happens does not work at all.

Critics argue that forced pre-market checks hand regulatory capture to big firms and push work overseas past export controls. National security export controls under the Export Control Reform Act of 2018 already limit dual-use items because foreign leaks escape border checks. Controlling domestic release points stops the spread of unaligned base models that lack checked safety baselines. The Bureau of Industry and Security has the legal power to link export licenses and release rules to tested skill limits like automated cyber attacks or biological synthesis screening.

Agencies can spot and test these skill limits before launch by using standard tests from the Artificial Intelligence Safety Institute. The National Institute of Standards and Technology showed in its 2023 Secure Software Development Framework that clear safety baselines work without killing open-source work in safer fields. Local open-source builders keep full freedom to build below the set size and skill limits while big labs face pre-launch audits. Caution must grow to match permanent risks because complex human systems need firm limits to survive a major crash.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

History shows that new tech spreads at the speed of habits, not code releases.

The Safetyist claims that releasing open model weights past set limits is a final step that permanently ends human control. This point addresses real dual-use risks in biology and cyber fields. Yet it treats software like a physical weapon that fires itself.

Export rules based on the 1979 Export Administration Act fail because software moves through peer networks, not shipping crates. When the Bureau of Industry and Security limited crypto tools in the 1990s under the International Traffic in Arms Regulations, foreign rivals just built open-source tools. Local startups paid the legal costs while code kept spreading. Today, licensing model weights builds big walls for top firms while failing to stop shared code.

Policy funds should fix known harms like job loss and biased code, not wild fears. Census Bureau data shows only 5.4 percent of U.S. firms used AI in 2024. The true block is slow office habits, not weak models. Bureaucracies take up new tools slowly through purchase rules, worker talks, and legal blame.

Sector watchdogs like the Food and Drug Administration and the Federal Trade Commission already have the power to stop harms in their fields. Using those bodies to check AI stops the need for fragile new central offices that give big tech firms more power. True safety comes from shared watchdogs and strict blame for bad results, not from trying to lock up source code.
