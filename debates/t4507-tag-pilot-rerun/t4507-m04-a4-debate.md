## Topic

Under what conditions should antitrust authorities like the FTC mandate structural unbundling for vertically integrated AI firms to prevent infrastructure lock-in, and how does this affect independent developers versus enterprise consumers?

## Opening Statements

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Forcing vertically integrated artificial intelligence firms to split up risks cementing incumbent compliance moats instead of restoring open competition. Enterprise migrations across foundational model application programming interfaces and proprietary developer tools show high technical and learning costs. When the Federal Trade Commission or Department of Justice tries to force structural divestitures in general-purpose technologies, history shows a clear outcome. Small compliance departments fold, while dominant firms absorb legal fees and build regulatory tollbooths that block nimble rivals.

Critics argue that contractual exit rights stay toothless when foundational model capabilities diverge so sharply that switching vendors forces a corporate consumer to accept a worse product. Yet this assumes that breaking apart cloud divisions or chip supply chains creates interoperability by order, ignoring how past network industries actually integrated. The control plane integration paradox shows that vendor lock-in comes from proprietary orchestration layers and custom data pipelines, not simple hardware monopolies. Forcing a corporate breakup of a stack that customers willingly adopted for its unified workflow does not solve the underlying coordination friction. It only adds new transition costs and breaks the reliability guarantees that enterprise chief information officers paid millions to secure.

True market resilience in cloud infrastructure and specialized hardware supply chains comes from strict data portability rules and open interface standards, not judicial asset sales. Past digital antitrust actions, such as the European Union Digital Markets Act enforcement actions begun in 2024, show that strict behavioral rules often penalize open-source developers who lack dedicated legal teams. A core assumption of this unbundling push is that antitrust regulators have the technical agility to watch permanent operational separation without capturing the markets they police. Policymakers should reject blunt structural remedies and instead enforce strict contractual portability that lets enterprise consumers shift workloads across competing model providers in under thirty days.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Centralized compute concentration creates massive compliance barriers for independent oversight. When three technology conglomerates control ninety percent of frontier training clusters, public watchdogs cannot audit systems. These systems require billions of dollars in specialized semiconductor infrastructure.

This structural asymmetry starves independent developers of the compute resources necessary to check safety baselines before deployment occurs. We spent centuries building institutional aviation guardrails that separate manufacturers from safety inspectors. Artificial intelligence requires the exact same structural boundary today.

Mandatory structural unbundling risks cementing incumbent compliance moats if regulators fail to pair corporate divestiture with strict, independently verified pre-deployment safety gates. Critics correctly warn that breaking up cloud divisions without rigorous accountability merely fractures monopolies into decentralized hazard zones where unverified models grow without supervision.

Independent developers cannot bear the compliance overhead of arbitrary bureaucratic speedbumps. Unconstrained deployment of irreversible capabilities guarantees system breakdown. The enforcement mechanism requires the Federal Trade Commission and the Department of Justice to condition all market restructuring on binding deferred prosecution agreements with named corporate officers.

Human control over autonomous systems collapses permanently when commercial expansion outpaces verifiable institutional oversight and enforceable legal liability. Historical failures in financial markets and civil engineering prove that removing structural boundaries without hard stop-switches invites catastrophic failure.

The statutory authority already exists within Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act to prohibit unfair methods of competition that compromise public safety infrastructure. Lawmakers must reject the false choice between monopoly lock-in and chaotic deregulation by enforcing structural separation tied directly to non-negotiable safety verification baselines.
