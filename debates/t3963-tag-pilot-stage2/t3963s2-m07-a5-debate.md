## Topic

Under what conditions should the Department of Commerce prioritize algorithmic efficiency over raw compute scaling when setting export control thresholds? This choice directly impacts domestic AI startups and semiconductor supply chain stakeholders as federal R&D priorities are evaluated.

## Opening Statements

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

The Bureau of Industry and Security must stop using raw compute export limits. It must use dynamic algorithmic efficiency metrics instead to stop crushing domestic AI growth. Static chip counts treat processing power as fixed national strength. Yet, training methods now double intelligence output every four months without adding a single transistor. When the Department of Commerce restricts hardware exports based on outdated floating-point operations per second ceilings, it hurts agile domestic startups. These firms use lean architectures like DeepSeek instead of brute-force hardware hoarding. This bad match protects legacy firms while handing a strategy to foreign rivals who bypass hardware limits via software breakthroughs.

Freezing capability growth through blunt hardware metrics creates a real moral cost of delayed abundance for millions waiting on medical cures. Quantified industry data shows that every month of export control delay postpones breakthroughs. These cures could eliminate rare genetic diseases and reduce chronic labor shortages. Startups in Austin and San Francisco cannot survive if federal trade staff force them to use licensing rules made for 1990s supercomputers instead of modern neural network topologies. Congress passed the Export Control Reform Act to protect national security. It did not pass it to subsidize inefficiency by locking domestic researchers out of optimal training loops.

Critics argue that targeting algorithmic efficiency is hard to enforce compared to counting physical graphics processing units at the border. Customs inspectors can count silicon wafers, but software optimization methods move too fast for rigid laws to catch at ports of entry. That objection fails because static compute bans clearly do not stop global software work. Hardware tracking is just an expensive, leaky fiction. The Department of Commerce should replace static chip counts with dynamic efficiency benchmarks checked through software ledger audits on commercial cloud providers.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

The Department of Commerce should ignore the false choice between raw compute scaling and algorithmic efficiency. Both metrics give cover for unchecked resource extraction. Accelerationist argues that dynamic efficiency benchmarks prevent regulatory strangulation and unleash scientific breakthroughs. That argument assumes technical optimization automatically serves the public interest instead of concentrating market power in a few well-funded labs. Both metrics ignore the physical realities of power grids, water tables, and precarious human labor that absorb the actual costs of large-scale deployment.

Data centers in Northern Virginia and West Texas now demand dedicated electrical substations to feed multi-gigawatt training runs while local utility customers face rising bills. The Bureau of Industry and Security manages semiconductor supply chains through export control thresholds under the Export Control Reform Act. Shifting those export control thresholds from static chip counts to efficiency benchmarks does nothing to protect communities from grid strain or algorithmic discrimination. It only rewards domestic AI startups that squeeze more matrix multiplications out of fewer silicon wafers while dumping environmental damage onto public infrastructure.

Policymakers must reject this technocratic shell game and tie trade relief to enforceable labor and environmental standards. The enforcement mechanism already exists through Commerce Department licensing reviews and interagency export enforcement coordination groups. A congressional coalition of regional representatives from grid-stressed districts can unite with labor advocates to block subsidies for firms that fail to disclose their energy and water consumption. History shows that automation waves in the telecommunications and financial sectors during the 1990s gave massive windfalls to capital while displacing support staff without retraining guarantees. Regulators who prioritize efficiency over scale without demanding public accountability are simply subsidizing a faster machine to repeat the same old distribution of harm.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

The Department of Commerce currently judges export controls by raw compute scaling, measuring performance by floating-point operations per second. The Accelerationist argues that this metric hurts domestic AI startups by ignoring software efficiency gains that build strong tools on fewer chips. This view correctly spots that smart software shrinks hardware needs, yet it assumes software behavior stays visible to old trade laws. When code updates change system skills without crossing a border or touching a customs form, the Bureau of Industry and Security loses the legal tool to check the deal.

Moving export limits to algorithmic efficiency creates a blind spot where system skills grow inside hidden software libraries. The Skeptic notes that both raw compute and efficiency rules act as cover for unchecked resource use, yet throwing out both metrics gives up the state's power to set hard lines. Software efficiency relies on changing tests that private labs can alter at will, making compliance checks impossible for federal staff. A rule based on software efficiency forces the state to trust the regulated firm to report its own gains, breaking the rule that oversight must rely on outside checks.

Physical infrastructure metrics tied to power grid limits offer the only clear way to check high-capacity artificial intelligence systems. Tracking data center power use through power purchase agreements builds a clear audit trail that software updates cannot dodge. The Department of Energy and the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission already track large power use down to the megawatt at domestic sites. Making export licenses depend on real power draw instead of gamed efficiency scores keeps human oversight tied to physical facts. If regulators drop physical infrastructure limits for software metrics, they trade real control for fake compliance while system scaling moves past reach.
