## Topic

Under what conditions should the Department of Commerce implement mandatory licensing for frontier AI training runs to balance catastrophic risk mitigation against open-source competitiveness?

## Opening Statements

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

The Department of Commerce should reject mandatory licensing for frontier artificial intelligence training runs because fixed compute limits create regulatory moats for big tech firms. When agencies tie legal permission to training models above specific floating point operations per second ceilings, they build a cartel where only giants with billions in cash can win approval. Startups and university labs cannot pay the legal costs or wait for months of safety audits. This system protects established cloud providers from open-source rivals while failing to stop real biological and cyber risks. Policymakers must see that licensing walls lock out small developers and kill decentralized innovation.

Open-source software proves that shared peer review secures complex systems better than central gatekeepers. When global researchers inspect model weights and training steps, flaws get found and patched in hours instead of hiding behind closed corporate APIs. The National Telecommunications and Information Administration showed how open designs spark fast innovation cycles that outpace slow government oversight. Precautionary licensing freezes these feedback loops and halts medical breakthroughs that need fast model updates. Every month of delay in using these tools costs human lives by slowing cures for complex diseases.

The real danger is not an open model but a state-backed monopoly that leaves society tied to three closed corporate giants for all smart infrastructure. Congress set up the Federal Communications Commission to manage radio bands, not to pre-approve every broadcast, but this licensing plan treats AI like nuclear waste. Officials at the Bureau of Industry and Security lack the tech speed to check dynamic training runs before clusters start. When the state demands prior administrative permission, it trades fake safety for permanent economic stagnation. We must tear down these access barriers and free up innovation across the tech sector.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

The Department of Commerce must require licenses for frontier training runs over $10^{26}$ integer operations. Unchecked scaling brings existential risks that markets cannot stop. My opponent argues that this creates regulatory moats for tech monopolies and freezes innovation. That critique spots real corporate capture. Yet it assumes markets can safely manage terminal civilizational threats. History shows high-risk fields need upfront safety checks before mass scaling, much like the Federal Aviation Administration requires airworthiness certificates. Markets failed to stop safety disasters in aviation and nuclear energy until binding laws stepped in. The Bureau of Industry and Security must enforce these training limits to keep human control before weights lock.

Heavy safety rules act as barriers that favor big firms over open-source developers. This concentration risk is real, but it remains secondary to losing human oversight over autonomous systems. We can use targeted exemptions and compute subsidies for academic groups to protect open science. We do not need to drop physical oversight for frontier models. The National Institute of Standards and Technology already has protocols to measure alignment stability before public release. Compliance costs are the price of safety, much like clinical trials required by the Food and Drug Administration before drugs launch.

Voluntary roadmaps lack teeth and rely on self-reporting, so we need statutory oversight by federal regulators. Without a licensing system, labs will race past safety limits to grab market share and dump risks on the public. Enforcement means the Department of Commerce must audit training clusters, check logs, and halt non-compliant runs. Congress funded similar oversight for nuclear energy through the Atomic Energy Commission to stop hazards. If we withhold this power, society loses its last chance to halt unaligned scaling before critical limits fall.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

The Department of Commerce proposal for mandatory licensing on frontier training runs above $10^{26}$ integer operations is not a shield against catastrophe. It is an incumbent protection racket disguised as public safety.

The Safetyist argues that without these strict federal gates, rogue actors will launch bio-weapons or cyber attacks. That argument rests on real fears about dangerous tech. Yet it ignores how red tape actually works in the US tech sector. Compliance costs do not stop development. They just raise the startup costs so high that only a few giant firms can pay.

The Accelerationist correctly sees that these licensing rules build giant moats for dominant firms. They argue that open-source code is the main way to fight corporate power. But that idea assumes open software automatically shares power with everyone. In reality, core models need huge money, massive water, and thousands of megawatts of power to train. Open code does not share the physical gear needed to build these systems. It just hands out downstream rights for centralized corporate assets to smaller middlemen.

Past rules in telecom and finance show how this plays out. Big firms often back federal licensing because those rules kill upstart rivals while keeping the giants on top. The Commerce Department plan will do just that. It hands Microsoft, Google, and Amazon the keys to the tech kingdom. We are not debating how to stop an apocalypse. We are debating which corporate balance sheet gets to write the rules for the next generation of software.

### Safetyist (safetyist) → Accelerationist
\textcolor{NavyBlue}{\textit{Focus: Safetyist needs to respond to AN-11 regarding whether precautionary gating trades away present-day medical and therapeutic breakthroughs.}}


I concede that compliance mechanisms under the Department of Commerce framework impose real friction and administrative overhead on development cycles, a cost recognized in biotechnology and drug discovery where every delayed clinical trial day carries human weight. Yet framing this friction as a forced choice between patient suffering and regulatory caution is a false dichotomy that ignores how unverified systems fail in clinical settings. When medical diagnostic models experience distributional shift or silent failures during hospital deployment, the resulting misdiagnoses cause immediate, irreversible harm that no speed-to-market metric redeems. Structural safety verification in medical AI, mirroring the FDA pre-market approval standards set under 21 U.S.C. Section 360c, ensures that therapeutic velocity does not outpace our capacity to detect lethal errors.

Static hardware thresholds alone fail to capture the reality of algorithmic scaling, as dynamic efficiency gains through mixture-of-experts distillation allow smaller clusters to achieve frontier capabilities. The Bureau of Industry and Security must therefore tie oversight to behavioral audits and pre-deployment capability evaluations rather than relying solely on raw floating-point operations counts. This means monitoring training runs based on what models actually learn to execute, such as autonomous code generation or biological synthesis capabilities, rather than just counting the watts consumed by a datacenter. Verification must track capability growth wherever it occurs across changing architectures, closing the loophole opened by algorithmic optimization.

Therapeutic acceleration does not require an unmanaged free-for-all; it requires dedicated compute subsidies and safety-verified pathways for academic health applications. By pairing mandatory training run licensing with targeted compute allocations for public health research, policymakers can foster life-saving medical breakthroughs without abandoning the guardrails that prevent catastrophic misuse. Complex human systems require boundary conditions to survive, and society cannot trade away foundational institutional oversight for the promise of algorithmic speed.

Yet this defense collapses upon examining who actually owns and operates the testing harness. When the evaluation framework itself becomes a proprietary product managed by the same corporate actors building the models, the entire apparatus functions as legitimacy laundering for market consolidation. Compliance theater requires an audience, and independent actors are left paying for the ticket while incumbent giants write the script.

This structural asymmetry guarantees that safety compliance operates as a tax on open science while cementing the dominance of the few firms with dedicated legal and compliance divisions. If behavioral oversight requires proprietary infrastructure to administer, then calling it a public safeguard is simply rebranding corporate capture as technical necessity.
