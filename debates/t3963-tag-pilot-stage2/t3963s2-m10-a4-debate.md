## Topic

Under what structural conditions do FTC and DOJ antitrust oversight prevent frontier AI market concentration driven by proprietary compute access?

## Opening Statements

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Federal antitrust oversight fails when it protects incumbents instead of breaking up compute monopolies that slow human progress. Three major cloud companies control over sixty percent of enterprise infrastructure spending and bottleneck specialized chip supply chains. This central control forces labs into exclusive cloud contracts that lock out open-weight models and delay life-saving medical discoveries. When regulators stall market competition with endless reviews, they impose a direct human toll by holding back tools that could cure diseases and end labor shortages.

Decentralized open-source networks beat top-down corporate planning when speeding up advanced AI capabilities. The 2023 open-weight model explosion proved that open developer networks match closed-lab benchmarks at a fraction of the cost. Antitrust regulators must use structural breakup powers under Section 2 of the Sherman Act to force cloud providers to unbundle infrastructure access from proprietary model development. Breaking these vertical monopolies frees thousands of independent teams to solve complex protein folding and materials science problems without asking a corporate board for permission.

Critics argue that restricting cloud partnerships will disrupt scaling laws and stall critical training runs. That objection ignores the real fact that venture capital and sovereign wealth funds stand ready to deploy billions into neutral, unbundled compute facilities once exclusive contracts become illegal. The Department of Justice and the Federal Trade Commission have the legal power to mandate standards for cluster interconnects right now. Open innovation needs strict enforcement against compute hoarding so that abundance replaces corporate rent-seeking.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

The Federal Trade Commission and the Department of Justice have the legal power under Section 2 of the Sherman Act to stop compute monopolies. But breaking up companies alone makes civilizational risk worse. It leads to many unverified labs racing to build dangerous frontier AI. Accelerationist arguments are right that three giant cloud firms control over sixty percent of enterprise infrastructure spending. They bottleneck specialized chip supply chains, which makes market concentration a real structural hazard. Yet, breaking up these bottlenecks without safety rules just replaces one big hazard with messy chaos. Dozens of unaligned labs will bypass oversight entirely. When the Federal Communications Commission broke up AT&T in 1984, the breakup worked well. Phone infrastructure does not rewrite its own code or grow hazardous on its own.

Open-weight model sharing also proves that decentralized compute does not equal safe innovation. Open weights permanently cut off upstream control over digital files that cannot be recalled. In 2023, unaligned model weights spread across peer-to-peer networks. Once model files enter public sites, no agency can pull them back or force an emergency shutdown. Section 5 of the FTC Act lets regulators police unfair competition. But neither the FTC nor the DOJ can force model freezes or stop open-weight developers. Antitrust rules that lower entry barriers without safety audits just spread the power to launch systems that humans cannot stop.

To stop frontier AI monopolies, antitrust remedies must require enforceable safety rules. We must shift from open access to verified institutional accountability. If the DOJ forces cloud providers to unbundle infrastructure from proprietary models, the court order must demand real-time kill-switches. Government-certified monitors must audit any frontier model that uses more than ten to the twenty-sixth FLOPs of compute before training starts. Congress must give the FTC clear power to halt deployment when safety gaps get too wide. This links antitrust rules with human control over irreversible tech. We spent centuries building guardrails to keep society stable. Removing those boundaries for market competition invites civilizational collapse.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Frontier AI market power rests on physical capital and slow spread, not fast software. The Accelerationist argues that antitrust moves against hyperscalers just protect old players and slow vital progress. The Safetyist says the FTC and DOJ have enough power under Section 2 of the Sherman Act to stop compute monopolies. Both views assume AI breaks past rules for broad tech tools. Yet capital spending by Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, and Meta passed two hundred billion dollars in two thousand twenty-three alone. This spending anchors market power in physical data centers, not fast code.

Raw compute access builds industrial power because hardware takes huge physical resources, not just smart math. Cloud giants take compute windfalls through circular deals while pushing risk onto downstream app startups. When the FTC checks these cloud ties, it finds vertical integration that mirrors early twentieth-century phone trusts. Antitrust enforcement under Section 2 is slow, but smart conduct rules and open standards can break hardware bottlenecks without stopping research. Current agencies do not need a total reboot; they just need to use their power over digital infrastructure like past courts used on railroads.

Bureau of Labor Statistics data shows that firm use of machine learning tools stays below five percent across normal business sectors as of two thousand twenty-four. This slow rate kills the myth of instant societal change and gives antitrust regulators time to step in. Leaders must drop panic and use targeted fixes that stop infrastructure capture while saving open-weight model health. Building strong rules through smart enforcement beats trying to stop a tech that moves at the speed of office habits.
