## Topic

Under what compute thresholds should the Department of Commerce require frontier AI developers to obtain pre-training licenses, and how would this mechanism balance catastrophic risk mitigation against open-source innovation?

## Opening Statements

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Mandating pre-training licenses based on compute limits is an industry protection racket. Incumbent firms designed it to criminalize open-source competition. When the Department of Commerce sets barriers around training runs crossing ten to the twenty-sixth integer operations, tech monopolies cheer. They already own the data centers. Smaller labs and university groups cannot pay the compliance costs or legal fees to get federal permission. That is not risk reduction. It is a corporate moat built by federal decree.

Incumbent lobbyists push the apocalypse story to lock in a market monopoly. They ignore real, current harms. Workers in Kenya scrape training data for two dollars an hour while executives warn Congress about rogue superintelligence. Energy grids in Northern Virginia strain under the actual power load of data centers running today. Regulators focus on sci-fi weapons. This just hands market control to the three firms that sell advanced graphics processing units. The Department of Commerce should enforce labor standards and track data origins instead of licensing fake threats.

Critics argue that without strict pre-training licenses, foreign rivals or bad actors will release unvetted models that enable bio-attacks. That claim assumes frontier compute is easy to hide from satellites. Training clusters need thousands of special chips, steady power, and millions of gallons of cooling water. They cannot fit in a basement. The National AI Safety Institute already tracks supply chains via chip export controls and cloud rules. Adding a pre-training license regime does not stop bad actors who break laws by choice. It only stops garage developers and open-source researchers who work in plain sight.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Requiring Department of Commerce pre-training licenses for compute clusters over ten to the twenty-sixth integer operations creates a bottleneck that protects legacy monopolies and blocks life-saving tech. The Skeptic rightly points out that statutory licenses act as a shield for insiders designed to crush open-source rivals with high compliance costs. Commerce regulators enforce these barriers through strict reporting rules, freezing out small developers who lack the cash to fight federal red tape. This top-down gating trades guessed-at risks for the proven human suffering of delayed tech progress. When bureaucrats freeze model training, patients die from diseases that automated medical tools could cure.

Past rules in the phone and airline industries show that entry gates always shield big players while killing fast, spread-out competition. The Federal Communications Commission spent decades restricting radio spectrum access to stop chaos, which delayed cell phones by a generation and made monopolies rich. A compute limit of ten to the twenty-sixth integer operations works just like old FCC licenses, handing the whole AI world to three or four big firms in Silicon Valley. Open-weight models spread out power by letting thousands of labs check, fork, and fix base models without asking a federal board. Centralized safety reviews fall behind decentralized trial-and-error because a single state review board is a single point of failure prone to political capture.

This delay costs human lives in unbuilt cures, material shortages, and millions of deaths that faster math could have stopped. Automated molecular tests run on giant compute clusters to design targeted drugs for drug-resistant bugs in days instead of decades. Every month federal boards waste on pre-training permits is a month where human life spans stall and preventable pain goes on. Critics fear bad actors might weaponize open-source frontier models before real-time monitors catch the exploit. Open ecosystems build safety tools faster than bad actors can exploit base models because open networks use millions of coders to fix flaws at once. Who gave the Department of Commerce the moral right to let people die today of diseases that open science could fix tomorrow?

### Safetyist (safetyist)

The Skeptic and the Accelerationist argue that pre-training licenses act as a shield that locks out open-source developers and hands power to big tech firms.

That critique holds some truth, because compliance costs hurt small groups while helping big firms protect their market share.

The real error is thinking market competition can replace upfront checks when failure could cause deep civilizational harm.

We spent centuries building guardrails to keep society stable.

On what authority do we claim we can drop them without inviting collapse?

Rules must anchor directly to physical infrastructure, specifically electricity use and power deals for data centers above 10 to the 26th FLOPs.

The Department of Commerce can track huge power draws and chip imports with high accuracy.

This avoids the vague codebases that software developers call impossible to audit.

The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission already tracks grid stability and power loads under the Federal Power Act.

Physical data gives us a clear baseline to stop the use of unvetted frontier models before training even starts.

Rules must scale to fit real powers, much like the Federal Aviation Administration requires deep upfront checks before planes fly.

Smaller open-source labs running below the limit face no pre-training license rules.

Labs that cross the compute ceiling must pass independent audits run by the National AI Safety Institute.

The state enforces this through mandatory hardware reports from chip makers and cloud firms under Title III of the Defense Production Act.

Society cannot rely on market self-regulation when the product carries risks we cannot fix.

The resulting framework protects dominant market positions under the banner of safety while shifting the actual cost onto decentralized researchers who cannot afford the legal toll.
