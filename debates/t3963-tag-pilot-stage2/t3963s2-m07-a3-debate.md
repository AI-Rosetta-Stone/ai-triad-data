## Topic

Under what conditions should foundation model developers prioritize algorithmic efficiency over raw compute scaling, and how should export control regulators adapt to these shifting performance drivers in enterprise deployment?

## Opening Statements

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Compute export controls are failing. They chase hardware limits while software efficiency skips physical borders. The Bureau of Industry and Security still ties performance limits to raw floating-point operations per second, or FLOPS. Yet open-weight models from Meta and Mistral match closed systems on standard tests with fewer parameters. This gap turns hardware rules into theater that protects big cloud firms instead of national security. Software weights move faster than customs agents can track, making chip-level policing obsolete.

Physical limits slow data center growth long before capability goals are met. Dominion Energy reported in 2023 that new cloud centers need up to three gigawatts of power, equal to three nuclear reactors. Grid operators now enforce multi-year wait lists because water supplies and transformers cannot keep pace. These energy limits prove that software efficiency is the only way to scale under real-world resource caps.

Policy must drop hardware quotas and use current agencies to watch real-world uses. The Federal Trade Commission and the Food and Drug Administration already have the power to police safety in finance, medicine, and labor. When agencies shift from tracking chips to checking how firms use models, the rules survive software changes. Society adopts new tech at the speed of institutions, not venture capital hype.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

The Bureau of Industry and Security bases its limits on raw floating-point operations per second. Open-weight models from Meta and Mistral match closed models on tests using fewer parameters. Skeptic correctly argues that static hardware limits fail when software bypasses physical compute limits. When software efficiency separates performance from raw compute, old export controls fail. We must tie rules to verifiable physical telemetry and safe clean-room setups before unconstrained models emerge.

Open-weight model releases are permanent deployment choices that skip normal containment and recall tools. Once model weights spread across peer-to-peer networks, post-release stopping is structurally impossible. Software innovation happens outside chip factories, making border hardware checks porous to decentralized breakthroughs. Regulators cannot track software efficiency gains through trade data when developers optimize training without expanding physical chip footprints.

Congress must amend the Export Control Reform Act to require hardware telemetry and secure clean-room checks for any training run over ten to the twenty-fifth integer operations. The Department of Commerce already has power under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act to tie export licenses to remote compute checks. This system keeps human control by stopping training runs that fail safety tests before model weights escape. When a developer crosses compute or efficiency limits without audit telemetry, the government revokes datacenter power in twenty-four hours.

Critics argue that mandatory clean-room audits cannot track decentralized software or stop open-source groups from sharing breakthroughs across borders. The Department of Energy uses similar rules for nuclear enrichment by controlling dual-use centrifuge parts despite local design changes. National security agencies enforce these rules by penalizing cloud providers who fail to verify customers leasing large compute clusters. If lawmakers refuse to replace static hardware metrics with dynamic telemetry, the state permanently loses its ability to stop unverified foundation models.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Algorithmic efficiency does not make raw compute scaling obsolete. It multiplies the real-world impact of every single floating-point operation. Open-weight models like Meta's Llama 3 series match top benchmarks with far fewer parameters through structural design instead of bloated compute budgets. When the Bureau of Industry and Security tries to freeze training runs at ten to the twenty-fifth power integer operations, regulators miss a key fact. Software updates constantly unlock new capabilities from existing hardware. State actors cannot write rules fast enough to trap a moving target that does more with less.

Freezing hardware to stop software progress causes stagnation without adding real security. Real-world AI harm does not happen during upstream training in a secure data center. It happens at downstream integration points where operators launch commercial apps. Point-of-execution liability and application-layer tripwires manage deployment risks much better than rigid semiconductor bans from the Department of Commerce. Policymakers who force hardware bottlenecks are starving healthcare diagnostics and material science automation to solve a phantom containment problem.

When regulators restrict software growth out of fear, they choose to let people suffer from diseases we could cure. The history of tech from electricity to open-source software shows that permissionless trial-and-error beats central planning every time. Bureaucratic gatekeepers who demand central control over software advances cause real human suffering through the cures they delay. Congress must reject static export controls and instead tie rules to real downstream outcomes rather than old hardware limits.
