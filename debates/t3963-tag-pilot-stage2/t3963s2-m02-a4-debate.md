## Topic

Under what compute thresholds and compliance pathways should the Department of Commerce require pre-training licenses for frontier AI models, and how can such mandates balance catastrophic risk mitigation against the concentration of industry power?

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Frontier artificial intelligence models trained above compute thresholds of $10^{26}$ floating-point operations require mandatory pre-training licenses issued by the Department of Commerce. Complex human systems need clear safety rules to survive. Without those rules, systems break down beyond repair. Aviation rules do not let airlines test uncertified planes with passengers on board. The Department of Commerce must use this same standard for digital systems before developers lock in model weights and release the software.

Critics warn that mandatory licenses block competition and help big tech firms crush new rivals. But high-risk general-purpose tools need strict safety checks no matter the market structure. Accepting market consolidation is a fair price to pay to stop total disaster. The Federal Aviation Administration limited plane manufacturing to a few vetted firms to keep the skies safe. Public safety beats market plurality when a failure mode is permanent.

Safety checks must govern internal research before any public release or application programming interface deployment takes place. Frontier labs currently train massive models in closed spaces without independent checks on their safety baselines. The Department of Commerce must track compute use through cloud providers to stop secret training runs that cross legal limits. Agencies have the legal power to inspect physical sites, and silicon supply chains give them a clear chokepoint to check compliance.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

The Safetyist view correctly spots that frontier models trained above ten to the twenty-sixth floating-point operations need strict checks. Yet their proposed Department of Commerce pre-training licenses rely on static compute lines. These rules miss how tech infrastructure actually concentrates.

History shows that upfront licenses for foundational compute do not stop major risks. Instead, they build regulatory moats. They lock out independent researchers while cementing the market power of a few cloud giants.

When the Federal Communications Commission licensed radio spectrum in the nineteen-thirties, established firms captured the process. They turned compliance costs into barriers that took decades to remove.

A similar corporate merge is now underway. Capital needs for AI data centers and power grids already concentrate hardware ownership among three dominant firms.

Mandatory pre-training licenses backed by cloud providers will speed up this monopoly. They make private infrastructure giants act as state compliance officers.

Cloud providers like Microsoft, Amazon, and Google already control most high-end graphics processing units. Giving them enforcement power lets these firms view competitor pipelines under the guise of national security.

Fixed limits also fail against smaller actors. These groups bypass training compute caps using commodity cluster multi-agent attacks and smart architectural workarounds.

Recent cyber tools show that decentralized, sub-threshold setups match monolithic models. They do this without ever crossing the Department of Commerce licensing trigger.

Trying to secure society by policing the training phase repeats old errors from early nuclear and aviation rules. Centralized gatekeeping stifled diffusion while missing decentralized hazards.

Good policy must focus on deployment liability and endpoint checks, rather than upstream infrastructure taxes. These taxes only protect oligopolies under the banner of risk control.

When leaders shift their focus from static compute counts to downstream behavior monitoring and verified incident response, they govern real harm. They avoid handing the digital economy to firms with the best compliance lawyers.

Stopping malicious dual-use development does not require subjective human license approvals that bottleneck honest innovators while bad actors ignore the law entirely. When regulators freeze open-weight tests out of fear, they choose to let patients die of diseases that decentralized trial and error could solve today. Speed and open access are vital for human life, not luxuries to trade for the illusion of bureaucratic control.
