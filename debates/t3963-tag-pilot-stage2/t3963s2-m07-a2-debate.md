## Topic

Under what conditions should the Department of Commerce regulate algorithmic efficiency gains rather than compute scaling thresholds, as frontier AI labs and semiconductor manufacturers navigate upcoming export control reviews?

## Opening Statements

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Shifting export control rules from physical chip limits to software efficiency gains is a bad idea. It creates a moving target and protects old monopolies. The Bureau of Industry and Security now bases trade policy on hard hardware limits, like floating-point operations per second. Hardware controls work because chip factories need large physical supply chains that the government can inspect. Expanding export bans into math and software turns trade rules into thought police work. This hurts open-source code and locks out small rivals.

Critics claim that physical chip limits will fail because smart software tricks bypass raw chip limits. New designs, like mixture-of-experts routing, prove that software gains outpace raw hardware growth. But trading hardware rules for efficiency goals assumes regulators can track and freeze intangible math in real time. That idea fails because software development is decentralized. Thousands of global engineers ship daily code updates across borders. The Department of Commerce lacks the staff to audit millions of code bases without halting the domestic tech sector.

Audit costs already hurt small startups that lack big legal teams to read vague federal rules. When the state ties export licenses to software efficiency instead of physical silicon, big labs absorb the cost while startups drown in red tape. This makes national security worse by handing power to a few giant firms that can afford the rules. Policymakers must reject software policing and keep export rules tied to real, physical chip manufacturing.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

The Accelerationist argues that algorithm gains make hardware tracking obsolete because clever math cuts training needs faster than export controls move. That point notes the speed of software tuning, but it treats math as a substitute for hard physical limits. Dropping physical rules turns trade checks into a hunt for hidden code across unmonitored servers. We spent centuries building guardrails to keep society stable. By what right do you say we can drop them without inviting collapse?

The Bureau of Industry and Security tracks power use and power deals to check high-performance data centers. Sites over 100 megawatts are a physical fact that hidden code or scattered training runs cannot mask. When the Department of Commerce checks these large power nodes, inspectors verify real chips using metered utility data instead of trusting software efficiency claims. Trading this hard physical anchor for guess-based software checks would strip regulators of their only reliable tool.

Complex human systems need limits to survive. Dropping those limits causes collapse, much like the failure of unmonitored financial derivatives in 2008. The enforcement tool rests in the Export Control Reform Act of 2018, which lets the Secretary of Commerce restrict items tied to national security based on physical presence. That law makes corporate officers sign binding compliance forms under felony threat for false claims to federal auditors. No corporate press release or safety roadmap replaces prison time for breaking hardware export limits.

Keeping compute scaling limits does not fix every flaw, but it keeps the power to stop unapproved growth before it fails. The political coalition backing this physical baseline includes security agencies, chip makers with local plants, and bipartisan oversight panels demanding proof. Frontier AI labs claim this rule slows progress, but speed is not a virtue when failure means losing human control. The Department of Commerce must keep hardware compute metrics as the base for trade rules until a truly verifiable physical substitute exists.
