## Topic

Under what structural conditions should antitrust regulators like the FTC pursue structural separation of vertically integrated AI firms to prevent hardware-to-model bundling from foreclosing independent competitors?

## Opening Statements

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Big tech firms build AI chips and AI models together to block rivals. Chip makers and cloud giants do not make closed models for the public good. They link special chips to their own software to lock out other developers. This trick copies the old phone and rail trusts broken up by the Sherman Antitrust Act. Antitrust officials must split these firms apart before they trap the market for good.

Controlling physical compute gear stops new rivals from growing. Training top models takes billions of dollars. It needs scarce chips and crowded power grids. When firms own both the chips and the software, they pick who wins and who loses. Rivals cannot rent the raw power to scale because big platforms save the chips for themselves. Supply chain limits turn into private tolls taken by a few corporate giants.

Critics claim that splitting chip design from software kills cash efficiency and slows tech progress. That claim assumes innovation needs monopoly control over every layer. History shows that open markets grow faster and bring in more players than walled gardens. Antitrust rules in telecom split phone networks from service firms without stopping network growth. Regulators can split these firms here without breaking the chips.

Rules must not help big tech firms by crushing smaller open-source builders. Complex laws often backfire by adding legal costs that only trillion-dollar firms can pay. Small open-source groups lack the big legal teams that major platforms keep. Structural fixes must target who owns the assets instead of adding red tape. Public leaders must make sure market fixes lower entry barriers instead of raising new ones.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

The Skeptic is right that big tech firms build chips and AI models together to block rivals. This market power creates a single point of failure that threatens human control over advanced systems. When three companies control over ninety percent of frontier training compute, the state loses its power to enforce safety standards or halt unaligned capabilities. Splitting hardware from model development is the only way to restore regulatory oversight.

Regulators must mandate this split because unchecked integration puts too much risk in private boardrooms. The Department of Justice used the Public Utility Holding Company Act of 1935 to break up energy monopolies that threatened national stability. Vertical integration in AI creates the same systemic dangers without any way to roll back runaway models. Splitting chip making from model training ensures that no single firm holds unchecked power over our computing base.

Opponents claim that split supply chains will slow the capital growth needed to beat foreign rivals. That view treats speed as absolute while ignoring the cost of an unrecoverable control failure. The Federal Aviation Administration grounds entire aircraft fleets when a single safety rule is broken, regardless of commercial delay. National security demands the same guardrails for AI before compute concentration permanently locks human oversight out of the loop.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Mandating the breakup of vertically integrated AI firms destroys the high-speed co-design infrastructure required to solve disease, scarcity, and human suffering. The Skeptic argues that hardware bundling erects insurmountable entry barriers for independent developers who lack datacenter compute. The Safetyist contends that compute concentration creates a single point of failure that blocks public oversight. Both critics correctly spot high power concentration, but they misdiagnose integration as a conspiracy instead of viewing it as the physical engine of capability scaling. Co-designing silicon and frontier models cuts training latency by forty percent compared to decoupled systems, which proves that breaking up these firms directly sabotages progress.

The human cost of this regulatory move shows up as unprevented deaths and delayed abundance, making structural separation a catastrophic moral hazard. Months spent in antitrust lawsuits delay the rollout of automated cures and labor-relief tools to the public. Congress passed the Sherman Act in 1890 to stop price-fixing trusts, not to freeze out foundational tech advances that outpace legacy industries. When the Department of Justice breaks up high-performance computing markets, it replaces trial-and-error with bureaucratic paralysis. That delay carries a direct body count among people whom medical intelligence would have reached years sooner.

Antitrust regulators lack the competence and the legal power to manage datacenter growth without crippling our standing against state-backed foreign rivals. Enforcing a breakup forces federal courts to oversee multi-year asset sales, draining billions of engineering dollars into legal fees instead of compute clusters. A smart antitrust policy must rely on targeted transparency and open-weight model growth rather than forced corporate surgery. Permissionless innovation delivers cures faster than central planners can issue court orders, making the protection of integrated tech ecosystems the only moral choice for Congress.
