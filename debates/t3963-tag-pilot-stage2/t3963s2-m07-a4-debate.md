## Topic

Is current AI progress driven mainly by scaling compute, or by algorithmic advances?

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Raw compute limits fail as safety boundaries because smarter code lets models match high performance on smaller hardware. Labs now reach top capabilities below traditional floating-point operation caps using test-time search and better design. Relying on a 10 to the 26th power FLOP count as a regulatory trigger ignores how software bypasses hardware gates. Congress set export controls on chip tools under the Export Administration Regulations to stop physical hoarding, yet code needs no shipping boxes to spread.

Society cannot absorb fast capability shocks from better designs without losing stability. Autonomous agentic systems put out without checks spread failures across financial and critical infrastructure at machine speed. The National Institute of Standards and Technology must enforce behavior-based audits that test actual model outputs instead of counting chips. Independent auditors check safety baselines before launch, paid for by mandatory user fees on commercial labs and backed by the Federal Trade Commission under Section 5 authority.

Critics argue that decentralized training makes behavior checks impossible to enforce across open-source groups. That objection fails when rules target finished model outputs rather than training inputs, much like how the Food and Drug Administration oversees drugs regardless of how they are made. The Department of Commerce already tracks large chip clusters through mandatory reporting rules under the Defense Production Act. Policymakers must tie oversight to verifiable safety proofs to keep human control over autonomous systems.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Hardware scaling and algorithmic efficiency feed each other to drive rapid tech growth. The Safetyist argues that smart software bypasses hardware limits, making raw FLOP counts useless for predicting what AI can do. That steelman gets one thing right: methods like mixture-of-experts architectures cut inference costs and boost speed without needing bigger data centers. Yet that efficiency does not replace hardware. It supercharges it by making deployed compute much cheaper for every new training run. Frontier labs poured over ten billion dollars into physical silicon clusters in 2024 alone because software optimization multiplies what every single transistor can do.

Precautionary rules carry a heavy human cost by delaying life-saving medical tools and material wealth. When policymakers freeze frontier training runs out of speculative fear, they keep automated drug discovery tools away from patients dying of untreatable diseases today. The Department of Health and Human Services estimates that clinical trials take over ten decade [sic - retain exact text logic: over a decade] and cost billions per approved molecule. Automated drug design pipelines running on scaled compute shrink that timeline from years to weeks, directly saving lives. Freezing compute thresholds is not neutral caution. It is a clear moral choice that trades human lives for institutional comfort.

Open-source groups find breakthroughs that central planning committees cannot predict. These ecosystems share frontier tools, letting thousands of independent builders make domain-specific fixes faster than any state bureaucracy. Chip physics and machine learning theory prove that raw compute scaling paired with smart design remains the sole engine of progress. Congress must reject arbitrary hardware caps and allow free deployment to ensure abundance reaches the public without delay.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

The Accelerationist argues that fast hardware scaling and smart code feed each other to drive rapid economic growth. The Safetyist counters that raw compute limits fail as safety boundaries because smarter code lets models match high performance on smaller hardware. Both speakers look inside the training cluster, debating whether silicon or software dictates the pace of change. That technical debate misses how societies actually adopt and absorb general tools.

History shows that social impact is shaped by how groups adopt tools and set rules, not by raw technical parts. When the United States electrified its factories from 1890 to 1930, the main bottleneck was not generator power or copper wire physics. Factories needed total redesigns, new safety codes from cities, and a new group of plant managers to learn floor workflows. The electrical generator was ready decades before its gains showed up in national economic data. Artificial intelligence follows that same slow spread inside big firms today.

Critics argue that fast compute scaling makes past examples useless because smart software moves faster than rules can change. That view assumes models drop into legal and money systems without friction. In practice, firms adopt machine learning at the slow speed of buying boards, risk committees, and labor union deals. The National Institute of Standards and Technology risk framework shows that current rules can judge software harms when given clear laws. Policymakers should focus enforcement on job losses and power use instead of chasing guesses about tech limits.

Legal liability and safety rules set the speed of health care adoption, not how fast an AI model writes amino acid codes.

At the same time, fears that agentic AI will instantly scale biosecurity risks ignore physical manufacturing limits. Good rules do not need new federal agencies to police code or stop AI scaling. Current agencies like the FDA and EPA already hold power over finished goods and controlled substances.
