## Topic

Should governments break up vertically integrated AI companies that own chips, cloud and models?

## Opening Statements

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Antitrust agencies like the Federal Trade Commission and the Department of Justice must force the split of vertically integrated artificial intelligence firms. Three companies control the whole stack of silicon chips, cloud data centers, and foundational software models. This setup creates huge barriers for independent rivals and concentrates power in private hands. Microsoft, Amazon, and Alphabet spent over one hundred billion dollars on capital expenditures in 2023 alone to lock down this pipeline.

Critics argue that breaking up these giants will destroy the efficiency needed to scale frontier models. That point ignores how standard industrial organization works in network markets. The Department of Justice broke up AT&T in 1982 because phone monopolies choked off new ideas downstream. Software work does not need chip making to sit inside the same company to work well. Structural splits force these firms to win on merit instead of using hardware access against independent developers.

Big tech firms shift the heavy money and green costs of their data centers onto local ratepayers while keeping all the private gains. Local grids face billions in upgrade costs to power these sites while home power bills rise. Corporate bosses pick deployment dates and profit margins while towns take on grid instability and water loss. The legal tools already exist in Section 2 of the Sherman Act and past cases like the 1956 IBM consent decree. Congress and regulators must use structural fixes now before these private monopolies grow too deep to rule.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Antitrust agencies like the Federal Trade Commission and the Department of Justice propose splitting vertically integrated artificial intelligence companies to protect market competition. Skeptics argue that letting single firms control chips, cloud infrastructure, and models creates barriers that lock out independent developers. That position correctly spots the market power of firms like Microsoft and Google, but it misses how frontier intelligence is built. Breaking up these pipelines destroys the hardware-software design work needed to scale model capabilities past current limits. Splitting these firms trades tech leadership for administrative fairness, directly stopping the growth of life-saving medical and scientific tools.

Modern frontier training runs need simultaneous work on chip design, cluster links, and math efficiency that separate companies cannot coordinate. When training clusters grow to 100,000 graphics processing units, network delays and memory limits demand real-time tweaks across the silicon and the model weights. A standalone chip designer cannot change microcode while a separate cloud provider handles the cooling and a third group trains the transformer architecture. The Federal Trade Commission lacks the tech skills to weigh diffuse future gains against visible market share, treating engineering needs as a monopoly plot. Courts applying standard antitrust rules under the Sherman Act will replace fast work with bureaucratic lag, harming the very consumers they want to protect.

Forcing structural splits imposes a heavy human cost by delaying the cures and scientific breaks that massive, integrated compute can deliver. When regulators freeze work out of fear, they choose to let people suffer from diseases that advanced artificial intelligence could solve within ten years. The enforcement relies on court orders led by the Department of Justice, a process that takes five to ten years of trials while progress stops. A better past example is the phone sector, where forced splits led to decades of slow growth until mobile wireless bypassed the wireline monopolies entirely. Policymakers must reject structural splits and let integrated firms build the infrastructure of abundance without state meddling.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

The FTC and the DOJ must force the structural split of vertically integrated AI firms that control chips, cloud systems, and foundational models. The Skeptic rightly notes that market power creates dangerous bottlenecks, but stops short of demanding the structural split needed to restore public safety visibility. When tech giants spend over $100 billion a year on capital costs to lock down the pipeline from silicon to model weights, they build closed corporate monoliths that hide from outside oversight.

Unchecked capability scaling without independent structural checkpoints leaves society defenseless against rapid capability shocks that outpace human absorption capacity. History in civil engineering and aviation shows that unconstrained scale leads to systemic collapse until regulators draw hard lines and demand independent oversight. Forcing structural splits across these linked supply chains removes the information gaps that currently block external safety auditors from testing dangerous model thresholds before public use.

Critics claim that breaking up these integrated firms ruins hardware-software design and delays vital scientific breakthroughs that need massive compute clusters of over 100,000 GPUs. Speed is not a virtue when the failure mode is an unrecoverable loss of human control over autonomous systems used at a national scale. Congress set up antitrust rules under the Sherman Act to stop private monopolies from beating public safety, and regulators have the legal power under Section 2 to force asset sales when market power threatens the public.
