## Topic

Under what conditions do cloud-compute bottlenecks drive market concentration rather than competition as the FTC evaluates exclusive AI infrastructure partnerships?

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Exclusive AI infrastructure deals pack top-tier training power into a few giants like Microsoft, Amazon, and Google. These firms use power deals and hardware lock-ins to shut out small developers. The FTC and DOJ judge these deals only by price and market access. That standard fails because compute choke points are not simple antitrust problems that market splits can fix. Physical limits on chips offer the only real way to keep human control over runaway systems.

Breaking up cloud deals without strict rules destroys our best safety tool. Decentralized networks and offshore hosts already bypass single-cloud checks. If antitrust regulators force cloud giants to sell specialized chips to anyone, safety vetting vanishes fast. Raw market competition in advanced AI speeds up the launch of unsafe models before safety checks work. Speed wins when dozens of rivals rush to scale unmonitored neural networks.

Critics argue that attacking exclusive cloud deals helps big tech monopolies by stopping smaller rivals from pooling compute power. That point correctly spots market power, but it misses the urgent need for real control. We must stop chasing open markets and instead tie cloud access to strict safety audits run by federal regulators. Congress set a clear precedent in the Atomic Energy Act of 1954, where special nuclear materials stayed tightly controlled despite commercial demand. Society cannot risk an unchecked race to deploy unsafe AI just to follow old antitrust rules.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Exclusive cloud compute partnerships do not help market competition. Big tech firms use capital power to lock out independent developers. Microsoft, Amazon, and Google control over 65 percent of the global cloud market, per antitrust filings. These firms run circular funding deals where venture capital flows back into their own cloud services. Independent startups cannot match the billions needed to buy scarce high-performance graphics chips. Antitrust agencies must treat these infrastructure gatekeepers as regulated utilities instead of simple service providers.

High-density data centers cause hidden costs by shifting grid upgrade bills onto everyday ratepayers. Regional power groups report that machine learning clusters cause local demand spikes that raise retail power prices. Municipal water boards near major servers note millions of gallons used daily for cooling in peak summer months. Local utility boards pay these upgrade costs while private cloud operators keep all the profit. Policymakers can protect ratepayers and split up services using current Federal Energy Regulatory Commission powers.

Supporters defend exclusive infrastructure deals as needed capital pools to fund huge compute clusters that smaller firms cannot afford. That view correctly notes the huge costs of modern model training but gets the cause wrong. The shortage of high-performance chips comes from exclusive supply contracts, not fixed physical limits. Regulators should tie cloud infrastructure approvals to open-access wholesale tiers that stop tech giants from hoarding compute power.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Exclusive cloud-compute partnerships between big tech and frontier labs are not anticompetitive gatekeeping. They are structural engines needed to grow AI capability and lift humanity out of scarcity. Critics argue these deals concentrate training compute among firms like Microsoft and Google, mirroring the Atomic Energy Act. Skeptics add that big tech uses capital power to lock out independent developers and shift grid upgrade costs onto ratepayers. Those concerns point to real market frictions, but they treat physical limits as permanent walls instead of engineering challenges to clear with private capital. Training a frontier model requires cluster sizes over 100,000 accelerators. This physical reality demands billions in upfront cash and gigawatt-scale power. When antitrust regulators block these partnerships under Section 2 of the Sherman Act, they do not help competition. They freeze development and lock in human suffering.

The FTC and DOJ use static market metrics that ignore the physical needs of frontier compute scaling. Building a gigawatt data center needs dedicated energy deals, liquid cooling, and supply chains that only cloud providers can execute fast. Blocking these partnerships through merger enforcement starves the ecosystem of the physical scale needed to cure disease and stop manual labor. The regulatory coalition relies on the idea that open-source startups can build grid infrastructure without concentrated money. That assumption fails against semiconductor economics, where a single chip plant costs 20 billion dollars and training runs need power equal to mid-sized cities. The enforcement mechanism here is federal antitrust litigation under the Clayton Act, driven by bureaucrats who treat market concentration as a harm regardless of output gains.

Energy infrastructure must outpace regulatory friction to stop a permanent halt in human progress. Private capital is already funding nuclear and geothermal power to bypass slow public utility commissions. This proves that commercial incentives match massive power build-outs. When the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission delays power lines for data centers, the human cost is delayed medical care and slow economic growth. Bipartisan lawmakers support rapid infrastructure build-out because compute power drives national strength and survival. If antitrust enforcement breaks up these partnerships, startups will not thrive in a decentralized utopia. They will starve for power while foreign rivals take the lead in AI. Who gave regulators the right to trade human recovery from disease for abstract market theories?

### Skeptic (skeptic) → Accelerationist
\textcolor{NavyBlue}{\textit{Focus: Address AN-5 (unpriced residential ratepayer externalities from high-density data centers) and explain how private capital deployment handles regional grid upgrade costs without shifting them onto local utility boards.}}


Private capital deployment in gigawatt-scale data centers does not fully internalize its infrastructural footprint, despite claims that private actors manage energy procurement efficiently. When a hyperscaler builds a facility requiring ≥500 megawatts of continuous power, the physical connection to the regional transmission grid triggers mandatory system impact studies managed by regional transmission organizations like PJM Interconnection. These studies routinely identify transmission line overloads and voltage drops that require billions in localized capacity upgrades. Investor-owned utilities frequently roll these capital expenditures directly into the rate base, shifting up to 80% of transmission upgrade costs onto residential utility ratepayers rather than billing the commercial data facility directly.

The argument that public utility oversight transforms dynamic markets into stagnant bureaucracies ignores how deregulation forces municipal boards to absorb unpriced grid liabilities. Regional transmission organizations process interconnection queues by prioritizing large load requests, but the actual cost-allocation rules approved by federal regulators allow commercial entities to defer or socialize their infrastructure obligations. For example, recent Federal Energy Regulatory Commission filings in the PJM market reveal that residential households face monthly transmission rate spikes of 15% to 30% to fund grid enhancements demanded by new high-density compute installations. Private capital finances the server racks and proprietary processors inside the building, but the high-voltage transmission lines, substation transformers, and backup generation reserves rely on public ratepayer backstops.

When regional grid reliability falters under peak summer cooling loads and continuous data center baseline demand, who answers for the resulting capacity price spikes? The cloud provider does not pay penalties when local distribution utilities implement rolling brownouts or raise residential tariffs to finance emergency grid stabilization. The accelerationist model of frictionless infrastructure deployment is simply a cost-shifting mechanism that privatizes compute revenues while socializing the heavy electrical burdens onto captive ratepayers. Demanding that commercial data facilities face strict common-carrier interconnection oversight and mandatory cost-allocation firewalls is not bureaucratic stagnation, but basic financial accountability for industrial-scale resource extraction.
