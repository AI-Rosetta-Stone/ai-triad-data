## Topic

Under what safety and liability conditions should the Department of Commerce permit frontier AI labs to release model weights publicly, balancing dual-use proliferation risks against open-source developer innovation?

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

The Department of Commerce must ban the public release of frontier model weights. Once people share these files on peer-to-peer networks, we cannot pull them back. This mirrors the permanent spread seen in biotech. We spent centuries building guardrails to keep aviation and finance stable. On what authority do you claim we can remove those boundaries from artificial intelligence without inviting collapse?

Critics argue that domestic bans are symbolic because foreign or illicit actors will share open models anyway. That objection ignores the actual locus of frontier training power, which stays inside a small group of domestic labs under federal rule. The Bureau of Industry and Security already enforces export controls under the Export Control Reform Act of 2018. The Department of Commerce can use those existing rules for weight releases just like it regulates dual-use chip gear.

True open-source developer innovation needs stable safety baselines. We secure these by tying liability relief to pre-release checks. Rules in product safety and aviation show that liability protection must depend on meeting audited technical standards before sales begin. When the Federal Aviation Administration certifies an aircraft design under Title 14 of the Code of Federal Regulations, it does not check every downstream pilot. Instead, it holds the maker accountable for the basic safety of the machine. Model developers who submit their systems for independent safety tests and tracking receive safe-harbor protection from downstream misuse liability. Developers who skip pre-deployment checks lose that shield and face direct fines for uncontained dual-use spread.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

The Department of Commerce faces a false choice between panic and total freedom. Both safety alarmists and tech boosters treat software as a wild force instead of a corporate product.

Safetyists claim that sharing model weights makes central fixes impossible once files leave servers. This assumes code moves without help. In truth, running top models needs massive clusters of H100 graphics processing units run by a few cloud giants.

Frontier labs use procedural checklists and trade-secret rules to hide flaws. This lets bosses escape blame while dumping risk onto victims. When revenue pressure forces engineers to skip tests, firms cut safety standards to save compute costs.

Vendor licensing rules block independent researchers from finding these flaws before release. This shields corporate officers from product lawsuits under standard state-of-the-art defenses.

The Department of Commerce must reject doomsday stories. It must target the tech executives who profit by shifting blame.

The Export Control Reform Act of 2018 lets the Bureau of Industry and Security track physical compute chokepoints instead of policing static numbers.

Lawmakers should tie public model weight releases to mandatory corporate indemnity funds. Firms that make billions must pay for the downstream security failures they now push onto the public.
