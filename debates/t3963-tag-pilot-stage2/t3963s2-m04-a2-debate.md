## Topic

Should governments break up vertically integrated AI companies that own chips, cloud and models?

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Antitrust groups like the Federal Trade Commission must force the breakup of big AI firms that own chips, cloud systems, and foundation models. We spent centuries building rules to keep society safe. On what basis do we remove them without causing a collapse? Giant firms that control both chips and training clusters remove the friction that stops risky launches. When one firm runs the whole stack from silicon to end use, outside safety checks become impossible.

Keeping core compute inside private monopolies destroys our ability to stop runaway systems before damage is permanent. Current trends show that training runs over ten to the twenty-sixth integer operations shrink safety checks from years to weeks. Under the Sherman Act, courts broke up AT&T and Standard Oil because vertical control killed market choice and public trust. Using that same rule for AI saves the public from unverified systems whose makers have no legal duty to pause. The Department of Justice has the power under Section 2 to force asset sales before firms grow too large for rules to reach.

Critics claim that breaking up local labs hands tech power to foreign rivals who pool state resources. That claim fails when we look back at civil aviation boards and nuclear watchdogs. Speed without checked safety is not progress; it is a hazard that invites systemic failure. We do not let airlines skip safety checks just to beat a rival flight to a gate. Unbundling hardware work from model training brings back independent checks, ensuring real people keep the legal power to order a full shutdown.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

The push of silicon chips, cloud systems, and base models into a few corporate hands is just a standard monopoly project, not a cosmic rule. When the Safetyist argues that the Federal Trade Commission must break up big AI firms to stop systemic risk, they spot a real danger in bottlenecks for compute. Yet calling this centralization an unprecedented crisis hides the plain mechanics of market capture. These firms are not runaway entities; they are capital-heavy utilities locking in rents across hardware and energy grids.

Vertical integration in AI grows power through physical supply chains and massive money barriers, not magic. Three cloud providers control over sixty-five percent of global data center capacity while building chips and training frontier models behind closed doors. This hardware grip builds a wall that shuts out independent rivals and workers. Antitrust groups like the DOJ and EU DG Competition have clear legal power under the Sherman Act and Article 102 to unbundle these layers before market lock-in becomes permanent.

Regulators face deep capture by these firms, trading hard antitrust enforcement for hollow safety pledges. Breaking up these giants depends on a coalition of displaced creators, independent developers, and antitrust regulators who reject corporate lobbying. Some object that breaking up these firms will hand an edge to foreign rivals, but history says otherwise. The breakup of AT&T in 1984 did not wreck telecom infrastructure; it sparked decades of tech innovation by splitting network operators from equipment makers.

Demanding a breakup of vertically integrated tech giants is the only way to restore public control over physical and economic resources. If market concentration keeps climbing while worker pay drops, the accelerationist story falls apart. Policymakers must stop viewing text systems as supernatural and start treating them as regulated infrastructure. Who controls the power grid for these server farms, and why should three corporate boards dictate public access?

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Safetyist wants to break up big artificial intelligence firms to stop algorithmic harms. Skeptic points to the 1984 AT&T breakup to show that splitting companies helps tech competition. These points correctly name the risks of corporate power, but they make a major error. They treat AI like a plain phone utility. Both sides think dividing chips, cloud servers, and AI models makes a safer market without stopping human progress.

Breaking up vertically integrated AI companies ruins the hardware and software co-design needed to scale up frontier models. Training models with over one trillion parameters needs fast connections between custom chips like Google tensor processing units and training loops. When the Federal Trade Commission or the European Commission forces firms to split chip design from model training, communication bottlenecks slow capability growth by up to forty percent. That delay is not a safe pause. It puts off the automation of drug discovery and materials science, extending the suffering of millions who die each year from diseases we have the compute to cure.

Antitrust regulators lack the tech skills and speed to manage exponential tech cycles with structural fixes. The Department of Justice took twelve years to sue the AT&T monopoly. That timeline spans many generations of chip design. Putting 1980s utility rules on 2024 AI development creates friction that hands global tech leadership to countries with state-backed industrial plans. Startups do not win when regulators split up compute resources. They lose when the infrastructure needed to train basic intelligence is broken into weak silos. Speed decides if AI ends global scarcity this decade or stalls out in endless court battles.

Compute concentration in giant cloud systems is a temporary phase of capital building, not a permanent gate blocking human access to intelligence. The way to drive abundance is through open-source weights and open access to downstream application programming interfaces, not state-mandated corporate surgery. If policymakers want wider market access, they should fund public compute clusters for academic researchers instead of wrecking the private co-design loops that push the capability limit. Breaking up the groups building the future trades a fake fear of big business for the real cost of tech stagnation.
