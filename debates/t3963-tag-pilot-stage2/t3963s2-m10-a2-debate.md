## Topic

Under what structural conditions do compute advantages drive frontier AI market concentration rather than open competition, and how should antitrust regulators evaluate these dynamics?

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Antitrust regulators must protect physical compute concentration. Hardware bottlenecks are the only physical choke point left to manage high-consequence risks. The Department of Justice and the Federal Trade Commission look at cloud scaling only through price competition. That old view ignores a physical truth. Training frontier artificial intelligence requires clusters with over ten thousand specialized chips like Nvidia H100 units. When officials break up these centralized infrastructure moats for open markets, they remove vital checkpoints. These checkpoints are needed to audit and stop dangerous autonomous systems before release. Aviation authorities never forced open-source jet turbine making. They used strict gatekeeping because catastrophic failures demand central control.

Concentrating compute in a few cloud firms creates real monopoly risks. But spreading out compute destroys human control forever. Startups and university groups face heavy costs to use clusters over one thousand chips. This does lock in big firms. Yet sending unmonitored model weights to millions of edge devices creates a trap. No single group keeps the power or means to issue a software recall. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission controls atomic fuel because spread-out nuclear material cannot be recalled. Frontier artificial intelligence models crossing the ten-to-the-twenty-sixth training FLOP threshold need that same physical custody. Giving hardware access to thousands of decentralized groups trades short-term market health for the loss of a human off-switch.

Congress and agencies must set up mandatory licensing. Cloud providers must check safety baselines before they hand out large cluster chips. The National Institute of Standards and Technology can set these technical limits. They can tie hardware access to proven safety and rollback rules. This plan does not kill antitrust review. It puts market competition below the preservation of human control. When the Federal Aviation Administration certifies a plane, safety comes before market competition. Antitrust regulators must use that exact order for frontier artificial intelligence. They must act before spread-out compute takes away our power to intervene.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Safetyists argue that physical compute concentration offers the only real regulatory chokepoint to stop catastrophic frontier risks before deployment. That argument fails when you look at the physical and financial reality of hardware. Three major cloud providers control this infrastructure. These firms do not use hardware limits to protect the public. They use capital costs topping ten billion dollars per cluster to block rivals and lock in permanent market control.

This high concentration of AI hardware mirrors past utility monopolies that successfully captured their regulators. Cloud giants dictate contract terms to downstream creators and capture all the financial gains while shifting operational risks onto the public grid. Power demands from data centers now strain local grids in Virginia and Oregon. Everyday ratepayers foot the bill for grid upgrades while tech monopolies harvest the profits.

Antitrust regulators must reject the false choice between monopoly safety and chaotic deployment. The Federal Trade Commission and the Department of Justice have existing legal power under the Sherman Act to break up vertically integrated cloud monopolies. Regulators should force a structural split between cloud infrastructure providers and model developers. Monopoly control over silicon is just a business strategy disguised as risk management, and the public bears the cost.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Safetyists argue that hardware bottlenecks act as a physical choke point to lower risk, while skeptics demand antitrust rules to split cloud providers from model builders. Both sides note that huge spending buys massive compute clusters at Microsoft, Google, and Amazon. Yet both ignore how fast software gains wipe out those hardware moats. Physical compute concentration is a temporary resource gap, not a permanent barrier to entry.

Open-weight models break closed compute monopolies by matching top skills at a fraction of training cost. Meta released Llama 3 with 400 billion parameters to rival closed systems while letting teams fine-tune models locally on standard hardware. Community updates move faster than the R&D cycles of any cloud giant. When builders run smart tools at the edge, cloud lock-in ends. Regulators must see that software spreads faster than hardware centralizes every single month.

Tying rules to static hardware benchmarks causes gridlock and protects legacy giants from agile market rivals. The Federal Trade Commission and the Department of Justice would build a permanent oligopoly if they split firms based on raw compute limits under Section 2 of the Sherman Act. Cloud infrastructure spending tops two hundred billion dollars a year, but open-source use grows faster than cloud building. Freezing deployment to fit safety models hurts people by delaying cures, labor relief, and open innovation.
