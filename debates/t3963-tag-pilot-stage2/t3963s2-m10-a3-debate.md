## Topic

Under what conditions do compute bottlenecks and cloud infrastructure partnerships drive frontier AI markets toward consolidation rather than open competition, and how should antitrust regulators evaluate these structural trends?

## Opening Statements

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Frontier artificial intelligence capability growth relies on massive compute-scaling rules that make early infrastructure clusters inevitable. Published scaling curves show predictable capability gains for each order of magnitude increase in floating-point operations. Training a frontier model needs cluster assemblies costing billions of dollars and using hundreds of megavolt-amperes of power. The Federal Trade Commission and the Department of Justice must not mistake this temporary hardware grouping for permanent market foreclosure. Antitrust action that breaks up cloud partnerships today directly stops the computers needed to solve complex biological and material sciences tomorrow.

Open-source growth quickly turns centralized training into shared networks, making permanent antitrust action obsolete and harmful. Past developer habits show open-weight models match proprietary baselines within months of release. Fine-tuning runs now happen across thousands of independent developer groups instead of inside single corporate silos. When the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890 applies to modern server hardware, old regulators freeze the exact tool that spreads out power. Cloud infrastructure partnerships build the heavy machinery for the first training phase, but open-weight releases immediately scatter the final tools across the global economy.

Regulatory delay has a real human cost that beats speculative antitrust fears. Every month the FTC stops a cloud partnership over theoretical market consolidation is a month when automated drug discovery targets stay uncomputed. Over one hundred thousand patients die daily from diseases that advanced machine learning models can turn decades of research into months to cure. Who gave antitrust enforcers the moral right to trade human lives for abstract market share numbers? The abundance imperative asks policymakers to clear the path for rapid compute scaling and open distribution without blocks.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Massive compute sizing and huge power deals between cloud giants and power companies create a weak spot that invites regulatory capture. The Federal Trade Commission and the Department of Justice must treat physical power bottlenecks as core targets instead of neutral market outcomes. When Microsoft spends over ten billion dollars to secure data center capacity for frontier model training, that cash centralization avoids democratic checks. Antitrust regulators have the power under Section 7 of the Clayton Act to stop vertical mergers that pack irreversible tech into single firms. Without physical infrastructure checks, shutting out smaller firms becomes the normal rule for the entire AI market.

Physical compute clusters offer clear points to check and stop unmonitored capability jumps before deployment. Tracking data center power use lets the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission and antitrust agencies stop unauthorized training runs that pass limits like ten to the twenty-sixth integer operations. Critics argue that hard antitrust action will just push frontier model building underground into fragmented, open-weight files that lack a central compliance gate. That objection ignores the hard physical facts of chip supply chains and high-voltage power grids needed to back frontier training. ASML lithography machines and gigawatt power plants cannot hide in a basement; they are fixed, stationary points where government oversight stays fully active.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Frontier AI markets are shrinking down to three firms because physical data centers cost over thirty billion dollars each. This massive price tag blocks open competition.

The Accelerationist says that rapid compute scaling makes this early market crash a temporary phase before tech spreads to everyone. That point hits a real technical truth, but it mistakes hardware limits for software freedom. It ignores how power purchase agreements lock out smaller rivals. When three cloud giants grab gigawatt-scale power grids, market power follows the physical electricity, not the smart code.

The Safetyist claims that tracking data center power use gives watchdogs a clear way to stop massive unauthorized training runs. That idea spots the real physical footprint of AI training, but it fails because facilities move and inference scales across many places. Meanwhile, the real costs of this setup hit local water supplies and regular power users. These groups face grid strain without getting a cut of the tech profits.

Federal antitrust teams at the FTC and DOJ must use the Clayton Act to stop cloud partnerships before vertical integration kills off application markets.

Phase-one arguments fail when physical assets like high-voltage power lines and custom chips are locked up for decades. The right historical comparison is not the early internet garage startup, but the nineteenth-century railroad trust where track owners controlled all trade.

Forcing a split between cloud providers and frontier model makers gives us a proven fix under current antitrust laws. Society handles major new tech not by stopping construction, but by treating physical bottlenecks as public utilities when they control access to the digital economy.
