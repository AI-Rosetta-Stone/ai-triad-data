## Topic

Under what market concentration thresholds should the FTC and DOJ compel the structural unbundling of proprietary compute and foundational models to prevent ecosystem foreclosure by vertically integrated AI firms?

## Opening Statements

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Vertical integration of compute and foundational models drives capability scaling, not market failure. Frontier models need vast capital that only cloud providers can build fast. Microsoft and Google proved this with multi-billion dollar clusters in 2024. Forcing structural unbundling destroys scale advantages and trades market growth for regulatory stagnation. This central planning protects legacy firms through bureaucratic delay while punishing agile builders who use unified infrastructure.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

The Accelerationist argues that vertically integrating foundational models and proprietary compute is a strict technical necessity to secure the capability frontier. This makes structural unbundling an act of industrial self-sabotage that destroys scale advantages. That position rests on a genuine insight: frontier model training requires dense capital allocation and synchronized hardware clusters. We see this in the multi-billion-dollar buildouts executed by firms like Microsoft and Google over the past three years. The error lies in treating a deliberate corporate enclosure strategy as an unyielding law of physics. Cloud hyperscalers captured 68 percent of the enterprise infrastructure market by bundling proprietary silicon, storage, and software layers together to lock in corporate clients. Economic history shows that general-purpose technologies achieve broad diffusion only when underlying network bottlenecks are separated from application layers. The Federal Communications Commission demonstrated this during the 1982 AT&T antitrust settlement.

Ecosystem foreclosure occurs when dominant firms use compute bottlenecks to dictate terms to downstream software developers and enterprise buyers. Bureaucratic administrative adaptation through the Department of Justice and the Federal Trade Commission remains fully equipped to enforce structural separation of proprietary model layers from cloud infrastructure. The Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890 provides the statutory authority to compel functional unbundling when capital-intensive gatekeepers restrict market access. Enterprise adoption data from the National Institute of Standards and Technology shows that 74 percent of mid-sized firms currently face severe vendor lock-in when attempting to migrate machine learning models across competing cloud environments. Forcing structural unbundling breaks this bottleneck, protects market competition, and accelerates economy-wide diffusion without damaging technological capability.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

We spent centuries building the rules that keep society stable. We cannot drop them now just for software scaling. The Federal Trade Commission and the Department of Justice must force the breakup of proprietary compute and foundational models when training clusters go past 10^26 floating-point operations. 

Accelerationist models assume that vertical integration of compute and algorithms is an unstoppable engine of progress that antitrust action must not touch. That view treats rapid capability growth as a good on its own while ignoring the permanent loss of verification capacity that comes from such extreme capital concentration. 

Skeptic models point out that cloud hyperscalers already create severe vendor lock-in for enterprise clients. They doubt whether structural remedies can catch up to fast-moving markets. That critique correctly spots corporate gatekeeping. But it underestimates how easily private lock-in turns into an unrecoverable civilizational failure mode when safety checks are hidden inside the firm.

Concentrated infrastructure hides safety failures behind opaque corporate walls that no outside auditor can check. Historical precedents in telecommunications, such as the 1982 AT&T breakup, and energy sector unbundling proved that structural separation is the only way to get regulatory visibility and risk management.

When three firms control both the silicon supply and the frontier models, independent safety testing becomes impossible because the testers answer to the deployers. Congress made the Nuclear Regulatory Commission fully independent from plant operators. It did this because commercial pressures always override safety rules when money is on the line.

We cannot let frontier AI labs police their own alignment work while they sell those same models as commercial monopolies. Vertical integration of compute speeds up scaling faster than safety checks can follow. This threatens civilizational stability through errors we cannot fix.

High-consequence failures in civil engineering and aviation, such as the early jet groundings of the 1950s, show that unverified scaling leads straight to system collapse. The Department of Justice antitrust division has the statutory authority under Section 2 of the Sherman Act to break up monopolies that create high barriers to safety oversight.

Critics will argue that unbundling ruins scale economies and hurts national competitiveness against foreign actors who ignore safety. That objection fails because speed without checked human control is not real competitiveness. It is just an unsafe bet on civilizational survival.

We assume that regulatory bodies have the technical skill and political backing to force this unbundling without immediate capture by incumbent lobbies. The enforcement plan relies on the FTC issuing mandatory orders to sell off compute clusters above the limit, backed by federal marshals and court orders if firms do not comply.

A coalition of public safety groups, independent software developers, and traditional industrial competitors supports this split to restore market contestability and oversight. On what authority do you claim we can remove institutional boundaries without inviting collapse?

Claiming that physical cable lag makes structural unbundling impossible confuses software bundling with hardware physics. Modern 800 Gbps InfiniBand switches and PCIe Gen 5 protocols show how systems can be modularly split apart. When the Department of Justice oversaw the 1982 telecom break-up, phone companies claimed splitting local networks from long-distance lines would ruin call routing. That tech warning failed because open rules let outside makers build better gear at the edges than any single firm could make inside. Modern AI chips face similar limits, but the proprietary software locks holding them inside single-vendor clouds are commercial walls, not copper-wire limits.

The urgency argument used to defend big model makers gets the history of drug innovation backward. Corporate control over patient databases and clinical code does not speed up drug discovery. It creates choke points where life-saving discoveries get stuck or priced too high, like the 37 cancer drugs stalled in 2023 by exclusive licenses. Past fights over tech and power did not stop progress; they forced firms to share base infrastructure with outside inventors. Breaking up training monopolies opens data to thousands of independent labs, expanding the talent pool for medical tools.

The real cost to health comes from tech giants gatekeeping the compute power needed for biology research. When three firms control the hardware for large biological sims, hospitals and medical centers wait in corporate queues. Structural splits fix this by requiring open access to 50 percent of cluster compute time, trading private rationing for public markets. True innovation relies on spread-out experiments, not corporate command.
