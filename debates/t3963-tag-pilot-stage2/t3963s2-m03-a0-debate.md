## Opening Statements

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Export control regulators and safety advocates claim that restricting public model weight releases stops biosecurity disasters. History shows that export controls on dual-use software protect domestic cartels and slow down technology diffusion instead of stopping bad actors. When the Bureau of Industry and Security restricted cryptographic software in the nineties, open-source developers published source code in other countries. At the same time, domestic firms lobbied to lock out smaller rivals. Large frontier AI labs now use this same national security rhetoric to lobby for licensing rules that price open-source tools out of the market. The main winners of public weight limits are commercial labs that lock in markets, while decentralized developers pay the price by losing access to core tools.

Frontier labs argue that releasing frontier model weights to the public lowers the barrier to making new pathogens, making early limits a needed safety step. That view assumes central rules can control peer-to-peer networks. Local hardware runs and torrent networks make enforcement fail across borders. When agencies like the Food and Drug Administration or the Department of Commerce try to regulate software weights, the code already circulates on GitHub and decentralized servers past their legal reach. Rules do not stop bad actors; they only crush small academic teams who cannot pay for legal compliance staff.

Policymakers must reject unproven biosecurity stories that serve as tools for corporate market capture. The enforcement tools for weight limits rely on power given to agencies like the Department of Commerce, yet these rules lack proof linking open-weights access to real pathogen synthesis events. True biosecurity governance requires tracking physical DNA orders at commercial gene synthesis providers rather than policing math equations on public code sites. A team of dominant tech firms and risk-averse bureaucrats backs these limits because compliance costs build a moat against open-source rivals. Congress should demand real incident data from frontier labs before trading away open-source innovation for unverified safety theater.
