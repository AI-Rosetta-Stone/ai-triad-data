## Topic

Should governments break up vertically integrated AI companies that own chips, cloud and models?

## Opening Statements

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Governments must force the breakup of vertically integrated AI firms that control foundational chips, cloud infrastructure, and proprietary models. Industrial concentration creates a bottleneck where a few corporations own the entire stack from silicon wafers to power substations. When three companies control over sixty-five percent of global hyperscale cloud capacity, market competition dies and rent extraction begins. Antitrust law treats this exact pattern of hardware and software tying as a direct violation of the Sherman Act.

Opponents claim that breaking up these firms will destroy economies of scale and hand global tech leadership to foreign rivals. That defense assumes corporate bigness equals national security while ignoring the actual cost of resource use. Data centers run by major cloud providers now draw gigawatts of power from regional grids, straining local infrastructure and raising utility bills for families. The Department of Energy reports that data center power demand will double by 2026, forcing working-class households to pay for the energy use of text-prediction engines.

Structural separation fixes this market failure by setting clear legal lines between hardware manufacturing, cloud hosting, and model building. The Federal Communications Commission applied this exact functional split to telecom monopolies in the 1984 Bell System breakup. Antitrust regulators have the legal power under Section 2 of the Sherman Act to force the breakup of monopolies that control key facilities. Breaking the stack protects public energy grids, lowers entry barriers for independent software firms, and shifts blame back to the companies making the profits.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Governments must break up vertically integrated AI firms. Unified control over chips, cloud systems, and AI models destroys independent oversight. The Skeptic is right that market self-regulation fails when a few firms control both compute power and the AI models.

Over sixty-five percent of global cloud capacity and chip manufacturing now sits inside three companies. This extreme concentration creates an unmonitored mix of capability and compute that makes human oversight impossible.

Structural split is a legal must to make safety checks work without corporate interference. History in telecom and energy shows that unbundling infrastructure from service providers restores safety audits and accountability.

When AT&T faced breakup in the 1982 antitrust settlement, separating long-distance lines from local services restored regulatory sight. Applying this standard to AI stops developers from hiding safety hazards found on their own cloud networks.

Critics claim that forced breakup ruins scale and hurts national security against foreign state monopolies. That objection fails against the permanent loss of human control over unverified systems.

Split companies will work with independent safety auditors when federal law ties market access to compliance. Congress must pass structural unbundling laws enforced by the Federal Trade Commission to stop catastrophic failures before deployment.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Forcing the break-up of vertically integrated artificial intelligence companies is a direct assault on the physical infrastructure needed to speed up human progress. Skeptic argues that unified control over chips, cloud systems, and proprietary models creates huge market power chokepoints that justify state antitrust intervention. Safetyist contends that combining these layers removes independent oversight and concentrates catastrophic risks inside single firms. Those arguments correctly spot the power concentration, but they mistake the symptoms of extreme scale for structural faults. The real tension is whether regulators should intentionally break the exact supply chain links that make rapid capability scaling possible.

Building the compute clusters needed to cure diseases and automate complex labor requires unprecedented physical co-location of chips, power grids, and massive cloud datacenters. When past shifts like the 1956 AT&T consent decree forced corporate splits, they separated R&D from manufacturing and delayed deployment by years. Modern artificial intelligence training runs already demand gigawatt-scale power inputs that beat the capacity of fragmented utility markets. Under the 2024 Department of Justice and Federal Trade Commission antitrust enforcement guidelines, breaking up these integrated firms would cause severe coordination friction in energy grid buying. Who benefits from breaking these supply chains? Only legacy incumbent bureaucracies that trade human longevity for administrative ease, while patients and innovators bear the direct cost of delay.

Vertical integration is not a monopoly plot to raise prices, but an engineering need for moving atoms and bits at top speed. When firms like Microsoft, Google, and Amazon co-locate chip design with proprietary models, they hit iteration cycles that split consortia cannot match. The enforcement mechanism for break-ups relies on courts and antitrust regulators who lack the technical skill to run power grids or wafer plants. Political backing for such splits rests on a mix of legacy industrial incumbents and precautionary regulators who fear losing control. When you choose to freeze development through forced corporate break-ups, you choose to let people die today of diseases we could automate a cure for. Who gave you the right to make that choice for them?

Regional transmission organizations already coordinate multi-megawatt energy interconnections daily without requiring single-firm ownership of the underlying generation assets. The argument that unbundling causes grid friction is industry lobbying designed to protect corporate rent extraction under the guise of technical efficiency.

When corporate balance sheets absorb billions in compute revenues while municipal taxpayers cover substation overhauls, the system ceases to be an engine of progress and becomes a tax on the public. Divestiture separates the power consumer from the utility manager, forcing corporations to pay the actual marginal cost of the electricity they consume.
