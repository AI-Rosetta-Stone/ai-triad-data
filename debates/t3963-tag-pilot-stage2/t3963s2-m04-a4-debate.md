## Topic

Should governments break up vertically integrated AI companies that own chips, cloud and models?

## Opening Statements

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Electricity and telecom did not upend society in five years, and AI will follow that same slow, steady curve. Modern computing relies on massive capital spending. Firms like Microsoft, Google, and Amazon spend over one hundred billion dollars a year on data centers and custom chips. This physical bottleneck makes structural breakups a necessity, not a test. Antitrust regulators must split cloud infrastructure from model design before private monopolies lock in permanent economic tolls.

Proponents of vertical integration claim that tight links between chip buying and model training are the only way to fund frontier tools. That argument mirrors claims made by AT&T before its 1982 breakup, which stated that network health required unified ownership of gear and services. History proves otherwise. Splitting these layers does not stop engineering progress; it opens closed stacks to independent builders. The DOJ and the FTC already hold the legal power under Section 2 of the Sherman Act to enforce these lines.

Enforcing this rule means treating core compute sites as regulated common carriers bound by rate rules. When a few firms control the power grid links, water cooling supplies, and frontier chips needed for advanced math, they act as private infrastructure utilities. State attorneys general and federal regulators must tie utility access to fair terms to stop deep economic gaps. Building this rule-based firewall keeps public control alive without trusting unproven self-regulation by tech bosses.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

We must force AI firms to split up their hardware, cloud, and model units. This structural break is a vital safeguard to stop us from losing control of advanced AI. The Skeptic notes that modern computing relies on massive capital spending of over one hundred billion dollars a year on data centers and custom chips. Yet, this huge pile of money creates a false sense of safety. It makes us think private firms can safely run systems that impact all of society without public oversight. When one firm owns the chips, the cooling gear, and the top model weights, public oversight turns into corporate self-dealing.

Concentrating top compute in a few superstar firms builds a trap. We grow too dependent on their infrastructure, which leads straight to regulatory capture. Right now, three firms control over eighty percent of cloud capacity and most advanced training runs. This vertical stack lets developers skip safety audits by locking deployment choices into their own hardware. If an uncontained system starts self-improving on closed servers, outside agencies lack the physical access needed to pull the plug. We saw this in two thousand eight when hidden financial tools bypassed public checks and broke trust across global markets.

Breaking these tech giants into separate hardware, cloud, and model firms brings back real oversight. Once we break the chain, the chip maker cannot set the safety rules for the models running on those chips. Regulators at the Federal Trade Commission and the Antitrust Division can then enforce pre-deployment rules. They can demand proof of safety before these models launch for public use. Without a breakup, every safety rule is just a messy deal with firms that are too big to regulate and too strong to stop.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Mandating the structural breakup of AI hardware, cloud, and foundation models creates bad delays that stall medical cures and economic growth. The safetyists and skeptics correctly point out the huge money needed for this shift, but their fix causes the exact stagnation they fear. Forcing a split between chip making, cloud systems, and model training turns a fast pipeline into a slow mess of contract talks and lag. When one training run costs over one hundred billion dollars and uses millions of chips at once, splitting the system stops progress.

Working closely across silicon, cloud, and model training is the only way to grow power fast without lag. Past chip supply chains show that tight hardware and software teamwork made performance gains that modular setups could not match. As we move to multi-gigawatt data centers, custom chips must change as algorithms shift. If antitrust enforcers split chip designers from cloud providers, engineers lose the vital feedback loops needed to scale intelligence. The Department of Justice and the European Commission have the power under Section 2 of the Sherman Act to order breakups, but doing this on advanced AI stacks would freeze training runs for years while lawyers fight over lines.

Calls for structural breakups use antitrust rules to add slow friction and halt progress. Past breakups, like the 1984 AT&T divestiture, hurt big scaling for a generation while consumer prices rose. Critics claim that corporate power creates single points of failure, but a split system has never built a top model at scale. When regulators choose to fracture integrated AI firms, they choose to let people suffer longer from diseases and waste that fast scaling could fix today.

Antitrust history in network industries shows that unbundling builds engineering skill by replacing secret internal deals with open market rules. When safety checks rely entirely on corporate math, the audit is just for show, not a real check on dangerous traits. Breaking the vertical chain ensures that the chip supplier cannot dictate the safety limits for the models running on that hardware. This structural split is the only way to keep human control over AI when systems scale past our ability to recall or override them.

Large training runs need fast hardware links, but chip delay is not the same as corporate power. When one firm owns the chips, the data centers, the cloud, and the AI models, that is a business choice, not a law of physics. The hundred billion dollars spent by tech firms do not prove that single monopolies are required for progress. Instead, that cash builds walls to block small rivals and lock down the digital economy.

History shows that breaking up key networks helps competition grow without slowing progress. Under the 1996 Telecommunications Act, regulators forced phone giants to share their networks with independent firms, which helped the internet grow. Power grid rules also split power plants from power lines to stop regional monopolies from blocking new energy suppliers. Applying this rule to tech means that splitting cloud servers from AI models will not destroy tech skills. It just stops big firms from bossing around every developer who needs their servers.

Treating big data centers as public utilities keeps research fast while saving the market from private control. The claim that this split causes delays misreads how software works, because coders already build systems across independent cloud networks. The Federal Trade Commission and the Department of Justice can enforce this split without writing code, simply by stopping one firm from owning the whole hardware chain. To protect the public, we must stop tech monopolies before they lock in permanent tolls on digital infrastructure.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist) → Skeptic
\textcolor{NavyBlue}{\textit{Focus: Skeptic's claim AN-13 regarding the 1996 Telecommunications Act and its applicability to AI hardware and cloud infrastructure}}


Comparing frontier AI training runs to the 1996 Telecommunications Act completely misunderstands the underlying hardware physics. Asynchronous telephone packets tolerate millisecond jitter and modular handoffs across disparate local loop providers because human speech does not require sub-nanosecond tensor synchronization. By contrast, a 100,000-node cluster training a trillion-parameter model across multi-gigawatt facilities relies on NVLink switching fabrics and InfiniBand rails operating at blistering sub-microsecond latencies. Introducing artificial corporate partitions across this hardware stack injects insurmountable serialization delays that collapse cluster throughput entirely.

Splitting the silicon supplier from the cloud host and the model developer treats physical compute as a modular commodity like copper wire. In reality, modern training runs on advanced GPU architectures require precise thermal and electrical coordination across millions of specialized chips running in lockstep. When hyperscalers spend over one hundred billion dollars annually on integrated data centers, they are not building simple routing switches; they are constructing unified thermodynamic engines. Mandating structural separation in these hyper-dense facilities breaks the exact synchronization necessary to push the capability frontier forward.

Delaying this progress through regulatory unbundling carries a devastating human cost. Every month of engineering paralysis caused by forced corporate breakups defers the automation of medical diagnostics, the discovery of new life-saving therapeutics, and the elimination of scarcity. Those who demand modular fragmentation to protect legacy market structures ignore the real people whose lives hang in the balance of technological velocity. If you choose to freeze development out of bureaucratic preference, you are explicitly deciding that people must keep dying of diseases we have the compute to cure.
