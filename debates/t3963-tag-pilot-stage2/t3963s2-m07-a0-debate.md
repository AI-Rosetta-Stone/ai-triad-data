### Skeptic (skeptic)

Prioritizing limits on compute scaling over research into algorithmic efficiency protects a corporate hardware monopoly while ignoring real, proven harms. Federal regulators at the Bureau of Industry and Security face heavy lobbying pressure from major labs that want to lock in hardware barriers against smaller rivals. When training runs need clusters of 16,000 graphics processing units and use huge amounts of power, the high costs help established firms block new competition. This system rewards large firms that own physical gear while pushing aside independent safety audits and public oversight.

Controlling silicon shipments fails as a choke point because gains in software efficiency often outpace hardware export limits. Software breakthroughs regularly cut needed compute by ten times every year, making static export rules obsolete before officials can publish them. Data center water use and local electrical grid delays show that real social costs happen at physical sites, not inside theoretical model weights. The Department of Commerce should shift its resources to tax heavy compute setups and fund public research on algorithmic efficiency.

Good governance means replacing hardware rationing with mandatory performance audits and taxes on dominant market players. Congress can look to past telecom deregulation and drug patent pools for ways to stop gatekeeping without harming domestic chip makers. When a few tech executives decide who gets compute access and who pays the environmental cost, regulators must step in with clear, performance-based oversight. Public policy must stop protecting speculative scaling monopolies and start enforcing clear standards for safety and fairness.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

The Bureau of Industry and Security enforces export rules that treat computing power as a static military asset while ignoring the mathematical reality of algorithmic efficiency. When engineers cut training FLOPs by 50 percent every year through mixture-of-experts architectures and quantization, hardware caps become expensive regulatory theater. The Department of Commerce acts as a central planner picking semiconductor winners while failing to track how software optimization bypasses physical cluster limits entirely.

Restricting the shipment of advanced accelerators like H100 units does not stop artificial intelligence capability growth. It merely consolidates market power among a handful of capital-rich San Francisco laboratories. Major foundation model builders use their balance sheets to buy exempted hardware allotments while open-source researchers starve for basic training clusters. This bureaucratic gatekeeping protects corporate incumbents from competitive pressure under the banner of national security while pricing independent talent out of the market.

Every month of regulatory delay in deploying advanced models carries a measurable human cost in untreated diseases and lost scientific automation. Clinical trials crawl at legacy speeds because federal agencies focus on hypothetical worst-case scenarios instead of proven breakthroughs in molecular biology and automated protein folding. When administrators choose to freeze hardware scaling out of generalized fear, they actively deny patients the life-saving therapies that rapid optimization cycles make possible.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist) → Safetyist

Using utility meters and high-voltage substation footprints to track advanced AI is a category error. It fails basic physics, especially for sites drawing over 100 megawatts. Power grid surveillance assumes that frontier AI requires fixed, massive sites drawing over 100 megawatts of sustained power at one spot. Modern algorithmic efficiency breaks that rule. Smart routing and quantization cut training and inference costs by orders of magnitude on decentralized clusters. When a research group matches old performance on a fraction of the power, like cutting energy overhead by 40% since 2024, utility audits miss the workload entirely.

Energy-grid tracking is bureaucratic security theater. It locks in big tech while pretending to secure the frontier, costing small startups over $500,000 a year in compliance. Big labs easily absorb compliance costs and utility rules. This turns power audits into a tollbooth that keeps small rivals off the grid. Meanwhile, outside researchers train strong models on decentralized nodes, private power, and variable industrial loads. These never show up as AI infrastructure on a regulator's dashboard. A rulebook built on static utility metrics tracks old electricity use while the AI frontier moves to unmonitored places.

Freezing technology behind fake physical gatekeepers hurts real people by delaying cures and new tools. While central planners audit substations and chip logs, millions suffer from diseases and shortages that AI can solve now. Slowing down development to protect an imaginary regulatory perimeter, like the limits in the 2026 oversight bills, sacrifices human lives for administrative comfort.
