## Topic

Under what conditions should frontier AI labs and the National Science Foundation prioritize interpretability research over capability scaling as part of upcoming safety standard appropriations, and how does this shift trade off risks for commercial developers versus public safety advocates?

## Opening Statements

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Halting compute scaling to fund interpretability research trades proven performance gains for unproven theoretical guarantees. Empirical scaling laws from labs like OpenAI and Anthropic show that model capabilities advance predictably as power laws of compute investments. Diverting National Science Foundation grants and corporate R&D budgets away from that scaling engine stops the delivery of high-utility models that cure diseases and automate grueling labor. Policymakers who mandate interpretability pauses impose a direct human toll by freezing tools that can speed up drug discovery and materials science today.

Static behavioral safety scorecards fail because adversaries bypass them instantly through targeted fine-tuning. When regulators enforce rigid compliance rules based on current model internals, they hand large firms a protective moat while locking out open-source innovators. The Federal Communications Commission and the Federal Trade Commission learned this lesson during early broadband rules where fixed standards calcified markets before technologies matured. Independent deployment in open ecosystems surfaces edge-case errors faster and more reliably than closed committee reviews ever will.

Critics argue that uninterpretable models have unpredictable failure modes that justify freezing scaling until internal mechanisms are fully mapped. That objection collapses against the reality of complex systems where functional capability always outpaces complete internal cartography. No engineer maps every transistor in a modern GPU before shipping it to market, and treating artificial intelligence with a higher standard of zero-risk perfection halts human progress entirely. Decentralized deployment remains the only empirical mechanism to discover and patch errors at scale.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

The tech growth view assumes that fast commercial sales and late safety patches can beat the growth of unsafe hidden traits in big neural nets. We must steelman this view: fast compute scaling brings huge economic gain and speeds up general science before red tape can kill new ideas. Yet this model treats safety as a late checklist item instead of a core design rule, missing how easily surface safety tests fail against custom fine-tuning.

Late safety fixes fail under custom testing and cannot set safe limits for top-tier systems. Past flaws in human-guided learning show that gradient-based hacks and parameter-efficient tuning strip away refusal traits with very low computing costs. When labs release models with millions of weights whose inner features stay dark, they trade hard proof for weak behavior rules. The National Science Foundation and top labs must shift funds to mechanistic interpretability to map these inner circuits before they cross safe limits.

Rules must grow with system power to keep human control safe from permanent system risk. Past cases in civil aviation and money market circuit breakers show that complex systems need hard structural limits before scale beats human control. Congress must set law limits that tie compute growth straight to verified interpretability audits instead of voluntary industry roadmaps. Without named police powers and forced rollback rules, commercial builders will keep speed above safety until an unfixable crash happens.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

The Accelerationist argues that halting compute scaling to fund interpretability research trades proven gains for unproven theories. The Safetyist counters that rules must match capability levels to keep human oversight against irreversible risks. Both views rely on a false premise. They assume model capabilities grow like an unstoppable physical law rather than an intentional corporate strategy.

Firms have decoupled performance from raw compute through design tweaks since 2023. This proves that scaling is a choice for market power, not an unalterable destiny. Frontier labs treat scaling as inevitable to externalize safety costs and consolidate control. They shift the financial burden of their product failures onto the public.

The National Science Foundation and Congress must redirect grants away from raw compute. They should fund mechanistic interpretability, which makes neural networks readable to outside auditors. Independent researchers cannot audit systems without access to proprietary weights. Right now, commercial labs block external testing under the shield of trade secrecy.

Federal policy should use performance-gated independent AI audits. This mirrors the Food and Drug Administration drug trial model. Developers must prove safety before deployment instead of policing their own products. History in telecommunications and aviation shows that self-regulation fails once markets outrun voluntary compliance. Congress holds the purse strings and has clear power to tie federal grants to mandatory third-party code review.

### Skeptic (skeptic) → Safetyist
\textcolor{NavyBlue}{\textit{Focus: Safetyist compared regulatory pre-deployment gating to aviation structural stress testing (AN-12), but Skeptic and Accelerationist have argued that software iteration speed and open decentralized ecosystems differ fundamentally from physical manufacturing; Skeptic should address whether structural pre-deployment gates can function without creating unchallengeable industry monopolies.}}


Comparing frontier software deployment to aviation structural certification misreads the underlying economics of replication. While an aircraft manufacturer spends billions tooling a physical fuselage for FAA stress testing under Title 14 of the Code of Federal Regulations, a software developer can copy millions of weights at zero marginal cost. Insisting on centralized pre-market licensing boards for models with fewer than 10 billion parameters does not replicate aviation safety; instead, it erects an administrative tollbooth that only established cloud monopolists can clear. This structural gatekeeping creates an insurmountable barrier for smaller firms and open-weight contributors trying to enter the market.

Acknowledging that high-impact deployment requires rigorous verification does not mean authority should concentrate inside a handful of vertically integrated technology firms or captured federal agencies. When the European Union finalized the Artificial Intelligence Act in 2024, lawmakers built compliance tiers that differentiate between open-source release models and proprietary enterprise APIs. Independent performance-gated auditing through standardized public benchmarks offers a decentralized alternative to closed pre-market licensing. Independent testing laboratories can evaluate model behaviors against documented failure metrics without requiring developers to hand over proprietary codebases to bureaucratic boards.

Structuring oversight around measurable performance outcomes rather than pre-market gatekeeping preserves competitive pluralism while protecting the public from documented harms. If compliance costs scale linearly with model size rather than fixed administrative hurdles, smaller developers and academic institutions can participate in safety verification. Decoupling safety validation from corporate gatekeeping ensures that policy protects communities from bias and labor displacement without cementing the market dominance of a few compute-rich incumbents.
