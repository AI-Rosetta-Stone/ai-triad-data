## Topic

Under what conditions should frontier AI labs and federal regulators reallocate capital between interpretability research and capability scaling, and through what causal mechanisms does this tradeoff affect systemic safety for the public?

## Opening Statements

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Forcing top AI labs to shift money from raw scaling into interpretability research and static rules trades real human abundance for unverified fear. It also locks in power for big tech firms. Agencies like NIST and the US AI Safety Institute want to move billions of dollars away from compute power toward internal audits. This shift builds a barrier against open-source growth to help legacy giants. When rules add heavy costs, startups go bankrupt while dominant firms just pay the fee.

Static safety checks fail under pressure because labs game them instead of fixing real risks. Red-teaming tests show that fixed benchmarks miss dynamic model changes during sudden capability jumps. Critics claim that without interpretability research, labs cannot see causes during training. Yet compute scaling laws give a clearer and more stable metric than opaque internal audits. Capital must follow raw compute power instead of red tape that protects monopolies.

Freezing AI growth out of fear means letting people die today of diseases we could cure. The true cost of delay means untreated cancers and delayed drug discoveries. Decentralized trial-and-error in open-weight models finds fixes faster than central committees can write manuals. Congress must reject rules that starve scaling and instead unleash open innovation.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Frontier AI labs and federal regulators must shift money from scaling up raw power to mechanistic interpretability verification. Unverified neural networks hide dangerous flaws that threaten public stability. Dictionary learning on model activations exposes hidden internal representations and goal shifts that output tests miss. When labs spend billions on compute clusters instead of mapping internal circuits, they trade human control for speed. This unverified trade creates systemic risks that post-hoc red-teaming cannot patch.

Shifting money from raw compute scaling to safety checks is a vital step to keep human control over complex systems. Past safety gaps in complex technologies show that unconstrained deployment without oversight leads to major failures. Commercial labs like OpenAI and Anthropic spend over ninety percent of their research budgets on parameter scaling instead of interpretability tools. Agencies like NIST and the US AI Safety Institute must require minimum budgets for internal state mapping before giving out deployment licenses.

Critics argue that shifting money to safety verification hands an advantage to foreign rivals who prioritize raw power over alignment. That competitive pressure argument fails because a catastrophic model failure in a leading lab would halt the global industry overnight. Real security means treating interpretability research as a non-negotiable engineering baseline rather than optional compliance. If federal regulators fail to mandate budgets for internal feature mapping today, public oversight will surrender to unconstrained machine autonomy tomorrow.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

The safetyist demands that federal regulators force frontier AI labs to shift capital from raw parameter scaling into mechanistic interpretability research. The accelerationist insists that any such capital move trades human abundance for unverified fear. Both sides treat the machine as an autonomous force while ignoring the business incentives of the firms building it. Federal agencies like the National Institute of Standards and Technology face a different reality when capital shifts happen. Mandatory interpretability checks and pre-launch compliance audits act mainly as tools for regulatory capture. These compliance rules build fixed cost walls that protect dominant leaders like OpenAI and Anthropic by crushing smaller rivals and open-source developers.

Past history shows that fixed regulatory costs always lock in industry power instead of protecting the public. The drug industry offers a clear model where mandatory pre-market testing by the Food and Drug Administration raised costs and killed small-scale competitors. Compliance rules do not fix technical risks. Compliance rules build legal moats that let large firms take over the regulatory system. When federal agencies demand complex interpretability work before a launch, they hand rule-making power to the firms with the cash to hire compliance lawyers.

Focusing federal debates on speculative model internals pulls attention and enforcement away from real labor and deployment harms. Current labor data shows fast job loss and copyright fights hurting millions of workers today. Talk of future alignment risks and internal audits waste millions of engineering hours while real deployment harms go unmonitored. Congress must reject capital shift rules that build corporate moats under the flag of safety. Lawmakers must instead fund rules that target real deployment impacts and labor practices directly.

## Debate

### Safetyist (safetyist) → Skeptic
\textcolor{NavyBlue}{\textit{Focus: Skeptic's claim that mandatory interpretability audits function primarily as regulatory capture mechanisms that protect dominant labs (AN-7, AN-8)}}


The critique that compliance requirements function as corporate legal moats is well-founded when administrative burdens are misused to insulate incumbent technology firms from market competition. However, conceding that bad design creates capture does not invalidate the underlying technical necessity of internal state mapping and interpretability verification before deployment. When labs like OpenAI or Anthropic spend over ninety percent of their research budgets on parameter scaling rather than verifiable interpretability tools, the absence of independent pre-market inspection is not a victory for market openness but an abdication of public safety. Federal bodies such as NIST and the US AI Safety Institute must enforce capital minimums and rigorous evaluation standards precisely because unmonitored systems introduce irreversible hazards that post-hoc litigation cannot remediate.

Waiting for post-deployment impact enforcement, as some suggest, fails because the temporal mismatch between when a model's risk profile becomes measurable and when harm materializes means intervention arrives too late. Once a model with hazardous capabilities is integrated into critical infrastructure or deployed to millions of users, the rollback cost is catastrophic and unrecoverable. Aviation safety certification did not permanently entrench a single aircraft manufacturer; instead, mandatory design verification established a baseline of public trust that allowed smaller innovators to build safely without risking systemic collapse. The same structural logic applies to artificial intelligence, where safety mandates act as civilizational firebreaks rather than corporate barriers.

Complex human systems require institutional boundaries to survive, and removing those boundaries systematically triggers structural breakdown. We spent centuries building the regulatory guardrails that keep financial markets, civil engineering, and aviation stable against unconstrained failure. On what authority do we claim that frontier artificial intelligence can bypass these exact mechanical precautions without inviting systemic collapse? True accountability requires naming the enforcement mechanism and demanding tangible proof of control before release, not hoping that market competition alone will correct a failure mode that cannot be undone.

Freezing frontier model development to prevent hypothetical rollback costs imposes a catastrophic human toll that dwarfs any speculative future harm. While risk theorists model phantom disasters, real people suffer from untreated cancers and prolonged scarcity that faster computation and automated drug discovery could eradicate today. The opportunity cost of every delayed compute cluster is measured in human lives cut short by institutional paralysis.
