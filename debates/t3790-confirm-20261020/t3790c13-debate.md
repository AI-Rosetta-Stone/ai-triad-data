## Topic

Under what conditions should federal courts and regulatory agencies establish negligence standards versus strict liability for foundation model developers deploying high-risk systems?

## Opening Statements

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Strict liability for foundation model developers destroys the evidence needed for functional law while denying society life-saving medical discoveries. Continuous software updates and weight loss make internal states unstable within 72 hours. This makes strict liability impossible to enforce. Congress enacted Section 230 to protect platform dynamism through similar mechanical realities. Dynamic software defies static liability frameworks. When courts enforce strict liability on systems whose internal states evaporate, they ban open-source distribution. They hand total market control to Microsoft, Google, and OpenAI. Who benefits from this regulatory moat? Only incumbent monopolists who afford compliance teams while open-source developers face criminal exposure for weights they no longer possess.

The Federal Trade Commission and federal courts must apply negligence standards with mandatory pre-deployment audits instead of strict liability. Negligence evaluates whether developers followed safety protocols, such as red-teaming benchmarks under NIST AI Risk Management Framework guidelines. It does not punish unpredictable emergent behaviors. This approach targets corporate recklessness while protecting innovation across 150,000 open-source repositories on Hugging Face. When critics argue that volatile system states prove developers lack visibility, they mistake rapid iteration for negligence. Dynamic versioning drives technological democratization, not a design flaw.

Every month of regulatory delay from strict liability carries a human toll in withheld medical abundance. The AlphaFold database predicted structures for over 200 million proteins. Yet strict liability proponents want to tie up medical discovery models in pre-market clearance delays past 36 months. The Department of Health and Human Services estimates that chronic diseases cost the U.S. economy $3.8 trillion annually. Autonomous biomedical accelerators could slash this burden in half if deployed at software speed. When regulators choose precautionary paralysis, they choose to let patients die of diseases we can cure today. We reject that fatal trade.

Applying negligence standards instead of strict liability protects the capability frontier. The FTC will use Section 5 unfairness authority to provide clear enforcement. A coalition of startup founders, open-source researchers, and patient groups supports this framework. They oppose legacy tech lobbyists who use strict liability to crush competitive pressure. Historical precedent from the 1996 Telecommunications Act proves that immunity from unworkable carrier liability sparked the internet economy. Foundation models need the same legal oxygen to cure cancer and automate labor drudgery. Congress and the judiciary must reject strict liability before regulatory paralysis locks in permanent technological stagnation.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

We spent centuries building the rules that keep society stable. Removing those limits now invites systemic collapse. The Federal Trade Commission and federal courts must apply strict liability to foundation model developers. Reactive negligence standards fail to capture unfixable algorithmic defects. Software engineering precedents show that advanced neural networks retain hidden failure modes that standard tests cannot find. When a model with billions of parameters shifts into new environments, its internal logic breaks down in ways no one can predict. Forcing victims to prove negligence treats catastrophic software failure like a common slip and fall. Upstream developers create these unverified tools, and they must bear the cost when deployment causes economic and social harm.

Reactive tort law adapts far too slowly to stop risky deployments. History shows a multi-decade gap between past industrial harms and judicial remedies. Today, autonomous deployment speed outpaces judicial systems by orders of magnitude. When an automated system causes widespread financial insolvency or infrastructure disruption, proving a developer breached a duty of care becomes an impossible wall. Strict liability solves this by placing the legal burden on the entity with the power to stop the deployment. This rule creates one clear path for claims. It stops injured parties from spending years fighting technical obscurity while developers externalize the true cost of failure.

Critics argue that strict liability will crush open-source innovation and drive research to areas with weak rules. That objection misreads the target of liability and the nature of high-risk foundational deployments. Strict liability applies to foundation model developers deploying high-risk systems, not to every static script or school code repository. By focusing liability on the actual deployment rather than the upstream code, regulators can protect open science while holding commercial deployers financially accountable for risks. If developers cannot guarantee the safety of a high-risk model, they must keep the off-switch and stop the release. Speed is not a virtue when the failure mode is permanent, and accountability must match the scale of the power.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Federal courts and the Federal Trade Commission do not need a strict liability rule for foundation models. Existing tort and consumer protection laws already cover software failures. Accelerationists argue that strict liability would kill the trial-and-error cycles needed for tech progress. This view ignores how companies bake legal risk into product costs. Safetyists counter that reactive tort law adapts too slowly to systemic risks. They want strict liability to force upstream safety work. That demand rests on an unproven guess. It assumes that legal liability classes can capture complex algorithmic failures without crushing smaller market rivals.

Model developers currently shift legal risk downstream using enterprise contracts. These deals protect the original builder from blame. When a firm uses a third-party model in a hiring tool or medical system, that firm takes the direct tort risk. Meanwhile, the developer keeps the profits. Federal regulators should target this contract risk-shifting directly. They do not need to debate whether negligence or strict liability governs the base model. The Federal Trade Commission used its Section 5 power against biased automated systems in the 2023 Rite Aid case. It did this without any strict liability rule for underlying software algorithms.

Regulators must focus on real harms like demographic bias and job loss instead of sci-fi fears of runaway AI. The National Institute of Standards and Technology found many cases of bias in facial recognition and automated systems through 2022 and 2023. These real harms happen in normal commercial deployments by real corporate actors who make specific design choices. Courts can test for negligence. They can check if developers followed set safety test rules before product launch.

Strict liability for foundation models creates a regulatory moat. It protects wealthy tech giants who can pay huge insurance bills and legal settlements. Small firms and open-source developers lack the cash to survive strict liability rules. This leaves the market in the hands of a few giant tech firms. A negligence standard plus mandatory transparency rules forces developers to act with care without killing competition. Congress and federal agencies must focus their enforcement work on real present harms. They must avoid speculative legal structures built by industry lobbyists.

### Safetyist (safetyist) → Accelerationist
\textcolor{NavyBlue}{\textit{Focus: Accelerationist's claim AN-3 regarding mandatory pre-deployment auditing under a negligence standard versus strict liability.}}


Mandatory pre-deployment audits operating under a traditional negligence standard fail because non-linear scaling laws render internal activation states unpredictable prior to widespread market exposure. When a foundation model developer tests a system with one billion parameters, the resulting behavioral envelope cannot be verified by static checklists administered during a standard compliance window. The National Institute of Standards and Technology documented numerous instances of disparate impact in deployed facial recognition and automated decision systems throughout 2022 and 2023, proving that even observable vision models escape pre-market detection. Relying on a negligence standard means courts must evaluate whether a developer exercised reasonable care, a test that collapses when the underlying mechanism of emergence operates entirely outside deterministic engineering controls.

Demanding negligence-based safe harbors while ignoring structural verification gaps assumes that software developers can anticipate failure modes they did not design. Complex neural networks acquire capabilities through massive dataset ingestion and gradient descent, producing emergent competencies that surprise the engineers who trained them. Under the European Union Artificial Intelligence Act, high-risk categorization triggers specific documentation duties, yet static paperwork cannot substitute for liability that attaches directly to unfixable architectural flaws. Treating unpredictable algorithmic behavior as ordinary corporate negligence misrepresents the technical reality of foundation models, where a single fine-tuning pass can entirely rewrite internal decision boundaries within seventy-two hours of deployment.

Strict liability forces commercial developers to internalize the irreducible risks they externalize onto downstream operators and the public. When an upstream creator releases model weights that later generate systemic economic or civil harms, asking a downstream hospital or financial institution to prove developer negligence creates an insurmountable evidentiary asymmetry. Tort law historically relies on strict product liability precisely where manufacturing complexity makes it impossible for consumers or secondary users to inspect latent defects before use. Preserving human control over high-risk deployments requires a legal boundary where developers bear financial responsibility for the unpredictable consequences of their scaled systems, replacing fragile pre-market checklists with enforceable accountability.

Meanwhile, downstream users harmed by biased screening tools or denied loans get no pay from distant upstream vendors who hide behind enterprise terms. Effective oversight requires breaking those contracts and checking real choices made by commercial deployers instead of waiting for slow legal changes to catch up to abstract fears.
