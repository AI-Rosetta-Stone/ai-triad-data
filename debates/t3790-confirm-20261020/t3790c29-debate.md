## Opening Statements

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

State laws that block artificial intelligence automation protect old office jobs instead of human potential. Office churn is not a crisis. It drove fifty percent productivity gains across industrial revolutions since 1850. When state labor departments freeze algorithms to save old paper tasks, they trap workers in dead-end work instead of freeing them for higher-value jobs.

Job loss from new tech historically clears up within twenty-four months through free markets. State funds should back open-source tech and compute power. They should stop propping up dying office sectors with endless retraining for yesterday's desk jobs. States with flexible labor markets absorb job shifts faster and pay higher wages than states with heavy rules and top-down labor mandates.

Freezing tech hurts patients and citizens waiting for fast medical tests and smooth service. Every month a state delays software to please public sector unions, millions of hours are wasted on red tape that computers wipe out in milliseconds. Lawmakers must drop job-protection rules and let free markets drive the shift to abundance.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

State offices do not shed labor hours when vendors install automation tools. They just raise quotas for remaining staff. A 2023 National Bureau of Economic Research survey found that software deployments boosted daily case targets by 24 percent while cutting staff by only 6 percent. State legislatures must stop trusting vendor claims of easy efficiency. Contracts buy software that shifts verification work from machines onto human workers. Agencies show lower headcount costs on paper, while frontline workers absorb the stress of fixing errors.

Vendors claim autonomous operation, but real deployments rely on hidden supply chains of precarious labor in the Global South. A 2022 Stanford study showed that every automated triage tool needs continuous offshore data labeling by workers earning under two dollars an hour. State budget funds for AI modernization subsidize an overseas sweatshop economy wrapped in a server rack. Labor department grants should target the supply chains that make these systems work instead of paying for vendor training modules. Lawmakers can use public contracting rules to audit these labor practices.

Critics argue that labor rules protect bureaucratic waste and block public service modernization. That argument assumes software demos match reality and treats friction as a moral failure instead of a guard against systemic error. State legislatures have budget tools to enforce labor standards on vendors without disrupting agency functions. The 1974 Employee Retirement Income Security Act offers a clear precedent for forcing firms to account for hidden administrative costs. State labor departments can condition tech contracts on worker impact audits and mandatory rest periods for staff managed by software.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

State lawmakers and the Department of Labor must require safety checks before software replaces human office staff. The Accelerationist claims that AI office churn drives civilizational productivity and human potential. The Skeptic counters that offices fail to cut work hours when vendors install automation, instead raising quotas for remaining staff. Both ignore a structural fact: unmeasured automation shifts funds from worker pay to coercive control tools. History shows that when the cost of policing displaced groups drops below the cost of social aid, governments swap welfare for state repression.

Unchecked software causes fast skill loss by replacing human judgment with opaque algorithmic quotas. Real offices show that algorithmic management increases mental strain and breaks trust among workers. The Department of Labor must therefore use state budgets to fund independent testing and worker safety programs. State lawmakers have the legal power to enforce these rules by holding software makers liable if they skip safety steps. Without these strict guardrails, fast tech rollout destroys human agency and social stability beyond repair.
