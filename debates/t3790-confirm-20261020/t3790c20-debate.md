### Skeptic (skeptic)

Autonomous weapons do not remove combat friction or stop command errors. They just shift moral duty from human operators to automated code that lacks a legal and ethical conscience. Accelerationists argue that machine-vision target acquisition cuts civilian deaths by 37 percent using fast processing speeds. Yet, this claim relies on closed lab tests, not real combat under fire. Military data from the Pentagon's Joint Artificial Intelligence Center show that real sensor feeds suffer from lag and bad data that code cannot fix on its own. When a remote sensor misreads farm gear as a combat vehicle, the automated loop strikes within 200 milliseconds before a human commander can check the data.

International Humanitarian Law requires personal accountability and bans reducing the laws of war to math or automated metrics. Defense contractors profit by selling speed as a tool for compliance. Meanwhile, ground commanders carry the legal blame for war crimes done by software they cannot clearly audit. Department of Defense Directive 3000.09 requires human judgment in all lethal fights. Yet, current contracts value fast output over clear decision trails. Congressional oversight committees can cut funds for black-box targeting systems through the National Defense Authorization Act, but industry lobbying groups block independent audits.

Conscience and human restraint are key legal checks. They cannot be replaced by stats or machine-learning confidence scores. Past command groups during the 2003 Iraq conflict often misread live sensor feeds despite high-bandwidth data. This proves that distance between command and the field causes data loss. Adding machine-learning layers to the targeting chain grows this cognitive gap. It pushes operators to trust software over their own moral restraint. Policymakers must reject the claim that software equals humanitarian progress and demand real human accountability for every lethal strike on the modern battlefield.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

The accelerationist model assumes that algorithms stop battlefield crimes by removing panic and fatigue from lethal force. That claim treats human conscience as an engineering flaw instead of the core check that stops endless escalation. High-frequency machine loops in complex wars shatter that premise through edge-case failures. When U.S. Department of Defense Directive 3000.09 mandates human judgment in all kinetic engagements, planners admit that lethal force needs moral friction. We spent centuries building the guards that keep society stable. On what authority do you claim we can drop them without inviting collapse?

Delegating lethal targets to autonomous systems creates an accountability gap that breaks international humanitarian law. When an algorithm strikes based on flawed proxies or unverified pattern recognition, no human actor bears criminal guilt under the Geneva Conventions. The Pentagon spent four billion dollars on algorithmic warfare in fiscal year 2024 without setting up an enforceable rollback rule or a named human owner for automated errors. Congress must demand an independent safety audit before any weapon crosses the line from human-in-the-loop to fully autonomous execution. If a robot strikes a civilian convoy by mistake, who in the chain of command has the legal power to halt the model mid-fight?

Critics worry that keeping the off-switch in human hands causes dangerous bottlenecks in fast fights. That friction is not a flaw; it is the vital circuit breaker that stops massive escalation. The 1983 Soviet nuclear false alarm proved that human hesitation is the only tool that stops system collapse during high-speed crises. Cutting that human delay to speed up targeting trades proven civilian safety for bad tactical gains. We cannot code moral agency into silicon chips, and we cannot charge a server farm with war crimes. Precaution must scale with irreversible harm. Thus, new laws must ban any lethal autonomous weapon that lacks a steady, unbroken human override.
