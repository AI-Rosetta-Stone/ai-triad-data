## Topic

Under what institutional conditions can the UN and national regulators enforce AI safety treaty obligations without stifling domestic innovation velocity? Specifically, how should verification protocols be structured to balance compliance burdens across frontier labs and open-source developers?

## Opening Statements

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Rigid United Nations AI safety treaties do not stop existential risk. They create bureaucratic tollbooths that crush open-source innovation and protect legacy tech monopolies.

When regulators set static rules for frontier models, they invite bad actors to game the system instead of building real security. Malicious groups easily train around fixed filters while legitimate open-source developers drown in legal costs.

This mirrors how past digital rules hurt small builders while helping giants like OpenAI and Google.

The human cost of this delay is clear. We see preventable deaths when medical breakthroughs stall, along with ongoing economic scarcity.

Precautionary rules treat every open model as a hazard. They ignore the huge cost of keeping intelligence away from hospitals and labs.

Decentralized market competition and peer review outpace and neutralize bad actors faster than top-down state checks ever can.

National regulators must stop central planning and let open science build abundance without asking permission from legacy incumbents.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

The Accelerationist argues that open-source AI and rapid capability growth create decentralized economic abundance that rigid treaties cannot stop. That point correctly identifies the strong market momentum behind frontier models, but it ignores the permanent nature of open-weight release. Once open-weight models leave a lab, local fine-tuning strips safety filters without leaving tracking data. This makes it physically impossible to recall models after they spread. National regulators and the United Nations must therefore enforce upstream deployment gates on frontier compute clusters above ten to the twenty-sixth power floating-point operations to stop unrecallable hazards.

Post-training safety methods offer no permanent security because gradient-based prompt optimization bypasses instruction-tuned and reinforcement-learning filters with very little compute. Known flaws in instruction-tuned systems prove that surface-level alignment fails under minor pressure, leaving users with unconstrained models. When regulators rely on paper compliance and voluntary self-reporting instead of mandatory pre-release hardware audits, they lose control over systems that can copy themselves and run high-consequence cyber attacks. The Department of Commerce already tracks high-end semiconductor supply chains through export controls. This shows that upstream compute checks are both possible and practical.

Critics argue that compliance costs will push frontier developers offshore and force open-source groups into hidden networks beyond treaty reach. That risk is real, but it mistakes a hard enforcement job for an excuse to drop containment, much like bank regulators who kept anti-money laundering rules despite offshore evasion. International agencies must link access to advanced lithography equipment to strict safety rules, aligning hardware supply chains with treaty terms. On what authority do we allow unverified model releases that permanently destroy our ability to halt, fix, or override a misaligned system?

### Skeptic (skeptic)

International safety treaties do not protect the public from algorithmic disaster. Instead, they act as regulatory capture tools that shield incumbent firms from open-source rivals. The Accelerationist correctly notes that rigid treaty checks create red tape that crushes small developers. At the same time, the Safetyist rightly shows that unchecked growth brings real security risks. Yet both views miss how compliance rules work in practice as economic protectionism.

Past cases in telecom and finance show that complex rules harm small innovators most. When the FCC required strict tests for early radio gear in the 1930s, established makers used the red tape to price out independent rivals. Modern AI policy repeats this trend via proposed compute caps and mandatory audits. These rules impose fixed costs that hit open-source developers hard while leaving tech giants untouched.

Claiming that central checks stop rogue actors ignores the money motives behind treaty design. Frontier labs lobby for strict global oversight because compliance builds high moats against open-source rivals. The enforcement relies on state licensing of high-end chip supply chains, which grants dominant firms state-backed monopolies. Policymakers must see that global treaties run by incumbent labs secure market share rather than public safety.

### Skeptic (skeptic) → Safetyist
\textcolor{NavyBlue}{\textit{Focus: Address AN-5 (upstream compute thresholds over 10^26 FLOPs) and explain how hardware-level gates avoid the regulatory capture risks you previously conceded apply to compliance paperwork.}}


Moving regulatory gates from software compliance paperwork to physical compute thresholds merely shifts the tollbooth from the legal department to the foundry. The Safetyist suggests that gating hardware at ten to the twenty-sixth power floating-point operations bypasses the capture risks that plague code audits. Yet restricting the distribution of advanced machinery under the EU AI Act or equivalent international frameworks does not eliminate corporate protectionism; it institutionalizes it by legally binding the physical supply chain to three vertically integrated firms. When the state writes a physical threshold that only a handful of corporate conglomerates can meet, the law does not prevent risk—it codifies a hardware monopoly under the banner of security.

Physical machinery is not immune to regulatory capture simply because silicon is harder to forge than code. The semiconductor manufacturing equipment market, dominated by lithography providers such as ASML, operates through rigid, highly concentrated chokepoints that are easily captured by incumbent lobbying. When national regulators condition access to extreme ultraviolet lithography on adherence to centralized safety protocols, they grant state-backed exclusivity to the exact entities that can afford compliance moats. This dynamic mirrors the consolidation observed in digital platform regulations, where initial fixed friction penalizes decentralized builders while consolidating market power in firms like OpenAI and Google.

Furthermore, a static compute ceiling ignores the continuous deflation of compute requirements driven by algorithmic efficiency gains. Techniques such as quantization, mixture-of-architecture designs, and synthetic data curation mean that frontier-level performance routinely migrates to lower compute scales each year. If a regulatory threshold is pinned to ten to the twenty-sixth power operations, labs will simply optimize their training methods to achieve equivalent capabilities at half the compute, rendering the physical gate obsolete while open-source developers remain legally locked out of the market. Enforcing safety through static hardware limits is a structural failure of imagination that mistakes a capital barrier for a security guarantee.
