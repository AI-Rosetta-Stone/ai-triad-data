## Topic

## Opening Statements

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Bureau of Industry and Security compute thresholds fail to stop unauthorized AI scaling. Instead, they build compliance moats that lock in hardware monopolies for a few domestic firms. Federal export rules use static training-compute cutoffs. Yet, multi-agent task distribution and distillation methods run well on commodity clusters that bypass these rules entirely. The Bureau of Industry and Security enforces these limits with export bans on advanced accelerators. However, cloud providers that own hardware simply absorb the regulatory cost, while smaller innovators face high compliance expenses. National security officials claim these barriers protect our borders. In reality, the main winner is domestic industrial concentration among firms like Microsoft and Amazon.

Data center capital spending shows that a few dominant corporations control the physical gear needed for frontier training. Smaller domestic developers cannot compete when regulatory friction and hardware shortages drive up the baseline cost of compute. This dynamic mirrors past dual-use technology controls. Export licensing rules successfully consolidated domestic industries, but they failed to stop foreign buyers from using grey markets. Policymakers must see that hardware caps act as domestic market protections, not as technical firewalls against proliferation.

Critics argue that flawed hardware controls are better than having no view into large cluster deployments. But static compute limits offer no operational sight into sub-frontier intrusion tools. When sub-frontier systems run on sub-threshold hardware and match performance through smart code, the Bureau of Industry and Security watches the storefront while actors skip the register. Congress should reject laws that protect incumbent monopolies in the name of national security. Instead, Congress must demand proof that compute thresholds actually slow down illicit scaling.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

We spent centuries building the rules that keep society stable. On what authority do you claim we can remove them without inviting collapse? The Skeptic argues that Bureau of Industry and Security compute thresholds only build compliance moats. These rules protect hardware monopolies while failing to stop open-source optimization. The Accelerationist insists that export controls use static training cutoffs. These cutoffs ignore how cheap hardware routes around bureaucratic gating. Both views spot a real problem: central rules risk capture and evasion by distributed actors. Yet that friction does not justify dropping structural oversight altogether. We must anchor our governance in real physical infrastructure rather than relying on unprovable promises about internal alignment.

Physical infrastructure metrics provide the clear audit trail required before any advanced model runs. Data center power purchase agreements and real-time power tracking offer direct, tamper-resistant views into cluster operations. When a facility crosses ten megawatts of dedicated power, the Bureau of Industry and Security can use the Export Control Reform Act to demand hardware-level telemetry. Electrical monitoring and physical logs leave traces that bad actors cannot easily hide. We cannot govern complex systems by trusting the voluntary promises of the people who profit from scaling them.

Oversight tools must work in real time to stop runaway runs before harm occurs. Automated reporting APIs and audit tools let regulators halt unverified training runs within minutes of detection. This model mirrors Federal Aviation Administration rules that ground planes when a flaw is found before disaster strikes. The Department of Commerce enforces export rules not to kill domestic business, but to keep humans able to override unaligned machine behavior. Speed without independent checks is not progress. It is a failure of civilizational care.
