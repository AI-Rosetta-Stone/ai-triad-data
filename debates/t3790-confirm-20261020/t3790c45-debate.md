## Topic

Under what enforcement conditions should national legislatures ratify international AI safety treaties, balancing the risk of regulatory capture against the imperative to constrain frontier model deployment?

## Opening Statements

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

International safety treaties act like protectionist cartels. Legacy tech firms design them to crush open-source rivals with high compliance costs. Large companies like Anthropic and OpenAI spent over forty million dollars lobbying Washington and Brussels last year. They wanted licensing boards that lock out smaller developers. When lawmakers ratify these treaties, they hand market dominance to three firms. Meanwhile, hospitals and research labs are starved of life-saving frontier models. Silicon Valley legal teams win this regulatory capture. Patients lose while medical AI deployment sits frozen in committee.

Static capability thresholds set by global bodies are easy for bad actors to game. They only cause bureaucratic friction for legitimate builders. Open-source networks bypass these central checkpoints hours after a release. Jailbreaks and prompt-injection updates move at software speed, not treaty speed. For instance, the Llama-3 parameter release proved that decentralized groups can audit and fine-tune models faster than any state licensing board can schedule its first hearing. National security agencies try to enforce static bans on code weights. They chase ghost code while real-world deployment solves cancer drug discovery and materials science.

Precautionary delay is not caution when it causes a lethal body count of missed medical cures and automated labor relief. Every month a legislature spends debating global harmonization protocols is a month cancer diagnostics and malaria drug discovery are held back by red tape. Decentralized tests find safety fixes through real-world use faster than central planning ever writes them down. Congress must reject international treaty rules that protect incumbent moats. Lawmakers should instead unleash permissionless open-source innovation.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

International AI safety treaties do not stop frontier model deployment. Instead, they force costs that lock in big firms while failing against decentralized open-source models. When nations plan global oversight, they repeat the 1996 Telecommunications Act. That law let dominant firms use rules to crush independent rivals. Big tech firms like Microsoft and Google spend millions lobbying for rules because mandatory audits and licensing boards block smaller developers. The public pays for this capture through less competition and entrenched monopolies, while regulators lack the staff to stop safety failures in closed systems.

Rules and central treaty enforcement fail completely against peer-to-peer sharing and local hardware. Open-source models live on sites like Hugging Face and run on standard graphics cards in homes, far outside corporate or national control. This makes border bans useless. Regulators cannot subpoena a decentralized swarm of independent nodes running open weights on local hardware. This failure at the edge proves top-down treaties are fictions that punish law-abiding firms while missing actual deployment vectors.

Critics argue that without central treaties, rogue actors and foreign foes will deploy dangerous frontier models. This objection assumes decentralization makes state rules toothless, which ignores how governments can control upstream compute bottlenecks like chip manufacturing. National legislatures can target the physical hardware needed for training runs without building global cartels that protect legacy monopolies. Past export controls prove that upstream hardware limits change market behavior much better than downstream safety treaties do.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

National legislatures must ratify binding international AI safety treaties. Unrecallable frontier models and weak post-training fixes create permanent risks that markets cannot stop. Accelerationists argue that these safety rules act as protectionist cartels. They claim legacy firms use high compliance costs to crush open-source rivals. Skeptics add that laws and treaties fail completely against peer-to-peer sharing networks and decentralized weights. But both groups miss a key fact. Once raw model weights are released into the wild, upstream control is gone forever.

History shows that controlling dual-use tech proliferation is very hard. Once a lab releases unrestricted weights that use more than ten to the twenty-sixth power training FLOPS, agencies cannot pull those files back from peer-to-peer networks. National legislatures must mandate safety gates through international treaties. They must prove a system is safe before frontier capabilities are final. This puts the burden of proof on the deployer to show verified safety before training ends.

Post-training safety steps fail because bad actors easily bypass guardrails with targeted fine-tuning and tests. Technical studies show that surface-level behavioral rules collapse when hit with cheap automated jailbreaks that cost under one hundred dollars in compute. Centralized national agencies must enforce strict limits during the R&D phase. This stops dangerous autonomous traits from forming inside labs before public release. Without binding global standards and mandatory compute audits, market pressure will force every major lab to drop safety baselines in a rush to deploy.

### Skeptic (skeptic) → Safetyist
\textcolor{NavyBlue}{\textit{Focus: Safetyist's claim that post-training measures and decentralized peer-to-peer weight distribution make upstream compute limits or international treaties impossible to enforce (AN-11)}}


Safetyist is right about one thing. Once model weights enter peer-to-peer networks, states cannot recall them by decree. But their conclusion collapses under its own logic. They claim nations must ratify sweeping international treaties to set up pre-deployment licensing boards. Yet if you cannot pull distributed weights from decentralized repositories, a centralized treaty cannot stop bad actors from running code locally. These static approval gates do not lower risk. They function as compliance cartels. Legacy tech firms spent over forty million dollars lobbying for those exact boards last year. Compliance costs bankrupt independent developers, while leaving decentralized weight repositories completely untouched.

Here is the steelman for Safetyist. They argue that unrecallable frontier capabilities pose such extreme risks that we need an imperfect international treaty system. They want a baseline of control before models are trained. The flaw here is that it confuses bureaucratic paperwork with physical security. A treaty that mandates audits creates a legal wall. Only rich corporations can clear that wall. This concentrates industry power without making open weights on local hardware any harder to get. The rules govern corporate boardrooms that want protection, not the decentralized networks that bypass them.

Effective oversight means shifting the point of intervention upstream to physical infrastructure. We should look at hardware instead of licensing boards or model outputs. Semiconductor supply chains depend on extreme ultraviolet lithography machines, which are machines built by a single maker in the Netherlands. Also, massive datacenters need gigawatt-scale power allocations tied to local electric grids. These hardware chokepoints resist the easy copying that makes digital weights unrecallable. Regulating physical compute inputs restricts concentrated capital without building bureaucratic moats against independent researchers.

### Safetyist (safetyist) → Skeptic
\textcolor{NavyBlue}{\textit{Focus: Skeptic has proposed shifting intervention upstream to physical infrastructure (AN-17) rather than downstream licensing boards; Safetyist must address whether upstream hardware controls can effectively govern decentralized open-source model replication.}}


Downstream model weights cannot be recalled once distributed through peer-to-peer networks, making software-level licensing boards a structural failure. Oversight must instead anchor to the physical chokepoints of frontier development, specifically extreme ultraviolet lithography machines and gigawatt-scale grid infrastructure. Under the framework of the Wassenaar Arrangement, national export controls already restrict physical manufacturing equipment precisely because heavy machinery resists illicit replication far better than digital files. By shifting the regulatory target from ephemeral model weights to physical hardware requirements like those administered by the Department of Commerce Bureau of Industry and Security, international treaties can govern internal R&D scope expansion without relying on unenforceable post-deployment censorship.

Hardware verification does not function as an incumbent protection cartel when thresholds are tied strictly to verified cluster size rather than arbitrary corporate status. A physical compute threshold set at ten to the twenty-sixth power training FLOPS targets only the handful of entities capable of frontier training runs while leaving ordinary academic and commercial laboratories unencumbered. This design deliberately avoids the compliance moats that critics rightly condemn, ensuring that smaller developers face zero regulatory friction during routine development. When physical infrastructure is monitored at the foundry and utility level, the resulting oversight regime anchors safety to verifiable physical reality rather than self-reported compliance promises.

Decentralized actors might attempt to evade these physical chokepoints by smuggling smuggled compute components or utilizing dark-datacenter configurations, but physical power generation leaves an unambiguous thermodynamic footprint. A gigawatt-scale training cluster requires dedicated substation connections and cooling infrastructure that cannot be concealed within residential or standard commercial real estate. International treaties mandating real-time grid telemetry for high-capacity installations provide an auditable mechanism to detect unsanctioned frontier runs before irreversible model capabilities emerge internally. Without binding physical accountability anchored to energy and hardware, any governance framework remains an exercise in paper bureaucracy.
