This delayed software security fixes for years while bad states ignored the rules. Global bodies will fail the same way on a planetary scale.

These bodies build moats. The rules protect rich tech giants from fast rivals. Who gains from a global license for frontier models? Legacy software firms that can pay for big legal teams in Washington and Munich. Who pays the cost? Sick people waiting for cancer screening AI and labs cut off from open weights.

How do these rules work? National export agencies choke off chips to stop rivals. This protects market share behind a mask of safety.

Decentralized markets fix flaws faster than panels write drafts. Telecom groups spent decades debating protocols. Meanwhile, open-source coders built the modern internet. The push for treaties brings together rich lobbyists and risk-averse bureaucrats. The pushback comes from open-source coders, hospitals, and nations that want tech freedom.

Fear without proof is just survival for big agencies. When leaders freeze new AI out of fear, they cause disease and slow the economy. The burden of proof falls on those who want bans. Real-time checks catch bugs faster than treaty drafts.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Complex technical systems need strict rules and safety checks before deployment to survive heavy scaling pressure. The Federal Aviation Administration sets airworthiness rules under Title 14 of the Code of Federal Regulations before any commercial plane carries a passenger. Frontier artificial intelligence labs now scale clusters past 100,000 specialized chips without equal safety rules or outside checks. Fierce market rivalry forces firms to skip alignment work to grab market share first. Global treaties must set up mandatory checks before any group trains models that use more than 10 to the 26th power integer operations.

Critics argue that global treaties will just protect big tech firms and push risky work into unregulated places. State-level enforcement already exists through chip export controls run by the United States Department of Commerce and allied nations. The Wassenaar Arrangement shows that governments can limit dual-use factory tools across borders when political will matches physical reality. National regulators must link access to extreme ultraviolet lithography machines directly to compliance with binding model tests. Treaties work not by trusting corporate self-control, but by controlling the physical chokepoints of hardware production.

Hardware safety rules offer the only check we can prove against a catastrophic loss of human control. Labs can hide software weights, but chip fabs stay concentrated in a few secure sites worldwide. Treaty check teams can inspect supply chains and demand physical kill switches on clusters that pass set compute limits. Nations spent decades building non-proliferation rules for nuclear materials to stop irreversible harm. Frontier AI systems with autonomous tools need that exact same setup of strict pre-deployment checks.

Regulators ignore real harms like hiring bias and job loss while chasing fake doom. Federal agencies found systemic wage drops and bias in automated tools last year without needing global treaties.
