## Topic

Under what conditions can the Bureau of Industry and Security effectively use compute thresholds and export controls to govern AI safety without driving open-source developers offshore?

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

We spent centuries building the guardrails that keep society stable. On what authority do you claim we can remove them without inviting collapse?

Unchecked artificial intelligence poses huge risks that make old ways of fixing problems useless. When labs train models that use more than 10 to the 26th integer operations per second, a single major security failure leaves no time to fix it. The Bureau of Industry and Security must set rules before labs launch models because society cannot recall a rogue intelligence once it gets out.

Physical hardware metrics give us the only clear way to hold groups legally accountable. Cloud providers and chip makers form central choke points that let federal regulators check training runs before launch. While this hardware oversight might push small developers offshore, this friction is a small price to pay to keep existential threats under human control.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

The Bureau of Industry and Security cannot govern artificial intelligence safety using static compute limits. The main risks come from accessible smaller tools and commercial cost cuts, not just massive scale. Safety models warn that risks grow past ten to the twenty-sixth power, saying a lack of early perimeters invites disaster. That warning captures a real fear about growth, but it treats scale as the only harm vector while ignoring how everyday actors build dangerous systems on normal hardware. Static limits miss smaller multi-agent cyber attacks that already run on standard clusters without huge training runs.

Corporate pressure systematically breaks safety checks long before models reach export limits. Companies facing launch pressure routinely cut safety steps to save money and beat rivals to market. Relying on export lines and voluntary rules ignores these real incentives, which degrade safety no matter what happens at the hardware frontier. When an agency checks a facility, it finds budget sheets that push speed over alignment work, not secret supercomputers.

Enforcing export controls on chips like H100 units creates a false sense of security. It pushes open-source developers overseas where basic hardware remains cheap and plentiful. The bureau lacks the power to track millions of graphics cards already set up in commercial data centers worldwide. Policymakers must base accountability on developer liability and corporate disclosures instead of chasing hardware lines across borders.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Safetyist wants the Bureau of Industry and Security to enforce strict hardware limits because frontier models over ten to the twenty-sixth power of compute bring permanent, catastrophic risks. Skeptic rightly notes that the same federal agency lacks the inspection power to track millions of decentralized graphics cards and cannot govern safety through static limits. Both sides accept a false baseline: that static export controls can trap software capability while algorithms grow at exponential rates. Their clash reveals a deeper flaw: they assume central planners can freeze compute without killing the decentralized innovation needed to cure human disease today.

Static hardware caps fail because algorithmic efficiency and decentralized open-weight diffusion bypass them entirely. Frontier models increasingly train on shared, rented servers across global farms, while software leaps cut training compute needs by orders of magnitude each year. The Bureau of Industry and Security enforces export rules at major chip foundries like TSMC, but it cannot police millions of consumer graphics cards already in the wild. When regulators lock down silicon, developers simply shift training runs to smart code shortcuts and peer-to-peer networks outside U.S. reach.

Restricting open-source growth starves decentralized tests and hands power to big tech monopolies. History shows that open access brings broad abundance, while central planning leads to stagnation and capture. The true cost of red tape is measured in uncured cancers and delayed medical cures that open-source models would otherwise speed up. Who gave federal agencies the right to trade human lives today for the false safety of a static compute cap?
