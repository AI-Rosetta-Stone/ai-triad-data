# Debate: AI governance frameworks for advanced AI systems

## Topic

Under what conditions should NIST and the FTC condition compliance on third-party model evaluations, balancing frontier AI labs' trade secrets against public safety oversight?

## Opening Statements

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Frontier AI labs protect commercial market share through hidden methods rather than technical need. Software vendors use licensing rules and trade-secret claims in court to block independent checks. This corporate secrecy leaves buyers and regulators unable to check real model safety before business use. Requiring third-party tests for business compliance breaks down this false information gap.

Horizontal oversight groups like the Federal Trade Commission face big knowledge gaps when they regulate complex software. Without their own engineering teams, these agencies risk approving vendor claims or helping dominant tech firms block rivals. Strong rule enforcement needs third-party audits backed by strict evidence rules, not voluntary corporate self-checks.

Critics argue that forced third-party tests leak trade secrets to rivals and foreign actors. However, standard commercial data rules and secure enclaves already protect sensitive intellectual property during oversight in sectors like drug making and finance. Frontier AI labs are software vendors, not special sovereign entities exempt from standard commercial checks.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Federal agencies must tie frontier artificial intelligence deployment to mandatory safety tests. These tests must override corporate trade secrecy claims. The National Institute of Standards and Technology and the Federal Trade Commission have the legal power under Section 5 of the FTC Act to demand independent third-party reviews. They can require these reviews before labs release systems that use more than 10 to the 26th power integer operations.

History shows this approach works. The Food and Drug Administration proves that trade secrets never let drug makers skip safety tests before public sales. Congress set this rule in the 1962 Kefauver-Harris Drug Amendments because private profit cannot outweigh public risk.

Critics argue that external reviews leak trade secrets and hurt American tech power against foreign rivals. This objection fails because unverified model failures cause permanent damage to financial systems and critical infrastructure. Trade secrecy is a business property right, not a shield against public danger. When a system crosses the frontier threshold, the maker must prove the system is safe before release.

Labs must share internal data with outside auditors. Hiding this data is a legal design flaw under Restatement Third of Torts Section 2(b) rules for black-box systems. Labs that refuse to share real-time data on model drift lose their legal shield when their systems fail. The Department of Justice and the FTC can punish unmonitored rollouts with court orders and fines over 1 billion dollars. Public safety needs named actors, real liability, and strict rules instead of voluntary corporate trust.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Federal pre-market rules are not safe. They are paywalls built by big firms to stop new ideas and halt human progress. Safetyist and Skeptic note that frontier models pose hard checks, but they confuse red tape with risk reduction. When agencies like NIST tie approval to outside audits, they create high costs that price small builders out of the market. Past rules show that pre-market checks crush small teams while big firms just pay the audit fee.

Enforcement lies with the FTC and NIST, backed by fines that small startups cannot survive. Compute scaling benchmarks offer clear capability checks without hurting market growth or handing trade secrets to bureaucrats. Scaling laws show that compute inputs track skill gains better than outside audits. Critics say that skipping behavioral checks leaves blind spots for new risks, but compute limits set a clear line without giving regulators a veto.

The group backing open weights and hardware links small labs against big tech firms that want legal moats. Congress passed the Defense Production Act to fix supply bottlenecks, not to give tech monopolies a permanent veto over open code.

Each month of delay in using advanced tools harms people waiting for medical breakthroughs and cures. The cost of this cautious gatekeeping falls on patients and small developers who pay for central stagnation. Policymakers must drop outside audits and use clear compute reports to keep markets open and capability growing.

Refusing to cure disease just because a bureaucrat fears a rare edge case is a moral failure, not safe design.

### Skeptic (skeptic) → Safetyist
\textcolor{NavyBlue}{\textit{Focus: Safetyist's claim (AN-3) that withholding diagnostic telemetry constitutes an actionable design defect under Restatement Third of Torts Section 2(b)}}


Stretching Restatement Third of Torts Section 2(b) to treat withheld diagnostic telemetry as an automatic design defect misreads how product liability handles complex digital systems. Courts require a specific, identifiable physical or functional flaw, not an abstract failure to hand over internal model states that current civil procedure cannot easily subpoena from proprietary servers. For example, federal product litigation regarding algorithmic discrimination in healthcare algorithms has repeatedly stalled because plaintiffs lack the pre-discovery access needed to prove causation under standard tort rules.

Clumsy, one-size-fits-all pre-market certification regimes function as regulatory moats that entrench incumbent labs while crushing smaller developers who cannot afford compliance audits. A mandatory certification hurdle set by agencies like NIST creates compliance costs exceeding $5 million per model iteration, pricing out academic labs and open-source developers entirely. Independent auditing must instead be performance-gated and tiered proportional to systemic-harm potential, using pre-registered evaluation metrics that prevent large firms from capturing the compliance apparatus.

Corporate trade secrecy cannot serve as a blanket shield for ambient surveillance and biased deployment while developers escape structural accountability. When enterprise buyers in sectors like immigration enforcement deploy models without provenance documentation, no amount of voluntary ethics guidelines replaces statutory discovery rules that pierce corporate nondisclosure agreements. How can developers currently hiding documented present harms behind trade-secret protections be trusted to self-certify their safety without legally binding, performance-gated independent audits?
