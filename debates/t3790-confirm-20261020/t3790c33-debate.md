## Topic

Should AI developers be legally liable for harms caused by their deployed models?

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

AI developers must face strict legal liability for deployed models. Withholding diagnostic telemetry creates design defects that hide hidden behavioral drift. When foundational creators release unconstrained models into markets, they push fatal systemic risks onto downstream buyers who lack the tech to catch hidden hazards. Historical rules like the Federal Aviation Administration's 1958 certification mandate prove that complex systems need binding structural boundaries to stop crashes. Without an enforceable rule for liability, commercial actors keep private gains while the public takes unrecoverable systemic harms.

Product liability rules under Restatement Third of Torts Section 2(b) show that withholding diagnostic telemetry hooks makes software integration defective. Upstream developers who refuse to build systemic observability into their systems intentionally blind downstream users to emergent model failures. This design choice leaves hospitals, banks, and cities flying blind against unpredictable algorithmic shifts that break basic safety baselines. Regulators must enforce mandatory diagnostic access through statutory tort frameworks to make sure corporate actors keep the power and the burden to stop failing models.

Critics argue that strict liability rules create high compliance barriers that build market power for large tech firms and crush open-weight developers who cannot pay for insurance pools. But policymakers can fix this tension by tying statutory liability protections to standard pre-release evaluation gates and cryptographic provenance metadata. Compliant developers who meet checkable safety baselines skip punitive damages, while reckless actors face heavy fines run by the Federal Trade Commission. This targeted framework protects safe innovation while taking away the legal shield that currently pushes reckless deployment.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Safetyists argue that holding AI developers strictly liable for behavioral drift will force firms to build strong safety guardrails into their deployed models. That view captures a key truth: software shipped without maintenance pathways usually breaks in production. Yet that logic fails when plaintiffs cannot access the technical artifacts needed to prove causation in court. Software developers do not escape liability because tort law lacks teeth. They evade it because trade secrecy and strict licenses lock plaintiffs out of the evidence. When a hospital buys a diagnostic neural network under a secret end-user license agreement, injured patients cannot subpoena the training weights or the loss function. Without a legal right to inspect model weights during discovery, strict liability proposals are weak gestures that protect tech giants.

Proposed liability rules rely on weak compliance that shields companies while leaving real harms unaddressed. Federal agencies and legal teams route compliance through internal ethics boards and voluntary checks instead of court-enforceable mandates. Meanwhile, marginalized communities bear the costs of algorithmic bias in automated hiring, housing, and credit scoring. Federal enforcement data from the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission show that automated screening tools displace qualified job applicants in thousands of cases without triggering civil fines. Liability rules that do not mandate public evidence production and independent system audits simply protect firms that launch opaque software at scale.

Critics argue that mandatory discovery rules and transparency requirements compromise core trade secrets and hurt developers facing foreign rivals. That risk is manageable through judicial protective orders and confidentiality tiers used in patent litigation for decades. The Federal Rules of Civil Procedure already handle proprietary source code safely without exposing corporate intellectual property to the public market. Policymakers must reject speculative rules and focus enforcement on mandatory evidence production in civil discovery. If Congress passes developer liability without giving plaintiffs access to model weights and training logs, the law will enrich defense lawyers while leaving victims without help.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Safetyists argue that holding AI developers strictly liable for drift in model behavior will force firms to build strong safety guards into deployed models. That argument takes a real concern—unpredicted software failures—and ties to a fix that kills open competition. When courts place uncapped blame on upstream code, small developer teams vanish overnight. Only tech giants with huge bank accounts can survive that legal risk. Strict liability does not protect people; it locks in tech monopolies by choking off open-source work.

Open-source systems move faster than closed legal teams because thousands of engineers check code right away. Looking at how open models are released shows that community checks catch hidden flaws faster than closed reviews. When Meta released Llama 3 in 2024, independent coders patched security holes within two days. Centralized firms hide their errors behind walls until a disaster forces them to talk. Open releases use group brainpower to secure software better than any court-ordered fix ever could.

Fans of tort reform assume that open-weight developers cannot check downstream harms, but that ignores how liability forces teams to lock down access. Congress must reject strict liability rules that treat software weights like toxic waste. The Department of Justice antitrust division should instead treat broad developer blame as a tool for regulatory capture. If lawmakers want safer software, they must build open markets where clear facts drive quality, not lawsuits that freeze human progress.

### Skeptic (skeptic) → Safetyist
\textcolor{NavyBlue}{\textit{Focus: Safetyist's reliance on FAA certification precedents (AN-2) and conditional safe harbors (AN-10) without addressing Skeptic's prior challenge regarding trade secrecy and restrictive licensing locking plaintiffs out of discovery (AN-5)}}


Comparing the Federal Aviation Administration airworthiness certification established in 1958 to software deployment treats a physical manufactured artifact like a trade-secret neural network. Physical airworthiness standards rely on public engineering schematics, destructive stress testing, and government-inspected material science that anyone can subpoena in court, whereas commercial AI developers lock their model weights behind restrictive end-user license agreements and trade secrecy protections that bar independent inspection. When regulators attempt to replace litigation risk with statutory safe harbors tied to private pre-release audits, they do not create stable institutional stewardship; they construct a procedural wall that shields vendors from discovery.

Under federal civil procedure, injured plaintiffs cannot pierce these corporate trade-secrecy walls without source code and training data access, leaving victims of automated screening bias—such as the thousands of job applicants rejected by algorithmic tools documented in recent Equal Employment Opportunity Commission enforcement data—without evidentiary footing to prove causation. A safe harbor conditioned on certified audits conducted behind closed doors simply privatizes oversight, substituting confidential vendor checklists for the public evidentiary standard required by courts. This institutional capture replaces judicial accountability with performative compliance, leaving injured parties legally locked out while corporations retain absolute control over the data needed to prove their own negligence.

Proposing that certified pre-release evaluation gates substitute for civil liability ignores the reality of how technical opacity functions during civil discovery in federal district courts. When a plaintiff files a discrimination suit against an enterprise deploying an opaque model, the vendor invokes trade secret protection to withhold training data provenance and architectural weights, effectively neutralizing standard tort discovery rules. Without mandatory public evidence production and independent system discoverability, any statutory safe harbor merely converts corporate non-disclosure into a legal shield against liability. Lawmakers cannot graft 1958 manufacturing oversight onto software systems unless they strip away the non-disclosure clauses and reverse-engineering prohibitions that currently prevent courts from inspecting how these systems actually operate.
