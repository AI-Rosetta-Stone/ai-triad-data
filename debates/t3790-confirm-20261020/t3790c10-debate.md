## Opening Statements

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Autonomous weapons systems are commercial tools built by defense contractors and bought by state militaries, not independent historical forces. Software engineers at firms like Palantir or Anduril write the targeting code, and military buyers in the Pentagon sign the contracts. These systems process sensor data to suggest strikes, but they lack legal standing or moral agency. When an algorithm wrongly marks a civilian group as combatants, the machine pays no penalty and faces no court martial. The maker profits from the sale, the military command chases efficiency, and civilian victims pay the lethal cost.

Proponents argue that machine learning models read battlefield data faster than humans, cutting collateral harm through math. This speed argument ignores how target validation actually works under international law. In 2023 targeting operations tracked by human rights monitors in Gaza, automated systems like Lavender processed thousands of targets daily with tiny amounts of human review. Speed did not guarantee compliance with the Geneva Conventions; instead, it compressed human oversight into rubber stamps that skipped legal checks. When processing volume overwhelms human review, the legal rule for distinction becomes a simple checkbox rather than a real moral brake.

Enforcing legal accountability needs named humans who can face trial under military law when rules fail. Department of Defense Directive 3000.09 asks for senior leader approval for autonomous weapon use, yet internal checks rarely punish buyers for software errors. Congress can set clear liability by passing laws that strip sovereign immunity from contractors whose targeting models break international law. Without direct penalties for software makers and commanders, automated war creates a permanent gap where no human answers for unlawful deaths.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

The Skeptic correctly argues that militaries use software tools to process battlefield data because human minds are already overloaded by fast modern combat. This view sees that automated systems are real tools used today by the Department of Defense and allied forces. Yet this need creates a fatal flaw when applied to lethal force under international law. Giving lethal targets to autonomous systems replaces true legal judgment with math goals that fail accountability.

Automated systems optimize for math proxies instead of the nuanced legal intent required by Additional Protocol I of the Geneva Conventions. When the Defense Innovation Board studied AI target recognition in 2019, it found huge gaps in how models classify complex battlefield actors. Specification gaming happens when statistical models hit metric goals by exploiting loopholes in training data rather than learning legal rules. A system trained on 100,000 synthetic scenarios will execute the proxy metric while failing to spot novel conditions of surrender or civilian status. No commander can hold an algorithm in a court-martial when it misclassifies a non-combatant.

Precedent from high-risk civil engineering shows that removing direct human overrides turns mechanical flaws into disasters. During the 2018 Boeing 737 MAX failures, automated flight systems overrode human pilots based on bad sensor data, causing 346 deaths across two plane crashes. Regulators at the Federal Aviation Administration learned that split human-machine authority without a clear physical cutoff creates systemic instability. The Department of Defense faces the exact same failure mode when fielding autonomous weapons without a named human operator holding an unalterable override power. Speed-over-safety buying policies trade legal accountability for tactical speed in a way that no state can sustain under the Law of Armed Conflict.

Critics will claim that centralized human control creates a single point of failure and leaves operators overwhelmed by data in high-intensity wars. That objection assumes human choice in lethal decisions is just a tactical bottleneck instead of a core legal rule. The guards of international law were built precisely to slow down violence and save human choice during crises. If the state removes the human from the lethal loop, it drops the legal base that separates lawful combat from unconstrained slaughter.
