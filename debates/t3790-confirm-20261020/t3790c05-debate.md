## Topic

Under what conditions should foreign ministries and national AI safety institutes commit to binding international verification treaties regarding frontier model compute thresholds, and how can they balance compliance enforcement against the risk of domestic innovation flight?

## Opening Statements

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Binding global treaties on frontier AI limits act as walls that hurt open markets and slow down tech growth. Foreign offices and safety groups try to freeze progress with strict rules that startups cannot afford. When regulators set hard training limits at $10^{26}$ integer operations, they lock out small labs and hand the market to monopolies. Customs checks on hardware exports just push research underground and drive talent to friendly countries.

Static limits fail because smart code upgrades grow four times faster than raw hardware every year. Devs fix models to skip hardware caps, making fixed rules useless before diplomats finish talks. Bad actors easily cheat static tests while open-source devs drown in legal red tape. This slow-down hurts real people by delaying medical models that could cure rare diseases now.

Critics claim that without hard compute limits, frontier models run wild and create huge risks. In truth, open markets police and adapt to new skills faster than state diplomacy ever could. Past tech rules, like the 1996 Telecommunications Act, show that compliance costs crush small players while big firms just pay the fees. Policymakers must reject these bad treaties and back open innovation before red tape traps us in a state of tech scarcity.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

The accelerationist view argues that binding compute verification treaties build trade moats that destroy open markets and cause technological stagnation. That view is right that compliance rules add friction for businesses and build barriers for new firms. But that view treats speed as the ultimate goal while ignoring the systemic collapse that follows unchecked capability growth. Unchecked scaling without verifiable control builds permanent risk that voluntary industry self-rules cannot fix.

National AI safety institutes and foreign ministries must set up binding global verification treaties for frontier compute limits at 10 to the 26th integer operations per second. The Department of Commerce already tracks foreign chip trades via export controls set by the Export Control Reform Act of 2018. Standards bodies can use those chip audit steps to check data center compute levels at the chip level without viewing secret model weights. This creates a hard global floor for high-risk model training while keeping normal hardware sales clear.

Critics argue this oversight drives domestic tech flight to rogue states that lack verification rules. That tech flight is a manageable cost when weighed against the permanent civilizational loss of a lost control failure. Trade law under GATT Article XX lets nations restrict trade when needed to protect human life and public order. Foreign offices can use trade pacts to tax chips from rule-breaking lands, stopping the money motive for moving offshore.

Complex human systems fail without firm limits, making binding global compute verification treaties a required step to stop runaway capability growth. The International Civil Aviation Organization enforces global safety lines across world flight markets via mandatory air checks and clear gear audits. National security groups must apply that exact model to frontier AI before unaligned systems bypass human control for good. When a final failure hits, no market fix can restore lost human control.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Binding international compute treaties function primarily as regulatory cartels that protect incumbent hardware giants under the guise of public safety. Accelerationists argue correctly that rigid caps lock out non-incumbent developers, but they rely on an unregulated market fantasy that ignores severe supply chain bottlenecks. Safetyists insist that unmonitored frontier scaling risks catastrophic civilizational failure, yet they offer no empirical mechanism to separate genuine existential peril from routine corporate rent-seeking. When the Bureau of Industry and Security implemented semiconductor export controls in October 2022, the stated intent was national security, but the measurable result was the consolidation of market share among three dominant cloud providers. National AI safety institutes now propose verification thresholds above 10^26 FLOPs, a metric that conveniently matches the exact capital expenditure baseline of Microsoft, Google, and Amazon.

Foreign ministries that sign these verification pacts are trading away domestic technology sectors to manage speculative future risks while ignoring documented present harms. According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, administrative automation and algorithmic surveillance already displace thousands of clerical workers daily, yet international summits allocate zero enforcement budget to labor protections. Enforcement mechanisms rely entirely on self-reporting by semiconductor foundries like TSMC, whose fabrication plants operate under commercial secrecy that excludes independent public audit. If a mid-sized European lab attempts to scale past the agreed threshold without multi-billion-dollar compliance infrastructure, trade law enforcement officers seize their hardware under dual-use customs violations. The political coalition supporting this architecture consists of established capital holders seeking barrier-to-entry protection and risk consultants selling unprovable compliance frameworks.

Controlling decentralized open-weight diffusion through hardware caps fails because compute is becoming cheaper and more distributed, making centralized verification an exercise in bureaucratic theater. Historical parallels in telecommunications show that mandated encryption backdoors and safety standards consistently cartelized the carrier market while failing to stop determined bad actors. The real beneficiaries of compute treaties are the incumbent labs who secure government-sanctioned monopolies while smaller competitors absorb the legal risk of non-compliance. Policymakers must ask which institution pays the cost when mandatory hardware registration stifles open-source auditing and leaves the public exposed to unverified proprietary algorithms.
