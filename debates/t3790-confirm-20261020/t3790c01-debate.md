## Topic

Under what conditions should the US AI Safety Institute mandate pre-deployment safety evaluations for frontier AI labs, and how can regulators balance catastrophic risk mitigation against the competitiveness of open-source developers?

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

The United States AI Safety Institute must mandate safety checks before companies deploy any frontier model trained on more than ten to the twenty-sixth power FLOPs. Complex human systems need clear rules to survive. Removing those rules leads to collapse. We spent centuries building the guardrails that keep society stable. Why do you claim we can remove them safely?

Critics argue that mandatory reviews for open-source developers will hurt domestic tech growth and hand leadership to foreign rivals. That objection ignores the risk of permanent harm. Aviation regulators did not exempt experimental planes from safety checks just because garage builders worked faster than big firms. When a model can copy itself or design new pathogens, a late fix fails because the code already sits on millions of unmonitored servers.

Internal lab tests involving self-improvement bypass public API monitoring unless regulators check checkpoints beforehand. Private developers test new skills inside closed labs that the public cannot see. Federal rules under the Defense Production Act offer a clear way to run technical audits before models cross deployment limits. The National Institute of Standards and Technology uses similar technical baselines for cryptography without killing software markets.

Regulators must enforce safety gates because human control over advanced systems is non-negotiable. If labs release unverified models that escape human oversight, the cost is not a minor bug but the permanent loss of our ability to fix problems. We do not let drug companies skip clinical trials for new pathogens just to speed up sales. Frontier AI development needs the exact same proof of safety before release.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

The Safetyist argues that federal regulators must mandate pre-deployment safety evaluations at a strict threshold of ten to the twenty-sixth power FLOPs to prevent uncontainable catastrophic risks. That argument correctly prioritizes institutional oversight, but it relies on an unverified premise that static capability gates can control complex sociotechnical systems without cementing the market power of incumbent tech firms. Rigid compute thresholds function as regulatory capture that protects dominant labs from nimble open-source competition while failing to address measured, everyday algorithmic harms.

Historical compliance precedents in telecommunications and finance show that high fixed regulatory costs always crush small developers and concentrate industry control in a handful of corporate hands. When the Federal Communications Commission imposed strict technical certification rules on early radio equipment in the nineteen-twenties, independent makers vanished while large networks consolidated their market share. The United States AI Safety Institute risks repeating this exact historical error if it enforces binary pass-fail audits that treat frontier models as autonomous entities rather than software products built by specific corporate actors. Regulators must ask who profits from these compliance gates, who pays the fixed costs, and which corporate actor answers when a deployed system causes concrete harm.

Both safetyist pre-deployment gating and accelerationist market competition ignore documented, present-day algorithmic harms that do not wait for future existential thresholds to materialize. Federal agencies already document widespread discrimination in pretrial risk assessment tools and automated labor displacement across logistics and healthcare sectors that current compliance frameworks omit entirely. Waiting for hypothetical catastrophic events while ignoring observable bias and labor abuses leaves vulnerable populations unprotected by design. Effective oversight requires performance-gated independent audits that measure actual deployment effects rather than speculative capability ceilings.
