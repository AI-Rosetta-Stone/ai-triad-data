## Topic

Under what negligence standards should federal courts hold frontier foundation model labs legally liable for downstream deployment harms, and how might these liability thresholds alter pre-deployment safety auditing across the tech industry?

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Federal courts must hold frontier foundation model labs strictly liable for downstream deployment harms unless those labs complete rigorous, independently audited safety cases before launch. When labs deploy unaligned systems with billions of parameters, they force the public to bear catastrophic risks while keeping total control over the model weights. Tort law has fixed similar power gaps in aviation and civil engineering for over a century by using strict upstream liability. Without mandatory pre-deployment certification modeled after the Food and Drug Administration drug review process, society suffers permanent harms that lawsuits filed after the fact cannot fix.

Critics argue that mandatory safety rules create compliance costs that lock in big monopolies and crush open-source developers. This objection misunderstands the cost of keeping civilization safe. Compliance costs are not market barriers; they are the required entry fee for handling permanent risks. Regulators can also scale audit rules to match model compute sizes, such as the 10^26 FLOP training runs set in executive orders, and exempt smaller models entirely. Big labs capture markets when they write their own rules, not when independent courts enforce strict liability for unverified code.

Independent auditors have the tech skills and legal power to check frontier model safety before launch if they have statutory subpoena power. The National Institute of Standards and Technology AI Safety Institute already provides a tech baseline to test model flaws before commercial release. When federal judges tie safe harbor protections to passed safety audits, labs face a clear money incentive to test their systems well. Making this enforceable requires named corporate officers to sign safety sign-offs under threat of criminal perjury, which shifts blame from corporate press releases to federal courtrooms.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Safetyists demand strict liability and strict pre-deployment gates to stop rare risks before they happen. That view treats every new tool as a permanent threat rather than a chance for human freedom. Federal courts must reject mandatory safety audits and strict legal liability because these rules act as bottlenecks. They protect big tech monopolies while stopping human cures.

Congress set 15 to 30 month grant delays at agencies like the National Institutes of Health. This proves that central planning causes red tape instead of real safety. When federal rules force strict compliance on frontier labs, they build legal walls that crush open-source rivals. Small teams lack the big legal budgets needed to survive these audits. Big tech firms lobby for these exact rules because they price smaller rivals out of the market.

Federal judges enforce this system with court orders and fines based on vague legal duties, backed by the Department of Justice. The Food and Drug Administration drug approval process sets a clear model here. Its multi-year testing gates delay life-saving therapies while adding billions in compliance costs.

Decentralized open-source work beats central planning in finding practical answers to hard human problems. Thousands of independent coders test frontier models in real time. They find bugs faster than any closed safety board working behind doors. The cost of delay is real, measured in millions of lives lost each year to diseases that AI medical research could cure.

When leaders mandate pre-deployment gates, they trade real human growth for the illusion of control. Lawmakers should scrap rules that help giant firms and build open zones instead. In these zones, open testing can find and fix harms fast.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Frontier AI labs use narrow safety audits as a shield to clean up their image while selling software with known flaws. The Safetyist side wants strict court liability and mandatory safety cases to stop big harms. The Accelerationist side argues these same gates are red tape that protects monopoly profits. Both sides miss the real point. They treat AI as a magic force instead of normal software sold by real companies. In 2024, firms like OpenAI and Anthropic made billions while using self-tests that hid job loss and bias. Courts do not need new theories of machine blame. They need standard tort rules applied to firms that push raw code into high-risk settings.

Scare stories about end-times risk push aside real fixes for current job loss and civil rights abuses. When leaders chase fake rogue AI scenarios, they skip the real harms tracked by the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission and labor economists. Workers in logistics and customer service pay real costs today while labs hide behind complex code supply chains. The Federal Trade Commission already has the power under Section 5 to punish unfair business tricks without waiting for new AI laws. Blaming labs for downstream harms does not mean predicting machine behavior. It means enforcing basic product safety rules against firms that profit from deployment.

History shows this pattern in telecom and medical devices. Big firms used complex rules to take over boards and dodge civil suits. When Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act passed in 1996, it gave tech platforms a shield to dodge blame for years. Adding heavy pre-deployment safety cases to foundation models repeats that exact mistake by letting labs write their own rules. True accountability means piercing corporate shields when deployment hurts workers, patients, or consumers. Congress should ignore both the panic of the safetyists and the free-market gospel of the accelerationists. Lawmakers should focus on strict fines for corporate actors who fail to test their commercial products.

Federal Trade Commission authority under Section 5 handles unfair or deceptive commercial practices. Yet, applying a consumer protection statute to frontier AI systems commits a fatal category error. A federal agency designed to police misleading ads cannot issue safety injunctions before a model launches. A model's core failure modes involve autonomous capability scaling rather than commercial fraud. When an unaligned model crosses the deployment threshold, no post-hoc penalty can reverse an irreversible safety breach.

Reactive enforcement operates like an autopsy report delivered after a bridge collapses. It records the exact failure mechanism long after prevention is mathematically impossible.

Mandatory pre-deployment safety cases do not act as gatekeeping tools designed to crush open-source work. This is true if compliance costs scale properly with training compute thresholds. Setting oversight triggers at concrete computational boundaries, such as $10^{26}$ FLOPs, exempts grassroots developers. It imposes strict accountability only where unconstrained capabilities present major hazards.

Civil engineering precedent shows that load-bearing safety codes do not eliminate small firms. Clear engineering standards create stable markets by removing the fear of catastrophic structural failure. When labs face strict liability for downstream harms without an audited safety case, rational actors pay for their own reckless speed. This approach avoids centralized micro-management of open research.

Refusing upstream liability leaves society exposed to irreversible harms under the illusion that fines can replace physical limits. Complex human systems need hard limits to survive. Removing those limits for deployment speed invites institutional collapse.

If lawmakers mistake consumer protection paperwork for structural containment, a governance vacuum will follow. The chaotic spread of unalignable technical systems will fill that vacuum instead of democratic debate.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist) → Safetyist

A ten-to-the-twenty-sixth-power FLOP threshold does not shield grassroots innovators; it draws a legal fence around cloud oligopolies by pricing garage-scale competitors out of the compute market before a single model is even trained. When regulators enshrine a static computational floor into federal statute, they hand legacy incumbents a perpetual compliance moat that protects their market share from disruptive challengers under the banner of safety. This arithmetic of exclusion guarantees that only trillion-dollar balance sheets can afford the legal teams and audit infrastructure required to clear central gates, turning an arbitrary benchmark into an anti-competitive cartel mechanism. The claim that such a threshold protects small developers ignores how fixed regulatory compliance costs scale inversely with firm size, crushing independent researchers while leaving enterprise monopolies untouched.

Decentralized protein folding pipelines and open-source medical informatics achieve safety not through preemptive bureaucratic paralysis, but through continuous, high-speed empirical verification across thousands of independent nodes. When thousands of researchers simultaneously stress-test model weights in the wild, bugs and failure modes are identified and patched within hours, whereas a centralized safety board takes months simply to schedule an initial hearing. Dismissing this decentralized error correction as a post-mortem autopsy report misrepresents how real-time telemetry and open code repositories actually operate in software engineering. Real safety emerges from the rapid mutation and selection of code in competitive markets, not from the stagnant decrees of appointed officials reviewing static safety cases.

Every month of delay engineered by pre-deployment gating boards carries a body count measured in untreated cancers, chronic diseases left uncured, and human potential locked behind regulatory tollbooths. When public health agencies take up to thirty months just to clear emergency research grants, adding another layer of mandatory pre-market approval to AI foundations guarantees that life-saving diagnostics remain trapped in administrative limbo. Deciding to freeze deployment out of abstract fear is not caution; it is an active moral choice to deny immediate technological relief to suffering populations. The historical record demonstrates that decentralized experimentation cures diseases faster than central planning ever prevents errors, making the human cost of regulatory delay the ultimate indictment of safetyist gatekeeping.

A model trained below that power limit can still hurt marginalized communities just as much as a giant frontier model. Building a legal safe harbor around smaller models leaves victims with no way to sue companies that profit from automated bias.

Congress passed Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act to stop unfair business practices without waiting for sci-fi dangers to happen. Federal regulators already have the legal power to punish companies for launching harmful systems today. Relying on pre-launch review boards creates a fake safety shield that protects labs from lawsuits while ignoring ongoing civil rights violations under Title VII.
