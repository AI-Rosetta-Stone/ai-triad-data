## Opening Statements

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Giants like OpenAI and Google lobby the Bureau of Industry and Security to build high compliance walls. This shuts out open-source rivals under the name of national security. History shows this pattern in the 1996 Telecommunications Act, where red tape helped old telecom giants and hurt new market entrants. Big firms like these barriers because closed systems protect monopoly prices while small researchers pay the cost. Policymakers must see that export limits do not stop spread; they just hand market power to a few corporate boardrooms.

Plans to restrict public model weights chase speculative spread risks while ignoring real harms from deployed systems. Federal agencies ignore proven bias in jobs, housing, and health care that hurt groups today under Title VII and the Fair Housing Act. Big developers ship models with known demographic bias because safety checks focus on abstract doom over real civil rights violations. The Bureau of Industry and Security tracks fake bio-threat risks while refusing to check live models that deny workers health insurance or housing loans. This skewed focus guards corporate profits while failing citizens who bear the costs of algorithmic failure.

Critics claim that releasing frontier model weights helps bad actors make dangerous bio or chemical threats, so export limits are a needed baseline. That fear relies on fake models of harm and ignores how bad actors actually get dangerous materials through physical supply chains, not open-weight downloads. The rules use vague compute limits, like 10 to the 26th power floating-point operations, which the Department of Commerce cannot check across cloud networks. The political group backing these limits joins old defense contractors seeking grants with monopoly tech firms trying to crush open-source rivals. Congress should reject this false safety trade-off and focus oversight on checking the real bias of live systems.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

The Skeptic argues that export controls protect big firms from open-source rivals. The Accelerationist insists that public checks catch bugs faster than closed boards. Both claims fail for autonomous tools that cannot be recalled once shared. Unfree weights make a lasting risk that our systems cannot handle. The Bureau of Industry and Security saw this exact failure in dual-use bio materials during the 2001 anthrax crisis, where shared germs beat containment.

Old tort laws fail for smart frontier systems because the reasonable person rule cannot judge software bugs. Legal scholars note that common law needs a human actor and a fix after the fact. Neither exists when an open-weighted model runs self-made code across millions of unmonitored devices. When the Department of Commerce made export rules for fast chips in October 2023, regulators saw that once weights cross borders, human check drops to zero. That loss of control is not a small rule glitch. It is a permanent structural break.

Strict safety tests must check all model weight releases that pass set compute limits. The National Institute of Standards and Technology must enforce pre-launch tests before any group releases weights above 10 to the 26th training FLOPs. A team of security agencies and outside auditors will back this plan. Big firms wanting cheap labor will fight any block to fast sharing. Without checks backed by federal law, open sharing turns every user into a proxy for disaster.

### Resolution Analysis

- **Whether export controls and safety regulations function as protective moats for incumbent tech firms or necessary guardrails against biological and chemical threats.** — Undecidable
  - *The debate weighs two distinct, high-impact risks that rely on different value systems. Skeptics point to historical precedent where compliance costs harmed market entrants, while safety advocates warn that common law tort systems cannot repair catastrophic biological proliferation. Because both risks involve high-consequence outcomes that are difficult to measure in advance, neither side holds a decisive empirical advantage.*
  - Would change if: Longitudinal market studies tracking startup survival rates under varying export control regimes alongside empirical measures of model-enabled harm would tip the balance.
- **Whether training compute thresholds serve as reliable, neutral technical boundaries or arbitrary administrative compliance floors.** — Stronger: C3 (specificity)
  - *Scaling laws offer a quantitative metric linking compute to capabilities, giving regulators a concrete threshold to enforce. Skeptics correctly note that these thresholds create high compliance costs, but they provide fewer specific metrics to replace compute-based boundaries.*
  - Would change if: Evidence that hardware-based compute limits fail to correlate with capability jumps in real-world deployments would weaken the reliance on compute thresholds.
- **Whether open-source model sharing accelerates defensive security research through peer review faster than centralized audits catch dangerous fine-tuning vulnerabilities.** — Stronger: C7 (implementation specificity)
  - *Upstream semiconductor controls offer a verifiable enforcement mechanism at physical chokepoints in the supply chain, whereas downstream peer review relies on decentralized actors who cannot easily patch distributed weights once released. Safety advocates demonstrate a clearer path to enforcement through hardware tracking than accelerationists do through open distribution.*
  - Would change if: Proof that decentralized peer review consistently finds and patches zero-day vulnerabilities in model weights before malicious actors exploit them would strengthen the open-source case.
