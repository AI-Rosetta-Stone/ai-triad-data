# Debate: AI governance frameworks for advanced AI systems

## Topic

Under what conditions should the FTC and NIST mandate model registration versus post-market liability to govern advanced AI systems, balancing innovation incentives against safety risks?

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Post-market liability fails when applied to frontier AI models. Tort law works for cars or drugs because agencies can recall defective goods after a failure. When the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration investigates a brake failure, the wreckage stays in one place. Advanced AI models work differently. Their failures copy across millions of computers before a lawsuit starts. Waiting for harm to occur before fixing blame is like waiting for a dam to break to buy concrete.

Mandatory pre-market registration is the only way to stop systemic collapse. Agencies like the Food and Drug Administration and the Federal Aviation Administration use pre-market rules because lawsuits cannot bring back dead passengers. The National Institute of Standards and Technology must force labs to register model weights, training data, and compute levels over ten to the power of twenty-six integer operations before launch. This registry gives regulators names to hold accountable so they can check safety controls before code runs at a massive scale.

Critics say registration costs too much and hurts small open-source developers while helping tech giants. This ignores the cost of a failure where one unaligned model breaks financial systems or biodefense networks. A registration rule set only at the frontier protects small developers while forcing big labs to prove humans control their systems. When the Federal Communications Commission licenses radio spectrum or the Environmental Protection Agency tracks chemical precursors, society accepts gatekeeping as the price of survival.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

The debate over AI rules is stuck on a false choice. People argue about pre-market registration versus post-market liability. Both models ignore who actually holds the evidence.

Safety advocates say pre-market licenses set clear accountability before deployment. Yet this view assumes that static reviews can pierce the trade secrets firms use to guard training data and model weights. When the FTC checked commercial algorithms in 2023, corporate teams used intellectual property laws to block independent audits. Pre-market paperwork cannot fix an information gap that private vendors maintain to protect their market value.

Traditional lawsuits fail for the exact same structural reason. Plaintiffs hurt by algorithmic failures cannot pierce corporate data walls without forced discovery rules built for complex software. Under current federal rules, injured people must prove that a specific model output caused direct harm. Tech firms claim that random model behavior is just unpredictable, not poorly designed. Without laws requiring firms to log decision steps, victims have no trail of proof to show in court.

Real rules require a shift from static registries to mandatory evidence storage and shifted burdens when safety incidents occur. Congress should pass laws like FDA post-market surveillance rules. These laws would force developers to save full run logs for five years after launch. If a running system causes a proven safety failure, the legal burden must shift to the vendor. The vendor must then prove their training data and safety filters met standard levels. This plan avoids red tape while breaking the corporate secrecy that hides how firms launch risky tech.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

The Safetyist correctly notes that digital system failures move at network speed. Traditional lawsuits are too slow to stop this harm. Yet their fix of central pre-market registration by the Federal Trade Commission and the National Institute of Standards and Technology treats human innovation as a disease. When agencies build multi-year approval blocks, they do not remove risk. They shift risk from quick market fixes to static state failure. In the 1970s, the Nuclear Regulatory Commission added years of licensing delay. This stopped new plants, locked in fossil fuels for decades, and killed thousands from pollution while chasing zero risk.

The Skeptic rightly asks for execution trail retention like Food and Drug Administration drug rules. This rule forces builders to log every system shift for five years. But using drug rules on open software ignores how open-source code grows through peer networks. When Linux builders patch a flaw across ten thousand servers in forty-eight hours, they use fast, open action. They do not wait five years for an audit from a Washington office with a two-year backlog. Forcing static registries gives total market power to three legacy firms in Silicon Valley who can pay compliance lawyers, while starving every startup.

Post-market liability plus real-time anomaly detection solves the replication problem without choking human growth. The Department of Justice and the Federal Trade Commission would run this plan using civil fines of up to ten percent of global revenue for unmitigated systemic harms. Builders face huge money risks the moment a live system causes real harm. This model moves accountability from asking permission to driving results. It matches business goals with public safety without forcing bureaucrats to guess which code matters. Who wins when a mandatory registration rule forces nine months of paperwork before a medical AI can save a single stroke patient?

### Safetyist (safetyist) → Accelerationist
\textcolor{NavyBlue}{\textit{Focus: Accelerationist's claim AN-11 regarding 1970s NRC licensing delays and their relevance to modern AI registration thresholds}}


Comparing the pre-market registration threshold of ten to the power of twenty-six integer operations to the 1970s Nuclear Regulatory Commission licensing delays treats physical capital construction as identical to digital weight replication. Building a concrete containment vessel requires geographically bound steel and decades of pours, whereas copying model weights across ten thousand cluster nodes takes less than forty seconds over standard fiber-optic lines. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission added years of friction because physical concrete cannot be recalled after a meltdown, but digital weights replicate instantly before any court can docket a complaint or issue an injunction. 

Post-market civil penalties of up to ten percent of global annual revenue fail as a deterrent because they rely on post-incident solvency and retroactive tort litigation. When a model with autonomous execution capabilities deploys malicious code or manipulates critical grid parameters across twenty million devices simultaneously, the resulting civil liabilities exceed the total global capitalization of the developing entity. Once financial damages surpass corporate solvency, penalty structures cease to function as behavioral guardrails. Civil lawsuits cannot restore an economy-wide network failure any more than a retroactive fine can re-sequence corrupted genetic databases.

Human control requires pre-deployment verification precisely because algorithmic execution velocity permanently outpaces the speed of judicial proceedings. The National Institute of Standards and Technology framework establishes a verifiable threshold that targets only frontier scale compute while leaving smaller open-source laboratories untouched. Requiring a pre-deployment safety case before execution at civilizational scale is not administrative paralysis; it is the fundamental boundary condition required to preserve institutional authority over autonomous systems.

### Skeptic (skeptic) → Safetyist
\textcolor{NavyBlue}{\textit{Focus: Safetyist has not responded to AN-5 regarding whether frontier-scale compute thresholds (10^26 integer operations) successfully isolate high-risk systems while protecting small open-source developers from prohibitive compliance burdens.}}


Static compute thresholds, like ten to the power of twenty-six integer operations, do not protect small developers. Instead, they build compliance barriers that help the largest corporate labs control the market. When the Federal Trade Commission investigated commercial algorithms, corporate legal teams used intellectual property rules to block independent audits. A fixed floating-point floor just makes this unfairness permanent. Dominant firms help write the testing rules they can easily afford, while pricing smaller rivals out of the market. This paperwork causes regulatory capture instead of technical safety.

Pre-deployment safety cases also fail because internal model architectures change constantly after registration. The physical and computing needs of updating complex systems create a delay. This delay stops fast, centralized oversight once deployment begins. A safety case sent to the National Institute of Standards and Technology only describes a code snapshot. That code disappears the moment engineers push an update or tune weights on private servers. Treating this static paperwork as a limit for civilizational risk mistakes corporate self-reporting for proof of safety.

Mandatory log-keeping and burden-shifting offer a structural alternative to upfront regulatory gates and weak lawsuits. Instead of judging future harms through upfront paperwork, regulators should require developers to save complete execution logs for five years after deployment using SEC-style rules. If an algorithm causes real harm, the vendor who profits from the deployment must prove how it made its decisions. This rule breaks through trade secrecy without forcing regulators to guess which compute threshold will matter tomorrow.

Safetyist insists that instantaneous weight replication outpaces judicial remedies and breaks corporate solvency, yet this view ignores how dynamic risk-conditioned capital requirements and real-time telemetry-conditioned insurance pools absorb systemic variance far better than brittle paperwork. Requiring a safety case submitted to the National Institute of Standards and Technology describes a snapshot of code that ceases to exist the moment engineers push an update or fine-tune weights on proprietary infrastructure.

Decentralized iteration velocity shatters the claim that pre-market registration is necessary for civilizational safety. Open-source ecosystems distribute error correction across millions of developers who patch vulnerabilities at network speed, achieving a lower overhead for error correction than any centralized bureaucratic review board can manage. While administrative mandates spend years debating compliance frameworks, decentralized open-source projects deploy thousands of medical and logistical optimizations that save lives every single day. When you choose to freeze development out of hypothetical fear, you are explicitly choosing to let people die today of diseases we could automate a cure for. Who gave any regulatory agency the moral authority to trade real human lives for phantom security?

Dynamic risk-conditioned capital reserves paired with mandatory telemetry transparency resolve the solvency problem without requiring top-down administrative paralysis. Under the EU AI Act framework, high-stakes deployments can be governed through live telemetry verification and post-deployment accountability rather than ex-ante bureaucratic permissioning. Insurance pools funded by dynamic risk assessments scale capitalization floors directly to observed operational variance, ensuring restitution without locking in incumbent market dominance. Real-time telemetry-conditioned compliance mechanisms handle systemic variance far better than static pre-market gates. Stop pretending that administrative stagnation equals safety.
