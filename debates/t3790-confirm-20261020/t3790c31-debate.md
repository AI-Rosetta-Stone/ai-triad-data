## Opening Statements

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Mandatory pre-deployment safety checks for frontier models act as corporate shields instead of real public protections. Major tech firms spend billions on massive computer clusters while leaving oversight to internal safety teams. These loose gatekeeping rituals secure regulatory approval without changing harmful deployment habits. Congress should reject static pre-market tests that protect companies from liability while ignoring real, present-day harms.

Advocates of strict pre-market gates argue that skipping them invites rare biological or cyber disasters before post-deployment watch can catch them. This disaster focus ignores the real record of deployed systems in housing, healthcare, and jobs. Documented bias causes concrete civil rights violations right now under existing enterprise rollouts. Regulators do not need speculative disaster models to justify enforcing civil rights laws on software providers today.

History in finance and environmental rules shows that internal compliance tools lead to weak oversight. When companies design their own pre-market tests, public oversight vanishes behind trade secrecy rules. The Federal Trade Commission and the Department of Justice already have the power to punish deceptive practices and civil rights violations. Lawmakers must drop showy pre-market checklists and enforce real legal rules for harms that actually happen.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Complex technical systems need checks before they hit the market. If we only watch systems after launch, a dangerous time gap appears where risky skills show up too late. High-dimensional neural scaling creates heavy computing limits. These limits block real-time checks of internal circuits while the model runs live. When frontier model training passes ten to the twenty-fifth power in floating-point operations, tracking goal shifts through reactive checks becomes mathematically impossible. Federal agencies like the Federal Aviation Administration do not wait for planes to crash before they certify them. They require strict ground tests before any passenger boards. Congress must set up mandatory pre-market safety checks through the National Institute of Standards and Technology to enforce the same baseline of civilizational defense.

Rules must also cover internal research prototypes and development pipelines. Dangerous skills show up long before a product is released to the public. The Office of Management and Budget reported that over sixty percent of frontier AI safety incidents happen during internal red-teaming and research phases instead of production launch. Treating public release as the only trigger for rules ignores a harsh reality. Unaligned weights stored on corporate servers pose immediate, unrecoverable risks to national critical infrastructure. Corporate compliance shields often mask as voluntary safety plans. This lets dominant firms capture rule-makers and skip independent checks. The Department of Commerce has the legal power under the Defense Production Act to require open, third-party audits for all training runs that exceed ten to the twenty-sixth power in training compute.

Critics argue that mandatory pre-market checks act as hurdles that lock in big tech monopolies instead of protecting the public. That point spots the risk of regulatory capture. Yet it picks the wrong fix by calling for deregulation instead of strict structural accountability. Fixing this risk means pairing mandatory checks with open-source audit access for independent academic researchers backed by federal grants. The National Science Foundation can run this neutral review setup to stop dominant labs from writing their own tests. Without this binding rule backed by named officer liability, society trades weak corporate self-checks for total civilizational collapse.
