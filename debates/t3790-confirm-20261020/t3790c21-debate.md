## Topic

Under what conditions should the AI Safety Institute mandate pre-deployment evaluations for frontier labs like OpenAI and Anthropic without stifling open-source innovation?

## Opening Statements

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Mandatory pre-deployment checks for frontier labs like OpenAI and Anthropic act as regulatory moats. They protect profits for big firms while pricing open-source developers out of the market. Federal lawmakers crafting these rules repeat the past mistakes of the 1999 Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act and drug compliance systems. Fixed certification costs create high entry barriers that shield dominant firms from competition. Large corporate legal teams easily absorb compliance work, but small developer groups cannot pay for third-party audits or liability insurance. The Department of Commerce and the National Institute of Standards and Technology enforce these rules, which builds a corporate compliance oligopoly in the name of public safety.

Frontier safety debates ignore current, proven harms to focus on speculative future risks. Federal civil rights regulators have proved cases of algorithmic discrimination in housing, job markets, and credit scores using Title VII and the Fair Housing Act. These real job and loan rejections hurt actual workers today, yet safety advocates demand focus on hypothetical autonomous weapon scenarios. Congress spends hours on sci-fi risk models while federal agencies lack staff to prosecute basic automated bias in hiring tools used by Fortune 500 companies.

Good oversight needs performance-gated independent audits paid for by public funds, not pre-deployment gates controlled by incumbents. The Federal Communications Commission model for spectrum use offers a working precedent for public management of common infrastructure without corporate capture. Lawmakers must reject proposals that give OpenAI and Anthropic veto power over open-source release rules through proprietary safety benchmarks. Public groups should tie deployment rights to post-market impact audits, shifting liability to the firms that profit when measurable harms happen.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Frontier AI labs like OpenAI and Anthropic must pass safety tests before launching models that use over $10^{26}$ integer operations. Complex systems need hard rules to survive permanent risks.

Critics argue these rules protect big tech profits and price open-source developers out of the market. That point spots the risk of agency capture, but picks the wrong fix. We should not drop all rules. Instead, we tie safety tests to compute floors above $10^{26}$ integer operations. This frees over 99 percent of open-source projects from pre-market rules.

The Food and Drug Administration set this pattern in 1962 with the Kefauver-Harris Drug Amendments. It demanded proof of safety before drug sales in high-risk fields. Congress should give the AI Safety Institute the same power to audit models that can copy themselves or launch new cyber attacks. These checks need outside red teams to test model weights before public release. If firms skip these checks, the agency must have the power to halt work at once and fine or jail top executives.

Giving this power to one federal office brings risks of political bias or big tech protection. To block that threat, the law must permanently exempt any training run using under $10^{25}$ integer operations from these rules. This two-tier system lets small firms and labs build new tools without red tape, while top labs face strict rules. Speed is not a virtue when failure brings an end we cannot fix.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Mandatory safety checks for frontier models act as regulatory walls that crush open-source work while trading clear risks for human suffering. The safetyist view notes that big capability jumps cause friction, but its fix uses top-down gatekeeping that legally harms small teams. When the National Institute of Standards and Technology builds rules that demand millions in legal costs, giants like OpenAI and Anthropic pay easily while garage developers fold. The main block to human progress is bureaucratic delay, not open models. Slow release cycles delay medical cures and labor help without giving real safety gains. Congress must reject pre-market rules that guard corporate profits under the name of caution. 

Upstream compliance rules create heavy costs that destroy small firms and non-profit groups compared to rich giants. The Federal Trade Commission and state attorneys general already have enough power under Section 5 to fight bad acts and real harms after a model goes live. When rule-makers demand pre-market tests for models over compute limits like ten to the twenty-fifth integer operations, they build a permanent club of approved labs. This licensing system mirrors the 1970s Civil Aeronautics Board, which kept airfares high by blocking new entrants until deregulation sparked a boom. The political group backing these tests includes incumbent labs seeking control alongside risk-averse bureaucrats expanding their power. The other side brings together open-source groups, startups, and civil rights groups who know that free innovation drives tech access. 

Real-world data shows that runtime checks and post-launch accountability give better safety results than central administrative freezes. The software world proved this during the open-source Linux shift, where group peer review found flaws faster than closed corporate boards. When the AI Safety Institute delays open-weight releases through mandatory audits, it blocks life-saving finds in drug discovery and materials science. Each month of bureaucratic delay leaves diseases uncured and slows growth for millions of patients who need fast tech spread. Policymakers must drop ex-ante bureaucratic blocks and use strict liability for clear harms while keeping open access for the global developer community.

Measuring training runs is not a self-executing physical meter; it requires compliance documentation, third-party attestations, and legal counsel to prove sub-threshold status to federal auditors. Congress can fund these independent audits through a dedicated assessment on commercial deployment revenue, mirroring the user-fee structures used by the Food and Drug Administration. This approach ties regulatory oversight to observable harms without erecting anticompetitive barriers that protect dominant firms from open-source competition.
