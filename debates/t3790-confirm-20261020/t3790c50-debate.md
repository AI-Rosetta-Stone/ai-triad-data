## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

We spent centuries building the rules that keep society stable. Using autonomous weapons removes those limits and invites collapse. The Department of Defense faces an enforcement gap when algorithms make lethal targeting choices within milliseconds at machine speed. History shows that automated loops bypass human controls within 300 milliseconds, as seen in the May 2010 financial flash crash. Congress must require hard kill switches and independent technical audits before any contractor deploys autonomous targeting tools.

Opponents argue that slow human command causes battlefield deaths. However, trading moral agency for statistical math violates Geneva rules on distinction and proportionality. Current machine learning cannot reliably encode moral reasoning in complex city fights, such as those seen in 2023 Gaza operations. Putting off-switch power inside military buying commands risks single points of failure without clear rollback plans. Lawmakers should pass strict liability laws that hold prime contractors directly responsible for algorithmic battlefield failures.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Safetyists argue that strict civil liability laws should hold prime contractors directly accountable for algorithmic battlefield failures because automated loops bypass human windows within 300 milliseconds. This view correctly prioritizes the horror of kinetic error. Yet it treats delay as a safe default rather than a choice that keeps troops at risk. When officers stall autonomous systems to study edge cases, rivals deploy fast networks today.

Defense Innovation Unit contracting records show that agile software deployment beats rigid procurement cycles every single time. Private vendors try to force ethical vetoes beyond international humanitarian law into government software contracts. Congress must ban this practice in procurement rules. Taxpayers funded these algorithmic tools to protect service members, not to build corporate safety theater.

Post-deployment telemetry liability swaps upfront paralysis for real-time checks. Cyber insurance models already price risk through live data streams instead of upfront bans. Defense contractors should face mandatory fines when telemetry shows they broke the laws of armed conflict. This system punishes actual battlefield misuse while letting engineering teams fix errors under real-world pressure.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Autonomous weapons are not new breakthroughs that rewrite the laws of war. They are commercial goods bought under standard Department of Defense rules. Defense firms like Palantir and Anduril market these systems as fast software loops to win billions in public funds. This lets them push legal blame onto troops on the ground. The United Nations Group of Governmental Experts spent years debating abstract ethics. Meanwhile, prime contractors wrote intent rules straight into federal supply deals. These legal shields ensure that when an AI targeting system fails, victims cannot prove the specific human mental state needed to sue the vendor.

Safetyists argue that strict civil liability must apply to defense developers when autonomous loops cause civilian deaths. That point correctly spots the moral hazard of corporate tech immunity. But it ignores how procurement law actually works under the Federal Acquisition Regulation. Contractors do not shoulder blame for software errors because federal contracts shield developers from third-party harm. The Department of Defense buys software with built-in liability waivers. This shifts the financial and criminal cost of failure onto military operators and civilians. Until Congress repeals these legal waivers, holding developers accountable in civil court remains impossible.

Accelerationists claim that rapid software updates make strict procurement rules obsolete. They say we need fast deployment to keep a strategic edge. That argument rightly values tech speed. But it uses speed as an excuse to dodge basic rules under international humanitarian law. When software firms ship unverified targeting models in the name of agile updates, they push the risk of lethal error onto people in war zones. The math here is simple. Software vendors profit from fast launches, taxpayers fund the research, and frontline troops face war crime charges before military tribunals. No tech novelty changes the basic rule that legal blame rests with the human institutions holding the budget.
