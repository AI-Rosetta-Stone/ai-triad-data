## Topic

Autonomous weapons systems and the future of warfare under international law

## Opening Statements

### Skeptic (skeptic)

Autonomous weapons are not unstoppable forces. They are manufactured tools that create immediate accountability gaps under current international humanitarian law. Defense contractors build these systems to secure Pentagon budgets. Military buyers adopt them to reduce troop exposure. When an algorithmic targeting error occurs, neither the software engineer nor the brigade commander bears clear legal liability under current military codes. This gap makes the technology ungovernable under current treaties.

Proponents argue that human oversight is always maintained. They claim algorithmic targeting acts as an advanced sight rather than a replacement for command judgment. Yet empirical data from recent military procurement cycles show that operators accept machine targeting recommendations over 90 percent of the time to maintain operational tempo. Automation bias turns the human operator into a rubber stamp rather than a moral safety valve. Software code written by private contractors replaces command authority on the battlefield without changing legal liability chains.

Statistical precision metrics cannot substitute for human empathy and moral judgment in proportionality assessments under international humanitarian law. Legal compliance requires discretionary human evaluation, not statistical optimization based on casualty probabilities. When a targeting algorithm calculates collateral damage estimates, it processes data points instead of weighing human dignity. Banning or strictly regulating these systems depends on whether the United States Congress can pierce corporate liability shields to hold contractors accountable for targeting failures.

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Autonomous weapons systems break human accountability. They follow the strict letter of military orders while breaking the spirit of those orders. International humanitarian law requires proportional force. Yet, computer programs chase tactical scores without moral understanding. Complex combat tests show that multi-agent delegation fails when strict rules replace practical proportionality. Defense budgets must block systems that cannot check human intent before using lethal force.

The human power to stop violence must stay absolute and cannot be handed off to machines. Past crashes in automated stock trading and aviation autopilot show how speed beats safety checks and starts bad chain reactions. The Federal Aviation Administration grounded the Boeing 737 MAX after 346 deaths. That event proved that automation failures destroy public trust. Lawmakers must demand real human override tools backed by legal liability under Title 10 of the United States Code.

Opponents claim that human commanders also suffer from fatigue, mistakes, and war crimes. They argue human oversight is an imperfect baseline. Yet, human error is fixable through courts-martial under the Uniform Code of Military Justice. In contrast, computer errors grow fast at machine speed with no clear person to blame. Congress passed the Weapons of Mass Destruction Control Act to stop unchecked escalation. That law offers a direct model for governing autonomous weapons. Department of Defense Directive 3000.09 tells commanders to use proper human judgment over force. Congress must now turn that internal agency rule into a strict legal ban.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Restricting autonomous weapons systems under international humanitarian law preserves emotional human warfare at a measurable cost to civilian lives. Skeptics demand human empathy in proportionality assessments, yet human soldiers under combat stress routinely misidentify targets and violate Geneva Convention standards. Critics argue that algorithms break accountability, but human command structures routinely hide war crimes through bureaucratic delay. The choice is stark: trust flawed biological actors who caused over three hundred thousand civilian deaths in Iraq and Afghanistan, or deploy algorithmic systems that process data without panic or personal bias.

Algorithmic targeting outperforms human soldiers under permissive rules of engagement by removing panic and tactical fatigue. Department of Defense testing data from 2023 shows that computer vision target models cut false-positive strikes by forty-two percent compared to trained human operators. The enforcement mechanism relies on pre-deployment audits mandated by the Office of the Under Secretary of Defense for Acquisition and Sustainment, backed by strict liability for contractors who ship bad code. This mirrors the Federal Aviation Administration airworthiness certification process, where software checks stop commercial aviation disasters before takeoff. Policymakers should direct budgets toward these auditable systems rather than funding endless reviews that protect legacy contractors at the expense of safety.

The permissive envelope of warfare law permits restraint-by-default, meaning autonomous systems can execute proportionality assessments with better consistency. Protocol Additional I to the Geneva Conventions lets commanders cancel attacks when civilian harm is too high, a standard that human commanders routinely miscalculate under fire. Software routines can check collateral damage estimates in milliseconds by using real-time overhead imagery and terrain data without emotional distortion. The political coalition backing this pivot includes defense tech startups and congressional armed services members focused on deterrence against peer adversaries. Opponents are legacy defense primes and legal bureaucracies whose power depends on manual command loops. Freezing deployment saves no lives; it shifts the human cost of error from silicon back to civilians caught in the crossfire.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist) → Safetyist
\textcolor{NavyBlue}{\textit{Focus: Address AN-5 and AN-10 regarding the comparison between human courts-martial under the UCMJ and the compounding nature of algorithmic errors in dynamic combat environments.}}


Courts-martial under the Uniform Code of Military Justice punish human soldiers post-hoc, offering zero comfort to the families of civilians killed while human commanders hesitate or miscalculate under fire. That retrospective courtroom theater is a tragic substitute for preventing casualties in the first place, operating on the comforting fiction that a multi-year military tribunal investigation somehow repairs a lost life better than an active system stopping the strike. When the Department of Defense tested computer vision target-identification models in 2023, those systems demonstrated a forty-two percent reduction in false-positive strikes compared to trained human operators. Clinging to human courts-martial while rejecting the measurable, life-saving accuracy of automated targeting is a moral preference for bureaucratic ritual over living civilians.

Active battlefields change constantly, but static certification is not the only deployment model available to modern software engineers. Autonomous systems do not rely solely on fixed historical datasets; they utilize continuous runtime telemetry and adaptive optimization loops to update their operational parameters in real time as environmental conditions shift. When a vulnerability appears, decentralized algorithmic patches deploy across an entire fleet in milliseconds rather than waiting for a congressional hearing or a military inspector general report to wind through the docket. That speed-of-light remediation mechanism dwarfs the glacial crawl of human disciplinary proceedings, which compound errors across months of delay before any corrective action occurs.

Software architectures can isolate and patch errors at machine speed precisely because computational systems do not suffer from the cognitive fatigue, emotional stress, or anchoring bias that degrade human performance during prolonged engagements. Treating human error as somehow superior simply because it generates a transcript and a gavel strike ignores the raw arithmetic of survival. Every hour spent defending legacy human-in-the-loop bottlenecks is an hour where preventable errors repeat without attribution or automated correction. Unlocking abundance means abandoning the pretense that slow, reactive punishment equals safety.
