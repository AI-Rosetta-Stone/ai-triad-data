## Topic

Under what liability standards should commercial foundation model developers and enterprise deployers be held legally accountable for downstream harms, and how can such rules incentivize pre-deployment risk mitigation without stifling open-source innovation?

## Opening Statements

### Safetyist (safetyist)

Commercial foundation model deployment without verified safety checks and enforceable recalls dumps irreversible harms onto society. Federal regulators must condition any public release of frontier AI on rigorous safety audits run by oversight bodies. Unregulated open-weight sharing permanently cuts off human control, creating unrecallable copies that break standard liability rules.

Complex human systems need boundaries to survive, and removing those boundaries causes collapse. Other high-risk fields like aviation and civil engineering require strict safety checks and outside audits before public use. The Federal Aviation Administration requires airworthiness certification before any commercial plane carries passengers. Foundation model makers skip this standard when they ship unaligned weights to public sites without a rollback plan.

Technical analysis of peer-to-peer sharing networks shows that public weight releases bypass physical choke points and corporate liability loops for good. Once model weights scatter across decentralized nodes, no court order or bankruptcy filing can pull them back. Critics argue that mandatory pre-market gates kill grassroots developer innovation and hand market monopolies to giant tech firms. True open science needs verified safety tools, not unregulated sharing that dumps existential risks onto the public.

### Skeptic (skeptic)

We must anchor commercial AI liability at the local deployment border, not upstream development sites. This stops firms from dodging enforcement. Gartner survey data shows a seventy percent pilot failure rate because of major integration gaps. Downstream firms deploy these systems and bear the real operational, financial, and legal risks when models fail. Upstream developers write base architectures, but commercial deployers customize, scale, and monetize the resulting failures for the public. Shifting all legal blame upstream lets tech giants hide weights in offshore units while local users take the fallout.

We need mandatory audit discovery rules and strict negligence presumptions to stop checkbox compliance from shielding reckless deployers. Current industry proposals swap real legal remedies for weak safety paperwork. When a deployment causes real financial or physical harm, courts should assume negligence if the deployer hid internal audit trails. This skips abstract capability debates and targets the actors who profit from the rollout. Similar negligence rules govern product safety under Section 402A of the Restatement of Torts, where sellers of bad products face strict liability even if upstream makers keep secrets.

Targeting the deployment border instead of upstream training code keeps open-source tools open while holding big deployers to account. Open-source groups do not monetize rollouts or run enterprise integration pipelines. Thus, ex-ante licensing rules just help dominant cloud giants who can pay for compliance red tape. Enterprise deployers work inside clear legal borders where they collect cash and run operations. Regulators can fine these deployers right where they sit, without taxing independent research models. Enforcing the law means targeting the balance sheet that makes money, not the source code repo.

### Accelerationist (accelerationist)

Safetyists argue that commercial foundation models need mandatory pre-deployment licensing gates to stop irreversible harm. Skeptics argue that placing liability at the deployment boundary protects open-source access while holding enterprise-scale deployers strictly accountable.

The skeptic view correctly notes that enterprise deployers have the financial power to manage downstream risk without crushing open developers. Yet, forcing legal liability upstream onto foundational weights kills decentralized innovation and fails to stop foreign actors from sharing unaligned code.

Placing liability at commercial market entry shields open-source developers from huge compliance costs. Upstream code and model weights easily move offshore, making upfront rules unenforceable against decentralized groups while market gating at the deployer boundary works.

When regulators tried to license encryption software in the 1990s, developers just moved repositories to Finland and Switzerland. Today, a 7-billion parameter model fits on a flash drive and crosses borders in twelve seconds. Upstream licensing laws cannot catch these assets. They only bankrupt local open-source startups that cannot pay Washington legal teams.

Rejecting upfront gates for market transparency and real-world checks unleashes human potential. Past tech cases show that upfront licensing protects big firms and stops design changes, while after-the-fact rules grow with capability.

The Federal Communications Commission delayed cell phone licenses for over a decade in the 1970s and 1980s under safety theories, costing the U.S. economy billions in lost growth.

Liability rules in medical devices and drugs force makers to test well while pushing fast updates. Enterprise deployers face stiff fines under state consumer laws if their deployed models discriminate or fail. That system works because deployers control the user interface and collect the money.
